## G1（简报：假设后替代方案排除段）

One might expect common owners to act directly, inflating their own portfolio companies' ESG ratings. Yet the more plausible channel runs through rivals, for two reasons. First, regulators have become alert to the anticompetitive potential of common ownership and have warned ESG rating providers to institute internal controls and interpret third-party data prudently (SEC, 2022). Under such scrutiny, visibly favoring one's own holdings invites detection, whereas procuring a worse rating for a target firm outside the portfolio obscures the beneficiary relationship and lowers detection risk. Second, ESG ratings diverge widely because rating standards differ sharply in what they measure (Berg, Koelbel, & Rigobon, 2022); against inconsistent yardsticks, stakeholders can benchmark firms only against same-industry peers. Evaluation is thus relative within industries, so depressing a competitor's rating mechanically elevates the affiliated portfolio company's standing even if the latter's absolute score never moves. Taken together, suppressing rivals' ratings lets common owners capture the benefits of informational competition while lowering the risk that their influence is detected—hence our hypothesis specifies an effect on targets rather than on portfolio companies.

ESG media controversy should strengthen this logic. Reputational damage rarely stays contained: it propagates through stakeholders and erodes the firm's financial performance (Zavyalova, Bundy, & Humphrey, 2021). Yet third-party evaluation offers a hedge. Because rating agencies present themselves as independent experts assessing firms relative to their peers, a favorable ESG rating can counterbalance the reputational injuries that media criticism inflicts (Rhee & Valdez, 2009). And because stakeholders treat ESG ratings as credible assessments, an improved rating can recast a controversy-plagued portfolio company as superior to its same-industry rivals, restoring the favor that controversy destroyed. A damaged portfolio company is thus precisely the holding a common owner has the strongest incentive to assist—and, given within-industry relativity, the natural means of assistance is to depress the relative reputation of the target firms with which it competes. That is, the rival-depressing effect becomes more valuable when the portfolio company most needs reputational repair. Thus:

When a portfolio company has just won a major ESG award, common owners should find it easier to sway ESG rating agencies in ways that favor the awardee and disadvantage its unrecognized peers. Major awards carry informational weight: by certifying the authenticity and impact of a firm's practices to stakeholders (Kovács & Sharkey, 2014), an award primes evaluators to interpret subsequent information in the winner's favor. Rating executives and analysts belong to precisely these stakeholder audiences, so positive disclosures about the awarded firm and negative ones about its same-industry competitors should both meet less resistance. The boundary of this mechanism matters: because most rating methodologies do not incorporate awards as a direct scoring input, an award cannot move ratings through the formula itself; it operates only by independently shaping the perceptions of those who gather and weigh the underlying information. As a result, an award opens a window of heightened receptivity within rating agencies—one that common owners can exploit to tilt ratings toward the decorated portfolio company and away from its unrecognized rivals.
