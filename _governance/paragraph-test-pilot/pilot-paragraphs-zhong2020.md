## P1

Customer concentration captures a firm's degree of dependence on its most powerful customers (e.g., Dhaliwal, Judd, Serfling, & Shaikh, 2016; Patatoukas, 2012). Due to their resource limitations, firms cannot supply all of the possible combinations of products desired by every customer. Instead, they may benefit from prioritizing those customers that are deemed most important (Levitas, 2013). For example, researchers have found that firms with high customer concentrations can earn large and stable incomes; have enhanced relationships and coordination; and obtain valuable feedback (Patatoukas, 2012). Thus, these important customers will gain more prominence in the resource allocation of firms.

## P2

Firms need to make investment decisions not only to address their current customers, but also to maintain their future vitality by attracting new customers. Nevertheless, it is often hard for firms to achieve such a balance, especially when the needs of their current customers may not match the needs of future customers. In particular, firms with a high customer concentration are more likely to collect and transmit information, and allocate resources in ways that enhance the value, power, and prestige of existing major customers. For example, Titman (1984) suggested that when firms depend on a few major customers for a large proportion of their sales, they make specific concessions to these major customers. In this regard, concern for the realized or potential benefit of their major customers drives firms to prioritize the expectations, preferences, and needs of these customers.

## P3

Meeting major customers' needs typically requires searches that are deep and more focused. Such search behavior may involve, for example, refining a particular competence, providing a more reliable output, or maintaining a consistent set of processes and procedures. Such priorities tend to further reinforce existing routines and lead to the development of very specialized competence rather than experimentation and risk-taking (Ahuja & Lampert, 2001; Levinthal & March, 1993). Moreover, the greater a firm's customer concentration, the more critical a few customers are, leading to more of the firm's organizational activities (e.g., R&D, marketing and sales, production, and logistics) being shaped by uncertainties involving major customers. Search breadth by nature is uncertain and risky (March, 1991). If it fails, it may cause a significant decline in profits from major customers. Thus, disproportionate resource allocation to existing major customers limits firm search breadth and displaces the concern for future customers. The marketing managers featured in Christensen and Bower's (1996) study confirmed that they chose to prioritize technological projects based on what major customers wanted while neglecting innovative projects for peripheral customers.

## P4

In their bestseller, The Customer Trap: How to Avoid the Biggest Mistake in Business, Thomas and Wilkinson (2015) suggested that the most innovative companies "create at the root level, [thereby] inventing core technologies with multiple applications," and that such companies are better at managing mega-customer demand, rather than just the demands of a few particular customers. It may thus seem obvious that innovative products, which result from broad search efforts, are the best means for both retaining existing customers and developing new markets. However, the largest existing customers often use their disproportionate size and buying power to disable such strategic imperatives (Thomas & Wilkinson, 2015). The most powerful customers may constrain the search behavior of firms to the extent that the firms cannot escape from the customer trap, even though they acknowledge the need for change (Levitt & March, 1988). Thus, we contend that firms with high levels of customer concentration are more likely to conduct a deep search into their existing knowledge base but are less likely to engage in a broad search for knowledge beyond the familiar. Thus, we propose Hypothesis 1.

## P5

The "capabilities" stream of research focuses on resources and capabilities as a source of inertia or local search (Gavetti & Levinthal, 2000). As Levinthal and March (1993) argued, "As they [firms] develop greater and greater competence at a particular activity, they engage in that activity more, thus further increasing competence and the opportunity cost of exploration" (p. 106). Therefore, prior experience and competencies (Ahuja & Lampert, 2001; Helfat, 1994; de Figueiredo, Rawley, & Rider, 2015), complementary assets (Christensen, 1997), and patterns of resource investment (Ahuja & Katila, 2004; Gilbert, 2005) leading to current competitive advantages, may constrain firms' future search behavior, and drive them to fall into a competence trap (Leonard-Barton, 1992). However, this research fails to consider how managerial cognition affects organizational search (Eggers & Kaplan, 2013) and neglects the role of demand-side factors (Adner & Levinthal, 2001; Priem et al., 2012).

## P6

Scholars have begun to recognize the importance of integrating different views (Eggers & Kaplan, 2013). For example, Kaplan (2008) examined the interactions between cognition and firm capability. Both Gans (2016) and McElheran (2015) tried to integrate the supply- and demand-side perspectives to explain how market leaders embrace innovation. Despite these developments, we still know little about the kinds of environmental stimuli that can be selected into the attention structure of top managers (Ocasio, 1997, 2011). We argue that because customers are among the most important factors in an organization's demand environment (Adner & Levinthal, 2001; Adner & Zemsky, 2006), they may affect the development of executive attention (Hodgkinson, 2015; Ocasio, 1997, 2011; Rosa et al., 1999). Executive attention in turn may drive them to match their firms' capabilities with perceived opportunities in the demand environment. Moreover, as "both the supply and 'demand' sides of transactional networks" (Hodgkinson, 2015, p. 19) affect managerial attention and cognition of the environment (McElheran, 2015), introducing supply-side factors into the logic chain (demand-side factors, cognition, and organizational search) can significantly improve our understanding of the phenomenon of organizational search.
