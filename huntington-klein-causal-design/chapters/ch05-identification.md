# Chapter 5: Identification

## Core Idea

Identification asks which variation in observed data contains the answer to the research question. It is a property of the design and assumptions, not of statistical significance or estimator complexity.

## Identification Contract

Write the design as seven linked statements:

1. **DGP**: What processes generate treatment, outcome, sample inclusion, and measurement?
2. **Target contrast**: Which counterfactual comparison defines the causal effect?
3. **Observed variation**: Why do units differ in treatment in these data?
4. **Answer-bearing variation**: Which part of that variation approximates the target contrast?
5. **Excluded alternatives**: Which rival paths must be closed or isolated away?
6. **Identifying assumptions**: What must be true for the comparison to work?
7. **Failure evidence**: What observable implications would contradict those assumptions?

## Workflow

1. Reconstruct the institutional process before specifying a model.
2. List every known source of treatment variation.
3. Classify each source as potentially causal, confounded, selected, measured with error, or post-treatment.
4. Use a DAG or equivalent causal model to connect sources of variation to outcomes.
5. Define the estimand induced by the surviving comparison.
6. Choose an estimator that preserves that comparison.
7. Design diagnostics around the assumptions rather than around coefficient stability alone.

## Worked Examples

> Source-grounded reconstructions from Huntington-Klein (2025); compressed and paraphrased.

- **Avocado prices and quantities**: observed price variation can reflect both supply and demand processes; without separating the source, a price–quantity slope does not identify a demand effect.
- **Dog escape**: changing one suspected exit and observing whether escape persists illustrates intervention-based elimination of rival mechanisms.
- **Alcohol and mortality**: rich data and prominent publication do not remove ambiguity about selection, confounding, exposure measurement, or target population.

## Context as Evidence

Institutional knowledge is part of identification evidence. It explains who assigns treatment, when anticipation is possible, which units can sort, what other events coincide, and which measurements are generated by the treatment itself. These facts cannot be recovered from a regression table.

## Evidence Ledger

| Design statement | Best evidence source |
|---|---|
| Why treatment varies | Assignment rules, institutions, actor behavior |
| Why comparison units are relevant | Sampling and eligibility process |
| Why rival paths are blocked | DAG plus measured/pre-treatment facts |
| Why timing is credible | Event chronology, anticipation window |
| Why the estimand is local or weighted | Identifying observations and estimator weights |
| Why inference is valid | Assignment/dependence structure |
| Why transport is plausible | Mechanism and target-population comparison |

Treat “exogenous” and “as-if random” as conclusions that summarize this ledger, never as substitute evidence.

## Failure Modes

- **Coefficient identification**: treating a uniquely estimated regression parameter as a uniquely identified causal effect.
- **Assumption laundering**: translating “as-if random” into a label without an assignment-process argument.
- **Variation opacity**: failing to say which units and time periods identify the estimate.
- **Diagnostic substitution**: treating balance or pre-trend tests as proof of untestable assumptions.

## Completion Check

A design is identified only conditionally: the answer must state the exact assumptions, the variation they license, the estimand obtained, and what remains unknowable if they fail.
