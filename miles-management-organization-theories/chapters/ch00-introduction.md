# Chapter 0: Introduction

## Core Idea

A useful theory specifies the important constructs, relates them, explains the mechanism that produces the relationship, and states the boundary conditions. Use theory to explain and predict—not merely to label variables or decorate hypotheses.

## Frameworks Introduced

- **What–How–Why–Who/Where/When**: Audit a theory through four questions.
  - **What** identifies the constructs while balancing comprehensiveness and parsimony.
  - **How** specifies direct, indirect, and reciprocal relationships among constructs.
  - **Why** supplies the causal or generative logic—the assumptions that make the model more than a diagram.
  - **Who, where, and when** bound the population, context, level, and time in which the explanation should hold.
- **Construct–proposition–variable chain**: Treat constructs as abstract concepts, propositions as claims about their relations, and variables as operational representations used in empirical tests.
- **Theory evaluation**: Judge a theory by explanatory value, predictive usefulness, empirical testability, parsimony, boundary clarity, and practical usefulness.

## Key Concepts

- **Construct**: An abstract building block in an explanation.
- **Proposition**: A theoretically justified relationship between constructs.
- **Variable**: An operational representation of a construct.
- **Boundary condition**: A condition under which the proposed mechanism strengthens, weakens, reverses, or ceases to operate.
- **Parsimony**: Retaining only elements that materially improve explanation.

## Mental Models

- Use **What–How–Why–Who/Where/When** before accepting any conceptual model as a theory.
- Think of a theory as a **causal map plus scope conditions**, not as a list of citations.
- Use multiple theories when they explain distinct mechanisms; avoid combining theories merely because they predict the same outcome.

## Anti-patterns

- **Boxes-and-arrows without why**: A diagram is not a theory unless the mechanism is explicit.
- **Variable-first theorizing**: Available measures should not determine the theoretical object.
- **Universal claims**: A claim without boundaries is usually overstated rather than powerful.
- **Citation counting as validation**: Popularity does not establish explanatory adequacy.

## Constructed Application

> Synthetic application derived from the theory; not a quotation or documented case from Miles (2012).

A researcher predicts that external knowledge improves innovation. The **what** is external knowledge, absorptive capacity, and innovation; the **how** is that knowledge must be recognized, assimilated, and applied; the **why** is that prior related knowledge enables interpretation and recombination; the **who/where/when** might be R&D-intensive firms facing rapid technological change. Measures come only after this conceptual chain is stable.

## Key Takeaways

1. Begin with the phenomenon and explanatory mechanism.
2. Separate constructs from measures and propositions from statistical hypotheses.
3. Make boundary conditions part of the theory, not an afterthought.
4. Compare theories by what each explains and what each leaves unexplained.
