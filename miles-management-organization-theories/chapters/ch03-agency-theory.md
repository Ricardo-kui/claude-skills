# Chapter 3: Agency Theory

## Core Idea

Delegation creates governance problems when principals and agents have divergent interests, different risk preferences, and asymmetric information.

## Frameworks Introduced

- **Principal–agent model**: Use when one party delegates decision authority and cannot fully observe another's type or conduct.
- **Hidden information versus hidden action**:
  - Hidden information before or during contracting creates adverse selection.
  - Hidden action after contracting creates moral hazard.
- **Governance choice**: Compare monitoring, incentives, bonding, insurance, residual loss, and internal performance rather than assuming tighter control is always efficient.
- **Behavior- versus outcome-based contracts**: Prefer behavioral control when conduct is observable; prefer outcome incentives when results are measurable and external uncertainty is manageable.

## Key Concepts

- **Information asymmetry**: Unequal access to decision-relevant information.
- **Adverse selection**: Choosing an unsuitable agent because type or ability is concealed.
- **Moral hazard**: Agent behavior shifts after delegation because actions are difficult to observe.
- **Agency costs**: Monitoring and bonding expenditures plus residual loss.
- **Risk sharing**: Allocation of uncertain outcomes between parties with different risk tolerance.

## Mental Models

- Use **delegate → information gap → incentive divergence → governance response**.
- Ask whether the governance mechanism costs less than the expected opportunism it prevents.
- Treat stewardship or intrinsic motivation as rival explanations when self-interest assumptions are weak.

## Anti-patterns

- **Assuming all agents are opportunistic**: This can crowd out trust and prosocial motives.
- **Monitoring without cost accounting**: Control can consume more value than misconduct.
- **Equating outcomes with effort**: Random shocks make outcome-only contracts noisy.

## Constructed Application

> Synthetic application derived from the theory; not a quotation or documented case from Miles (2012).

A board hires a CEO whose strategic effort is difficult to observe. Stock compensation aligns some interests but exposes the CEO to market risk and may encourage short-term manipulation. The board combines long-horizon equity, process oversight, disclosure rules, and independent audits. The design should be evaluated against total agency costs, not a single incentive coefficient.

## Key Takeaways

1. Specify principal, agent, delegated task, information asymmetry, and conflict.
2. Separate adverse selection from moral hazard.
3. Model governance mechanisms as costly and incomplete.
4. Test conditions under which trust, stewardship, or multiple principals alter predictions.
