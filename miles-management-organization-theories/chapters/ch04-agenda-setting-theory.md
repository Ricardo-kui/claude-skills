# Chapter 4: Agenda Setting Theory

## Core Idea

Actors influence collective attention by determining which issues become salient, how they are framed, and which attributes or interpretations receive repeated emphasis.

## Frameworks Introduced

- **First-level agenda setting**: Use to explain why issue salience rises with frequency, prominence, and duration of attention.
- **Second-level agenda setting / attribute salience**: Explain not only what people think about but which properties of an issue dominate evaluation.
- **Framing**: Select and organize aspects of reality into a problem definition, causal interpretation, moral evaluation, or remedy.
- **Agenda building**: Trace reciprocal influence among media, organizations, political actors, stakeholders, and publics rather than assuming one-way effects.

## Key Concepts

- **Salience**: Perceived importance or accessibility of an issue.
- **Priming**: Changing the criteria people use for subsequent judgments.
- **Obtrusiveness**: The degree to which audiences experience an issue directly; less direct issues often leave more room for agenda effects.
- **Need for orientation**: Uncertainty and relevance that motivate attention to external agendas.

## Mental Models

- Use **attention is scarce**: gaining agenda position often displaces other issues.
- Separate **issue visibility**, **attribute framing**, and **behavioral response**.
- Examine who sets whose agenda and through what channel.

## Anti-patterns

- **Treating coverage as attitude conversion**: Agenda effects concern salience before persuasion.
- **Ignoring reciprocal causality**: Media, stakeholders, and organizations respond to one another.
- **Counting mentions without prominence or framing**: Exposure volume alone is incomplete.

## Constructed Application

> Synthetic application derived from the theory; not a quotation or documented case from Miles (2012).

A firm facing repeated product defects emphasizes supplier complexity while activists emphasize preventable safety failures. Both discuss the same event, but competing attribute agendas shape whether audiences judge it as an operational challenge or negligence. Researchers should measure attention, attributes, source interaction, and downstream evaluations separately.

## Key Takeaways

1. Define the agenda holder, target audience, issue, channel, and time lag.
2. Distinguish salience from valence and framing.
3. Model competing agendas and feedback loops.
4. Use longitudinal designs when attention accumulates or decays.
