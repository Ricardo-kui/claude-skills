# Chapter 6: Attribution Theory

## Core Idea

People explain outcomes by assigning causes, and those perceived causes shape emotion, responsibility judgments, expectations, motivation, and future behavior.

## Frameworks Introduced

- **Locus–stability–controllability**: Classify a perceived cause as internal/external, stable/unstable, and controllable/uncontrollable.
- **Covariation model**: Use consensus, distinctiveness, and consistency information to infer whether a cause lies in the person, target, or situation.
- **Intrapersonal attribution sequence**: Outcome → affect → causal search → attribution → causal dimensions → psychological consequences → action.
- **Interpersonal attribution**: Observers' causal judgments determine blame, sympathy, help, punishment, and evaluation.

## Key Concepts

- **Fundamental attribution error**: Overweighting dispositions and underweighting situations when judging others.
- **Self-serving attribution**: Claiming internal credit for success and externalizing failure.
- **Actor–observer difference**: Actors and observers access different information and perspectives.
- **Attributional style**: A recurring pattern of explaining positive and negative outcomes.

## Mental Models

- Use **cause as perceived, not necessarily true**.
- Before assigning blame, ask what base rates, constraints, and comparison cases are missing.
- Expect stable attributions to shape future expectancy and controllable attributions to shape responsibility.

## Anti-patterns

- **Treating performance as transparent evidence of ability or effort**.
- **Managerial self-credit**: Crediting leadership for success but employees for failure.
- **Correcting bias with another unsupported story**: Gather diagnostic information first.

## Constructed Application

> Synthetic application derived from the theory; not a quotation or documented case from Miles (2012).

A salesperson misses target. The manager initially infers low effort. Comparison data show that the whole region declined (high consensus), this salesperson succeeds elsewhere (high distinctiveness), and the miss occurred only this quarter (low consistency). The evidence supports a situational explanation and a market response rather than punishment.

## Key Takeaways

1. Separate observed outcome from inferred cause.
2. Measure causal dimensions and their consequences, not just internal versus external locus.
3. Use comparison information to reduce attribution error.
4. Study collective attributions when groups jointly interpret success or crisis.
