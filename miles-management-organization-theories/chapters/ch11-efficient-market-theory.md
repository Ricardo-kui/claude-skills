# Chapter 11: Efficient Market Theory

## Core Idea

When prices rapidly incorporate available information, predictable excess returns are difficult to earn because new price-moving information is itself unpredictable.

## Frameworks Introduced

- **Forms of efficiency**:
  - Weak form: prices reflect past price information.
  - Semi-strong form: prices reflect public information.
  - Strong form: prices reflect public and private information.
- **Random walk implication**: Successive price changes are difficult to forecast from already incorporated information.
- **Event-study logic**: Examine abnormal returns around information events to assess speed and completeness of price adjustment.

## Key Concepts

- **Available information set**: The information claimed to be embedded in price.
- **Abnormal return**: Return beyond that predicted by an appropriate benchmark.
- **Market anomaly**: A persistent pattern inconsistent with a specified efficiency model.
- **Joint-hypothesis problem**: Tests assess market efficiency together with the asset-pricing model used to define normal returns.

## Mental Models

- Use efficiency as a **conditional benchmark**, not a claim that prices are always correct.
- Distinguish unpredictable price changes from absence of irrationality or mispricing.
- In management research, use market reactions as investor interpretation, not automatic proof of long-term value.

## Anti-patterns

- **Equating price with intrinsic value**.
- **Reading a null event reaction as no substantive effect** without checking anticipation and measurement.
- **Transplanting shareholder rationality assumptions directly to employees or managers**.

## Constructed Application

> Synthetic application derived from the theory; not a quotation or documented case from Miles (2012).

A company announces a recall. An event study finds a rapid negative abnormal return. This supports the claim that public information altered investor expectations under the benchmark model; it does not establish the recall's total social cost, managerial culpability, or long-run operating effect.

## Key Takeaways

1. Name the information set and benchmark model.
2. Treat event timing, leakage, and anticipation as design issues.
3. Separate informational efficiency from normative desirability.
4. Use behavioral finance or information-friction theories for systematic deviations.
