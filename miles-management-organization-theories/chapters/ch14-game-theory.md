# Chapter 14: Game Theory

## Core Idea

Strategic choices must be evaluated in light of other players' possible actions, information, preferences, and credible responses—not in isolation.

## Frameworks Introduced

- **Game specification**: Identify players, available strategies, sequence, information, payoffs, and enforcement.
- **Dominant strategy and best response**: A dominant strategy is best regardless of rivals; a best response depends on their choice.
- **Nash equilibrium**: No player can improve payoff by deviating alone given others' strategies.
- **Repeated games**: Future interaction can support cooperation through reputation, reciprocity, and punishment.
- **Commitment and signaling**: Change rivals' beliefs or feasible actions through credible, costly moves.

## Key Concepts

- **Payoff**: Actor-valued consequence, not necessarily money.
- **Information set**: What a player knows when choosing.
- **Zero-sum / non-zero-sum**: Whether one player's gain necessarily equals another's loss.
- **Backward induction**: Reason from later decisions to earlier choices.

## Mental Models

- Use **if I do X, what will others rationally do next?**
- Change the game—players, sequence, information, or payoffs—when equilibrium is undesirable.
- Treat rationality and common knowledge as assumptions to test, not facts.

## Anti-patterns

- **Calling any competition a game-theoretic explanation** without a specified structure.
- **Ignoring cooperation, identity, fairness, or bounded rationality**.
- **Recommending a strategy that is not credible after rivals respond**.

## Constructed Application

> Synthetic application derived from the theory; not a quotation or documented case from Miles (2012).

Two firms repeatedly decide whether to maintain quality or cut costs. One-shot incentives favor cutting, but repeated interaction, observable quality, and reputational loss can sustain mutual quality. A regulator can alter payoffs with inspection and penalties; buyers can alter information with certification. Strategy follows game structure.

## Key Takeaways

1. Specify payoffs from each player's perspective.
2. Distinguish simultaneous from sequential choice.
3. Check equilibrium and off-equilibrium credibility.
4. Add behavioral theories when preferences are social or cognition is bounded.
