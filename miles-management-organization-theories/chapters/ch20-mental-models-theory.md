# Chapter 20: Mental Models Theory

## Core Idea

People act through simplified internal representations of how a system works; these models enable prediction and coordination but also filter evidence and sustain error.

## Frameworks Introduced

- **Model construction**: Actors select entities, causal links, boundaries, and assumptions to represent a complex environment.
- **Single-model bias**: People often construct one plausible model and reason within it instead of testing alternatives.
- **Shared mental models**: Team members coordinate when they share compatible models of tasks, roles, equipment, and interaction.
- **Model updating**: Surprise, feedback, simulation, dialogue, and comparison can expose and revise faulty causal structure.

## Key Concepts

- **Cognitive representation**: Limited internal model rather than a copy of reality.
- **Causal map**: Representation of perceived cause-and-effect links.
- **Model accuracy**: Correspondence sufficient for prediction and action.
- **Model similarity**: Degree of overlap across team members, distinct from accuracy.

## Mental Models

- Use **make the model visible** through diagrams, predictions, and assumptions.
- Seek both sharedness and accuracy; consensus around a wrong model is dangerous.
- Generate rival models before interpreting ambiguous evidence.

## Anti-patterns

- **Assuming disagreement is only poor communication**; actors may hold different causal structures.
- **Equating confidence with model quality**.
- **Forcing uniformity where specialized complementary models are useful**.

## Constructed Application

> Synthetic application derived from the theory; not a quotation or documented case from Miles (2012).

Sales sees falling demand as a pricing problem; operations sees a quality problem. Each predicts different customer behavior. The team draws causal maps, states predictions, and checks segment-level evidence. A revised shared model links quality failures to price sensitivity, changing both production and pricing action.

## Key Takeaways

1. Elicit assumptions and causal links, not only opinions.
2. Test models by their predictions.
3. Distinguish similarity, completeness, and accuracy.
4. Build routines for updating after anomalies.
