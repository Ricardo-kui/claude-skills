# Chapter 21: Organizational Ecology Theory

## Core Idea

Organizational diversity and survival reflect population-level processes of variation, selection, retention, competition, legitimacy, and structural inertia.

## Frameworks Introduced

- **Population ecology**: Study founding, growth, transformation, decline, and death across populations rather than treating adaptation by individual firms as the sole process.
- **Density dependence**: Rising population density initially increases legitimacy and founding; later competition dominates and raises failure.
- **Niche width**: Specialists exploit narrow environments; generalists span broader conditions, with relative advantage depending on variability.
- **Structural inertia**: Reliability and accountability favor stable structures but make fundamental change slow and hazardous.
- **Liability patterns**: Newness, smallness, adolescence, aging, and transformation can each alter mortality risk.

## Key Concepts

- **Population**: Organizations with a common form competing or interacting in a resource space.
- **Niche**: Environmental conditions and resources supporting a form.
- **Organizational form**: Recognizable blueprint carrying identity and legitimacy.
- **Selection**: Differential founding and survival that changes population composition.

## Mental Models

- Use **population composition can change without firms adapting**.
- Separate age, size, cohort, period, and density effects.
- Treat change as potentially survival-enhancing and disruption-inducing at the same time.

## Anti-patterns

- **Anthropomorphizing populations as deliberate actors**.
- **Inferring selection from one successful firm**.
- **Ignoring left truncation, right censoring, and changing risk sets** in survival analysis.

## Constructed Application

> Synthetic application derived from the theory; not a quotation or documented case from Miles (2012).

In a new industry, more entrants initially validate the category and attract investors. As density rises, competition for customers and employees intensifies. A specialist may thrive in a stable niche but fail after abrupt demand shifts; a generalist pays coordination costs yet survives broader variation.

## Key Takeaways

1. Define population and niche before modeling survival.
2. Use event-history designs with changing hazards.
3. Separate legitimacy and competition mechanisms.
4. Compare ecological selection with strategic adaptation explanations.
