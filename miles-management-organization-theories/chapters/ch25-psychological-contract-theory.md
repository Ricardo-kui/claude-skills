# Chapter 25: Psychological Contract Theory

## Core Idea

Employees hold subjective beliefs about reciprocal promises and obligations with an organization; perceived breach and emotional violation arise when expected obligations appear unfulfilled.

## Frameworks Introduced

- **Psychological contract formation**: Pre-entry expectations, recruiting messages, socialization, policies, observed practices, and agents' statements create perceived promises.
- **Transactional–relational continuum**: Contracts range from specific, economic, short-term exchange to open-ended socioemotional obligation.
- **Breach–violation distinction**: Breach is a cognitive assessment of unmet obligation; violation is the emotional response that may follow.
- **Incongruence versus reneging**: Parties may understand promises differently, or one party may knowingly fail to deliver.

## Key Concepts

- **Perceived promise**: Communication or practice interpreted as a future commitment.
- **Reciprocity**: Belief that contribution creates a corresponding obligation.
- **Contract fulfillment**: Perceived delivery relative to promised obligation.
- **Organizational agent**: Recruiter, supervisor, policy, or practice treated as speaking for the organization.

## Mental Models

- Use **promise → contribution → expected obligation → fulfillment judgment → response**.
- Treat the contract as individual and evolving, not a shared legal document.
- Manage expectations through clarity, consistency, and explicit renegotiation.

## Anti-patterns

- **Equating unmet desire with breach**: A promise or perceived obligation must be involved.
- **Assuming managers know employees' contracts**.
- **Overpromising during recruitment and relying on disclaimers later**.

## Constructed Application

> Synthetic application derived from the theory; not a quotation or documented case from Miles (2012).

A recruit accepts lower pay after repeated assurances of rapid development. Training is canceled without explanation. The employee perceives breach because a promised exchange motivated acceptance; anger marks violation. Early disclosure, alternatives, and explicit renegotiation can limit—but not erase—the response.

## Key Takeaways

1. Identify the promise, source, recipient contribution, and time horizon.
2. Measure breach separately from violation and outcomes.
3. Account for idiosyncratic interpretation and organizational agents.
4. Study repair, renegotiation, and repeated breach over time.
