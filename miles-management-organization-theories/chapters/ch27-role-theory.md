# Chapter 27: Role Theory

## Core Idea

Social positions carry patterned expectations that guide behavior; performance and strain depend on how roles are communicated, learned, negotiated, and combined across senders and contexts.

## Frameworks Introduced

- **Role episode**: Role senders hold expectations, communicate them, the focal person interprets and acts, and senders respond.
- **Role set**: One position links the focal person to multiple stakeholders with distinct expectations.
- **Role stressors**:
  - Ambiguity: uncertainty about expected behavior or evaluation.
  - Conflict: incompatible expectations within or across roles.
  - Overload: demands exceed time or capacity.
- **Role taking and role making**: Actors learn expectations but also negotiate and personalize enactment.

## Key Concepts

- **Role**: Patterned behavior expected in a social position and context.
- **Role identity**: Self-meaning attached to occupying a role.
- **Interrole conflict**: Incompatibility across roles.
- **Intrarole conflict**: Incompatibility within a role or among its senders.

## Mental Models

- Use **position → senders → expectations → interpretation → enactment**.
- Diagnose unclear priorities before attributing failure to motivation.
- Allow role innovation within nonnegotiable accountabilities.

## Anti-patterns

- **Treating the job description as the full role**.
- **Assuming all role conflict is harmful**; some tension prompts learning or change.
- **Ignoring informal role senders and identities outside work**.

## Constructed Application

> Synthetic application derived from the theory; not a quotation or documented case from Miles (2012).

A project manager is told by engineering to maximize quality, by sales to customize rapidly, and by finance to cut cost. The strain is not vague stress but intersender conflict. A governance forum clarifies priority and decision rights, reducing conflict while preserving role discretion.

## Key Takeaways

1. Identify focal role, role senders, and each expectation.
2. Separate ambiguity, conflict, and overload.
3. Observe role negotiation and enactment over time.
4. Account for multiple identities and boundary-spanning roles.
