# Chapter 30: Social Capital Theory

## Core Idea

Actors obtain resources through relationships and network positions; structural connections, relational trust, and shared cognitive systems affect access, mobilization, and use.

## Frameworks Introduced

- **Three dimensions**:
  - Structural: ties, configuration, and reachability.
  - Relational: trust, obligations, norms, and identification.
  - Cognitive: shared language and narratives.
- **Bonding versus bridging**: Bonding creates solidarity within a group; bridging provides novel resources across groups.
- **Closure versus brokerage**: Closure supports trust and enforcement; brokerage spans structural holes and creates information or control benefits.
- **Access–mobilization–return chain**: Network resources matter only when accessible, activated, and converted into valued outcomes.

## Key Concepts

- **Social capital**: Actual or potential resources embedded in and available through relationships.
- **Structural hole**: Separation between otherwise disconnected contacts.
- **Reciprocity norm**: Expectation that assistance will be returned.
- **Network liability**: Costs from exclusion, maintenance, conformity, or obligations.

## Mental Models

- Use **ties are channels; capital is the obtainable resource and capacity to mobilize it**.
- Balance bonding for coordination with bridging for novelty.
- Specify whose capital, located where, benefiting whom.

## Anti-patterns

- **Counting contacts as social capital**.
- **Assuming dense networks are universally beneficial**.
- **Ignoring inequality and appropriation of network benefits**.

## Constructed Application

> Synthetic application derived from the theory; not a quotation or documented case from Miles (2012).

A research team has strong internal trust but repeatedly recycles the same ideas. A boundary spanner connects it to clinical partners, bringing novel problems and data. Translation routines and trust allow the team to mobilize—not merely access—the new resources. Brokerage without integration would yield little value.

## Key Takeaways

1. Separate network structure from relational and cognitive mechanisms.
2. Identify embedded resources and mobilization behavior.
3. Examine maintenance costs, lock-in, and exclusion.
4. Treat social capital as multilevel and actor-specific.
