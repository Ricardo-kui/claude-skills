# Chapter 31: Social Cognitive Theory

## Core Idea

Behavior, personal cognition, and environment reciprocally influence one another; people exercise agency through efficacy beliefs, observational learning, goals, and self-regulation.

## Frameworks Introduced

- **Triadic reciprocal determinism**: Behavior, person factors, and environment influence each other over time with unequal strength.
- **Observational learning**: Attention → retention → behavioral production → motivation.
- **Self-efficacy sources**: Mastery experience, vicarious experience, social persuasion, and interpretation of physiological or affective state.
- **Human agency**: Intentionality, forethought, self-reactiveness, and self-reflection.

## Key Concepts

- **Self-efficacy**: Task- and context-specific belief in capability to execute required action.
- **Outcome expectation**: Belief that action will produce a result; distinct from capability belief.
- **Modeling**: Learning from observed behavior and consequences.
- **Self-regulation**: Setting standards, monitoring action, evaluating progress, and reacting.

## Mental Models

- Use **can do versus will lead to** to separate efficacy from outcome expectations.
- Build mastery before relying on encouragement.
- Treat employees as producers as well as products of their environment.

## Anti-patterns

- **Using general confidence as self-efficacy**.
- **Assuming observation automatically produces performance**.
- **Blaming low efficacy when tools, opportunities, or incentives are absent**.

## Constructed Application

> Synthetic application derived from the theory; not a quotation or documented case from Miles (2012).

Technicians must learn a diagnostic procedure. An expert models it while verbalizing cues; learners rehearse, receive feedback, and master progressively harder cases. Success raises efficacy, which increases persistence. If correct diagnosis is never rewarded or equipment is unavailable, high efficacy alone will not produce use.

## Key Takeaways

1. Match efficacy measures to task and context.
2. Preserve temporal ordering in reciprocal models.
3. Design models that learners attend to, remember, and can reproduce.
4. Separate efficacy, outcome expectation, intention, and actual behavior.
