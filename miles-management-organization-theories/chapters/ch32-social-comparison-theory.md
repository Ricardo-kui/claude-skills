# Chapter 32: Social Comparison Theory

## Core Idea

When objective standards are absent or identity-relevant evaluation is salient, people assess opinions, abilities, and standing by comparing themselves with selected others.

## Frameworks Introduced

- **Comparison direction**: Upward comparison targets a better-off other; downward comparison targets a worse-off other; lateral comparison targets similarity.
- **Assimilation versus contrast**: A target can inspire movement toward it or sharpen perceived distance from it.
- **Comparison selection**: Similarity, relevance, attainability, threat, and motive shape who becomes the referent.
- **Self-evaluation, self-improvement, and self-enhancement motives**: Different motives produce different targets and effects.

## Key Concepts

- **Referent**: Person or group used as the comparison standard.
- **Better-/worse-than-average effects**: Systematic self-positioning influenced by task difficulty and information.
- **Relative deprivation**: Perceived disadvantage compared with a relevant referent.
- **Social comparison orientation**: Tendency to seek and use comparison information.

## Mental Models

- Use **compared with whom, on what dimension, for what motive?**
- Provide objective feedback when arbitrary peer comparison is harmful.
- Upward comparison motivates when the target is relevant and attainable; otherwise it can threaten.

## Anti-patterns

- **Assuming upward is motivating and downward is comforting in all cases**.
- **Treating benchmark choice as exogenous**.
- **Confusing social comparison with distributive justice**.

## Constructed Application

> Synthetic application derived from the theory; not a quotation or documented case from Miles (2012).

A dashboard ranks salespeople against the national top performer. High performers assimilate and learn; novices perceive the gap as unattainable and disengage. Adding comparable cohort benchmarks, progress feedback, and strategy examples changes referent relevance and the likely comparison effect.

## Key Takeaways

1. Specify comparison target, dimension, direction, and motive.
2. Measure perceived attainability and identification.
3. Distinguish comparison from objective performance information.
4. Examine repeated comparison and referent switching over time.
