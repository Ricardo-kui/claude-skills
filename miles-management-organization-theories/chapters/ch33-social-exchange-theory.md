# Chapter 33: Social Exchange Theory

## Core Idea

Relationships persist when parties reciprocally provide valued economic or socioemotional resources, develop trust, and expect that benefits and obligations will balance over an open-ended horizon.

## Frameworks Introduced

- **Reciprocity cycle**: One party provides a benefit, the recipient incurs an obligation, reciprocation builds trust, and repeated exchange deepens commitment.
- **Economic versus social exchange**: Economic exchange specifies return and timing; social exchange leaves both less defined and depends more on trust.
- **Comparison levels**: Actors evaluate exchange against expected outcomes and available alternatives.
- **Power dependence**: Power grows when an actor controls valued resources for which the other has few alternatives.

## Key Concepts

- **Reciprocity norm**: Obligation to return benefits received.
- **Socioemotional resource**: Respect, support, approval, status, or care.
- **Trust**: Willingness to accept vulnerability based on expectations of the partner.
- **Dependence**: Value of the relationship relative to alternatives.

## Mental Models

- Use **benefit → felt obligation → reciprocation → trust**.
- Ask whose valuation matters; a low-cost benefit to the giver may be highly valuable to the recipient.
- Distinguish social exchange from immediate quid pro quo.

## Anti-patterns

- **Calling every correlation with support social exchange** without reciprocal sequence.
- **Assuming reciprocity is always positive**; harmful and escalating exchanges also occur.
- **Ignoring asymmetrical dependence and coercion**.

## Constructed Application

> Synthetic application derived from the theory; not a quotation or documented case from Miles (2012).

A supervisor provides discretionary development support. The employee interprets it as genuine rather than instrumental, feels obligation, shares knowledge, and later receives more trust. A one-time cross-sectional survey cannot establish this cycle; a temporal design should separate initiating benefit, interpretation, response, and reinforcement.

## Key Takeaways

1. Identify exchanged resources, actors, sequence, and expectations.
2. Model trust and obligation as mechanisms, not synonyms for exchange.
3. Include alternatives and dependence.
4. Distinguish psychological contracts when explicit perceived promises are central.
