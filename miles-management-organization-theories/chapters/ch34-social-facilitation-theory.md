# Chapter 34: Social Facilitation Theory

## Core Idea

The actual or imagined presence of others changes arousal, evaluation concern, attention, and effort, improving dominant responses on well-learned tasks but often impairing learning or complex performance.

## Frameworks Introduced

- **Dominant-response account**: Presence heightens drive; the most likely response becomes more probable—helpful when correct, harmful when incorrect.
- **Audience versus co-actor effects**: Passive observers and parallel performers can affect action through different cues.
- **Evaluation apprehension**: Expected judgment increases effort and anxiety.
- **Distraction–conflict**: Others compete for attention, producing overload or increased focus depending on task and intensity.
- **Choking under pressure**: Self-focused monitoring can disrupt automated expert performance; distraction can consume working memory on complex tasks.

## Key Concepts

- **Task mastery**: Degree to which the correct response is dominant for a particular performer.
- **Mere presence**: Influence of others without explicit interaction.
- **Arousal**: Activation that changes response probability and attention.
- **Electronic monitoring**: Technologically mediated audience or evaluation cue.

## Mental Models

- Use **presence × performer mastery × task demand**.
- Design observation differently for practice, routine execution, and high-stakes expert performance.
- Treat task difficulty as person-specific, not an intrinsic label alone.

## Anti-patterns

- **Assuming audiences always improve performance**.
- **Classifying tasks as simple after observing high performance**.
- **Conflating social facilitation with teamwork or social loafing**.

## Constructed Application

> Synthetic application derived from the theory; not a quotation or documented case from Miles (2012).

Public dashboards improve experienced agents' routine processing but impair new agents learning exceptions. The manager uses private practice and coaching for novices, then gradually adds peer observation. Experts facing a critical presentation use rehearsal and reduced self-monitoring cues to avoid choking.

## Key Takeaways

1. Measure presence, evaluation, distraction, mastery, and task demand separately.
2. Avoid post hoc task classification.
3. Consider virtual and electronic audiences.
4. Test nonlinear arousal and individual differences.
