# Chapter 35: Social Identity Theory

## Core Idea

People derive part of the self from group memberships; salient categorization produces in-group identification, intergroup comparison, norm conformity, and efforts to maintain positive distinctiveness.

## Frameworks Introduced

- **Categorization–identification–comparison**: Classify self and others, internalize group membership, and compare in-groups with out-groups.
- **Positive distinctiveness**: Favorable intergroup comparison supports self-esteem and group value.
- **Identity management**: Under low-status identity, actors may pursue individual mobility, social creativity, or social competition depending on permeability, legitimacy, and stability.
- **Self-categorization**: Identity salience shifts perception from personal uniqueness toward group prototypes.

## Key Concepts

- **In-group / out-group**: Groups included in or excluded from the active self-category.
- **Prototype**: Contextual representation of what defines a group member.
- **Identification**: Self-definition in terms of group membership.
- **Identity salience**: Likelihood that a particular group identity guides perception and behavior.

## Mental Models

- Use **which “we” is active now?**
- Create superordinate identity when subgroups must cooperate, while retaining valued subgroup distinctiveness.
- Distinguish identification from commitment, prestige, and simple group membership.

## Anti-patterns

- **Assuming stronger identification always benefits the organization**.
- **Treating group categories as fixed and natural**.
- **Using demographic similarity as identity salience without evidence**.

## Constructed Application

> Synthetic application derived from the theory; not a quotation or documented case from Miles (2012).

After a merger, legacy identities drive mistrust. Declaring one new identity immediately threatens continuity. Leaders build a superordinate customer-safety identity, preserve meaningful legacy expertise, and design joint successes. The mechanism is recategorization and positive distinctiveness, not generic team building.

## Key Takeaways

1. Specify category, comparison out-group, and context of salience.
2. Model permeability, status legitimacy, and stability.
3. Distinguish personal, role, relational, and social identity.
4. Examine both solidarity and exclusion outcomes.
