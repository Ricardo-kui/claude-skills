# Chapter 37: Stakeholder Theory

## Core Idea

Organizations create and distribute value through relationships with multiple parties who affect or are affected by the firm; managerial judgment must identify legitimate stakes and handle interdependence rather than maximize shareholder wealth alone.

## Frameworks Introduced

- **Descriptive, instrumental, and normative branches**: Describe stakeholder relations, predict performance consequences, or justify whose interests deserve consideration.
- **Stakeholder salience**: Power, legitimacy, and urgency shape managerial attention; salience is observed priority, not necessarily moral priority.
- **Stakeholder mapping**: Identify interests, contributions, harms, dependencies, influence, and relationships among stakeholders.
- **Value creation and trade-offs**: Seek joint gains where possible and make unavoidable trade-offs explicit and procedurally defensible.

## Key Concepts

- **Stakeholder**: Party that affects or is affected by organizational activity.
- **Stake**: Interest, right, contribution, exposure, or claim at issue.
- **Power**: Capacity to influence the organization.
- **Legitimacy / urgency**: Social appropriateness of a claim and time sensitivity of needed response.

## Mental Models

- Use **who creates value, bears risk, holds rights, and can influence action?**
- Separate ethical standing from strategic salience.
- Treat stakeholder relationships as a system; satisfying one party can alter others' outcomes.

## Anti-patterns

- **Everyone is a stakeholder, therefore no prioritization**.
- **Using salience as moral justification**.
- **Claiming win–win while hiding distributional losses**.

## Constructed Application

> Synthetic application derived from the theory; not a quotation or documented case from Miles (2012).

A plant closure benefits shareholders but harms employees and a dependent community. Mapping identifies legal rights, dependence, alternatives, urgency, and long-term supply effects. Management explores phased closure and redeployment, then records remaining trade-offs and compensation rather than asserting that aggregate value resolves all claims.

## Key Takeaways

1. State whether the argument is descriptive, instrumental, or normative.
2. Define stakeholder boundaries and stakes.
3. Analyze interdependence and distribution.
4. Use dialogue and fair process without assuming participation eliminates conflict.
