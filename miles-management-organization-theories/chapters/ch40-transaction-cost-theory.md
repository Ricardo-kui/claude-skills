# Chapter 40: Transaction Cost Theory

## Core Idea

Choose governance arrangements that economize on production and transaction costs by matching exchange hazards—especially asset specificity, uncertainty, and frequency—with safeguards and adaptation mechanisms.

## Frameworks Introduced

- **Make–buy–hybrid choice**: Markets use prices, hierarchies use authority, and hybrids combine contractual and relational safeguards.
- **Transaction dimensions**: Asset specificity, uncertainty/complexity, and frequency determine governance needs.
- **Behavioral assumptions**: Bounded rationality makes complete contracts impossible; opportunism makes unprotected dependence hazardous.
- **Fundamental transformation**: Competitive bidding can become bilateral dependence after transaction-specific investment.
- **Discriminating alignment**: Match transactions with governance structures that minimize comparative costs.

## Key Concepts

- **Asset specificity**: Value loss when an asset is redeployed outside a focal transaction.
- **Transaction costs**: Search, negotiation, monitoring, adaptation, and enforcement costs.
- **Safeguard**: Contractual, relational, or ownership mechanism protecting investment.
- **Maladaptation**: Costly failure to adjust when circumstances change.

## Mental Models

- Use **transaction attributes → hazards → governance → comparative cost**.
- Compare feasible alternatives, not markets with an ideal hierarchy.
- Include production efficiencies and governance costs together.

## Anti-patterns

- **Make everything specific in-house** without measuring bureaucratic costs.
- **Using trust as absence of governance**; relational governance is itself a mechanism.
- **Treating opportunism as observed misconduct only**; credible hazard can shape ex ante design.

## Constructed Application

> Synthetic application derived from the theory; not a quotation or documented case from Miles (2012).

A manufacturer needs a custom component requiring supplier-specific tooling. Spot purchase risks hold-up after investment; full integration sacrifices supplier scale advantages. A long-term hybrid contract with volume commitments, shared investment, audit rights, and adaptation clauses may minimize comparative cost.

## Key Takeaways

1. Define the transaction as unit of analysis.
2. Measure multiple forms of asset specificity and uncertainty.
3. Compare market, hybrid, and hierarchy costs.
4. Address governance endogeneity: risky transactions select stronger safeguards.
