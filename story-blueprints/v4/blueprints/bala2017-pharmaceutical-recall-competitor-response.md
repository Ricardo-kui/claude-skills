## Story Reading

### Theme question

When a competitor's product may be recalled, how should a multi-product firm defend the threatened category before uncertainty resolves and then allocate sales effort across its portfolio if the recall occurs?

### Whole-story synopsis

The paper opens with Vioxx to unsettle the usual story that a rival's recall simply frees demand for competitors. A recalled product can taint its entire category, so the competitor whose product remains on the market may lose patients and physicians unless it distinguishes the category from the recalled product. The black-box warning supplies the crucial temporal turn: the focal firm has an anticipated recall probability, not yet a realized windfall. It can first invest in category-defense effort through physician detailing or direct-to-consumer communication to retain switchers, then, once the rival's recall state is known, deploy sales effort for its product in the affected category W and for a product in an unaffected category B. The theoretical middle makes this sequence consequential. A loyal category segment determines how much market survives without defense; recall probability sets the expected return to early defense; relative category value shapes allocation; and cross-category sales-resource scope determines whether promoting one product helps or hurts the other. The propositions show that under economies of scope, post-recall effort tends to rise or fall together across W and B and rising recall probability makes pre-event defense and W effort complementary. Under diseconomies, the firm can favor one category while cutting the other, or even set one product's effort to zero; defense and W effort can instead be substitutes. The discussion returns to a practical correction of the opening intuition: the relevant response is not automatically more promotion after a rival's recall, but a conditional sequence of category preservation and portfolio allocation. These are model-derived results, not observed competitor behavior.

### Characters and storylines

- **Focal character:** the multi-product competitor, because it must convert a rival's possible recall into both an early category-defense choice and a later portfolio allocation.
- **Trigger character:** the rival product's black-box warning and possible recall, because it creates uncertain future category harm rather than a completed competitive opportunity.
- **Category characters:** affected category W and unaffected category B, because their joint use of sales resources makes the response more than a single-product decision.
- **Demand characters:** loyal physicians/patients and switchers, because loyal demand survives category concern while switchers can leave unless category-defense information retains them.
- **Resource character:** cross-category sales-resource scope ($rho$), because positive scope links effort gains across products whereas negative scope turns one product's promotion into a cost for the other.
- **Storyline 1:** warning and recall probability -> category-defense effort -> preserved category market for switchers.
- **Storyline 2:** realized recall -> post-recall sales effort in W and B -> allocation determined by scope, category value, and remaining loyal demand.
- **Intersection:** the early defense choice changes the market that later sales effort can capture, but resource scope decides whether exploiting that market reinforces or displaces the other product.

### Five acts

- **Exposition:** Vioxx shows that a rival recall can contaminate a category and threaten, rather than simply reward, the remaining competitor.
- **Rising action:** A black-box warning gives the focal firm a recall probability; category-defense effort can retain switchers before the uncertainty resolves, while a two-product sales force introduces cross-category scope.
- **Climax:** The equilibrium shows that with economies of scope, sufficiently large loyal demand, or intermediate loyalty plus sufficiently high recall probability, makes post-recall effort rise in both categories.
- **Falling action:** With low loyalty, category defense can substitute for post-recall effort; with diseconomies, effort can move in opposite directions across W and B or be shut off for one product, and higher recall probability does not guarantee complementarity.
- **Denouement:** Competitor response becomes a conditional portfolio-management problem: defend category viability first, then exploit the remaining opportunity only where its resource return warrants it.

### Tension

- **Source:** A rival's recall may release sales to a competitor but can also make physicians and patients abandon the whole category; the competitor must act before knowing whether the recall will occur.
- **Construction:** The paper makes the temporal and portfolio problem legible by separating pre-recall category defense from post-recall product effort and allowing the shared sales resource to be either complementary or conflicting.

### Alternative readings

- **author-signaled-alternative:** Prior work and managerial observations suggest competitors often increase post-recall sales effort, but the paper argues that category-level damage, expected recall probability, and portfolio resource scope make that modal reaction incomplete.
- **analyst_counterfactual:** The model assumes the black-box warning is a usable precursor and that category defense can retain switchers without an offsetting credibility penalty. In markets where early defense is not visible, trusted, or permissible, the proposed pre-event link may not organize competitor response.

- **Character discipline:** `works` — loyal demand, recall probability, category defense, and sales-resource scope have distinct roles in the focal firm's two-stage decision.
- **Knot integrity:** `works` — the paper identifies a genuine conflict between preserving a threatened category and reallocating shared sales resources across products.
- **Plot emergence:** `works` — the two-stage game follows directly from the warning-before-recall timing, and the scope parameter produces the paper's non-obvious complementarity, substitution, and withdrawal outcomes.
- **Tie–unravel alignment:** `partly_works` — the propositions answer the stated analytical question, but no data test whether the empirical precursor, segment structure, or resource scope actually produce the modeled response.

- **Boundary:** This evaluates storytelling, not the realism of the model assumptions, the prevalence of pharmaceutical recall responses, or research quality.

## Learning Affordances

### Introduction and Theory

Use this card when an external event is anticipated rather than already realized, threatens a wider category as well as a focal rival, and leaves a multi-product actor with a real pre-event and post-event allocation sequence. It is not a generic story of competitors benefiting from bad news, nor a reason to add a pre-event stage without a credible precursor and choice.

### Results and Discussion

The useful structural lesson is to reveal conditional regions that change the direction of a response, rather than force one average prediction: shared-resource economies make early defense and later effort reinforce each other, while diseconomies can create substitution or withdrawal. Its mechanism evidence is `not_directly_tested`; the regions are theory outputs, not empirical behavioral estimates.
