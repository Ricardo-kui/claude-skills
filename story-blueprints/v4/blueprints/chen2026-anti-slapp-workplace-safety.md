## Story Reading

### Theme question

Can legal protection against retaliatory suits make stakeholder voice a governance mechanism that improves workplace safety, and through what establishment-level responses would that occur?

### Whole-story synopsis

The paper expands governance beyond investors, boards, and regulators by arguing that nonfinancial stakeholders can discipline firms only if they can safely voice hidden risks. Anti-SLAPP statutes supply the external intervention: lowering the expected cost of speaking makes unsafe conditions harder to suppress. The theoretical middle turns this legal protection into a safety story. More credible employee and public voice raises the prospective cost of exposure, which should lead establishments to prevent hazards through closer headquarters monitoring and lower workload pressure. A within-firm, cross-establishment stacked DiD is offered as the evidence arena because establishments of one firm can face different state protections in the same year. The results show lower injury rates and counts after adoption, no significant pretrends, stronger effects for stronger statutes and where workforce retention or local reputation makes exposure costlier. The final analyses associate the effect with headquarters proximity, shorter hours per employee, and staffing growth. The conclusion returns to the opening claim that speech protection can have operational consequences, although the central voice-to-accountability mechanism remains an inference rather than an observed sequence.

### Characters and storylines

- **Institutional character:** anti-SLAPP statutes, because they change the expected legal cost of public participation rather than directly regulating safety.
- **Voice character:** employees and external observers, whose ability to surface local hazards is the link between legal protection and accountability in the authors' account.
- **Organizational characters:** headquarters monitoring and establishment workload/staffing decisions, proposed as the proximate operational responses.
- **Outcome character:** workplace injuries, the concrete harm that turns abstract speech rights into operational stakes.
- **Boundary characters:** labor mobility, rival concentration, social capital, and local political preference, which are intended to mark settings where exposure is more costly.
- **Storyline:** anti-SLAPP protection → less chilling of stakeholder speech → more credible exposure and accountability → monitoring and workload adjustment → fewer injuries.

### Five acts

- **Exposition:** Conventional governance overlooks stakeholders who lack formal levers yet possess local knowledge of concealed workplace hazards.
- **Rising action:** Anti-SLAPP laws lower the cost of speaking; the authors argue that anticipated scrutiny makes safety neglect costlier and motivates monitoring and workload adjustment.
- **Climax:** Within-firm cross-establishment estimates show declines in injury rates and counts after adoption.
- **Falling action:** Dynamic, statutory-strength, and exposure-cost tests reinforce the pattern; proximity and workload/staffing analyses are introduced as probes of the proposed responses.
- **Denouement:** The paper recasts free-speech protection as an institutional complement to internal governance and a lever for safer work.

### Tension

- **Source:** Workers may be the best local sensors of hazards while lacking the contractual or legal power to make their information consequential.
- **Construction:** The paper makes the gap actionable by treating protection from silencing litigation as a change in the expected visibility and cost of unresolved hazards.

### Alternative readings

- **author-signaled-alternative:** The authors acknowledge that firm-wide safety campaigns or compliance systems could follow adoption, but argue that firm-year fixed effects absorb uniform corporate responses.
- **analyst_counterfactual:** Adoption may coincide with other state-level changes that affect injury reporting or safety practice, and proximity can proxy for more than monitoring. The within-firm design and event patterns support the association, but neither stakeholder voice nor audit activity is directly observed.

## Learning Affordances

### Introduction and Theory

This card is useful when an external legal rule changes a stakeholder's capacity to make private information consequential, and that change plausibly creates a named accountability response. It does not license generic claims that a legal reform improves every socially valued outcome.

### Methods, Results, and Discussion

The card is especially useful for linking the legal, organizational, and establishment levels in one evidence arena. Its calibration is `partly_probed`: injuries and operational adjustments are observed, but protected speech, visibility, and monitoring are not directly measured.
