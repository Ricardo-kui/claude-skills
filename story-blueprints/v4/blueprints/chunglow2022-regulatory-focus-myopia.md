## Story Reading

### Theme question

When a firm faces short-term earnings pressure, why does a promotion-focused CEO become more willing to cut marketing and R&D for immediate results, while a prevention-focused CEO is more likely to preserve the budget despite the near-term penalty?

### Whole-story synopsis

The paper begins with the practical and theoretical problem of marketing myopia: cutting marketing and R&D can protect current earnings and stock price, but it borrows from future firm value. Rather than treating this as a uniform market-pressure response, the authors make the CEO's regulatory focus the middle of the decision. Under short-term pressure, promotion focus makes the immediate stock-price gain salient and permits a change from the established budget; prevention focus foregrounds the potentially larger long-term loss and prefers the safety of maintaining the status quo. This reverses a tempting but incomplete intuition that promotion-oriented leaders always prefer distant aspirations: here time is a constraint rather than a resource. Equity compensation, analyst coverage, and environmental turbulence then earn their places by changing the personal long-term gain, short-term benchmark salience, or long-term value loss within the same calculus. The empirical analysis connects predominance in textual CEO focus to an archival measure of unusually reduced marketing/R&D spending alongside unusually high profitability, then shows its relation to later firm performance. The Discussion returns to the opening paradox: a motivational orientation associated with seeking gains can produce short-termism precisely because the available decision window makes an immediate gain more psychologically compelling than a later loss.

### Characters and storylines

- **Decision character:** myopic marketing management, the discretionary cut that improves current earnings while eroding longer-term value.
- **Motivational characters:** promotion and prevention focus, because they make gains/non-gains and losses/non-losses differently salient in the same budget decision.
- **Temporal stakes:** near-term stock-price reaction and longer-term firm-value loss, which give the two foci substantively different reasons to act or preserve the budget.
- **Boundary characters:** equity compensation, analyst coverage, and environmental turbulence, which respectively modify long-run personal gains, immediate benchmark pressure, and the cost of the later loss.
- **Storyline 1:** promotion focus → immediate achievement gain and willingness to depart from the budget → greater propensity for myopic marketing.
- **Storyline 2:** prevention focus → attention to the larger long-term loss and aversion to commission error → budget preservation.
- **Intersection:** the paper explains a temporal strategy through the changing psychological valuation of the very gains and losses that the decision creates.

### Five acts

- **Exposition:** CEOs face pressure to deliver current results while marketing spending creates value over time; budget cuts make the conflict especially acute.
- **Rising action:** Regulatory-focus theory is reframed for time-constrained decisions, pairing gain/loss salience with preference for action versus maintaining an established strategy.
- **Climax:** Predominantly promotion-focused CEOs are associated with more myopic marketing management, while prevention focus points in the opposite direction.
- **Falling action:** Compensation, analyst pressure, and turbulence qualify the same intertemporal calculation; later firm performance gives the budget cut its promised cost.
- **Denouement:** The paper concludes that regulatory focus cannot be read as a fixed long- or short-term trait apart from the temporal structure of the decision it faces.

### Tension

- **Source:** Promotion focus is commonly associated with ambitious future-oriented striving, yet the paper predicts more short-term budget cuts under earnings pressure.
- **Construction:** The authors resolve the apparent contradiction by changing the decision context: when time is scarce, the immediate gain from meeting a benchmark can dominate the distant aspiration that promotion focus might otherwise encourage.

### Alternative readings

- **author-signaled-alternative:** The paper explicitly distinguishes the setting from research in which promotion-focused individuals have ample time to pursue distant goals and correct mistakes.
- **analyst_counterfactual:** Regulatory-focus text may capture broader communication style or firm culture rather than a CEO's budget calculus. The results support the predicted pattern, while the specific salience process remains inferred.

## Learning Affordances

### Introduction and Theory

This card is useful for a study in which the same action creates different gains and losses across horizons, and a leader's orientation plausibly changes their relative salience. The transferable action is to make the temporal structure of the decision explicit before invoking executive cognition. It is not a generic basis for assigning promotion to risk-taking and prevention to caution.

### Methods, Results, and Discussion

The paper makes a downstream outcome earn its place by using it to close the short-term-gain/long-term-cost story. Its empirical calibration is `not_directly_tested`: the archival evidence supports the proposed decision pattern, not a directly observed gain–loss weighting process.
