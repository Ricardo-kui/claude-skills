## Story Reading

### Theme question

When accidents at one organization threaten the legitimacy of an entire field, which other organizations will publicly defend the field's ongoing practices rather than remain silent or distance themselves from the crisis?

### Whole-story synopsis

The paper opens with railroad accidents that become media investigations of systemic industry failure, turning a local incident into a legitimacy threat for firms beyond the named culprit. Existing crisis research explains how the directly involved organization defends itself, and institutional theory often treats fieldwide disruption as an opening for change. Desai instead asks what organizations do when they seek to preserve the field's existing legitimacy. The proposed action is defensive institutional work: public rhetoric that reassures constituents, disputes negative claims, attributes blame outside the field, or otherwise defends ongoing practices. Yet this is not a simple spillover-to-response chain. Visible defense can reduce contagion of negative judgment, but it can also look manipulative and draw more scrutiny; silence, distancing, or exit may be safer. The theory therefore asks when a potential responder both faces meaningful spillover and can expect its rhetoric to be credible. Fieldwide prior scrutiny makes negative judgment broad enough that silence no longer avoids exposure; core similarity with accident-prone firms can make a responder itself stigmatized and discourage defense; similarity in accidents plausibly induced by external conditions instead makes defense more defensible. Panel evidence from U.S. railroads shows that others' accidents reduce defensive statements on average, while high fieldwide scrutiny and externally induced similarity increase them, and core similarity reduces them. A supplemental analysis links defensive statements to later lower fieldwide safety scrutiny. The Discussion returns to the key reversal: post-crisis rhetoric can be field-preserving institutional work, but only where exposure and credibility make active defense more viable than avoidance.

### Characters and storylines

- **Initiating character:** accidents at other field members, because they generate a disruption without directly implicating every potential responder.
- **Threat character:** field legitimacy, the shared judgment about whether ongoing practices in the organizational field remain appropriate.
- **Response character:** defensive institutional work, public statements that seek to reassure, reinterpret, or otherwise protect the field's legitimacy.
- **Decision tension:** defensive rhetoric can contain spillover but can also attract skeptical attention; silence, distancing, and exit remain live alternatives.
- **Exposure boundary:** fieldwide issue scrutiny, which makes negative judgments diffuse broadly enough that avoiding attention no longer reliably protects a firm.
- **Credibility boundaries:** core similarity to stricken firms raises stigma and favors avoidance; similarity in externally inducible accidents makes collective defense more credible.
- **Storyline 1:** others' accidents → field legitimacy threat → a risky choice between defense and avoidance.
- **Storyline 2:** high fieldwide scrutiny → unavoidable spillover exposure → greater likelihood of defensive field rhetoric.
- **Storyline 3:** the type of similarity determines whether the responder looks tainted by the crisis or plausibly shares an external vulnerability worth defending.
- **Intersection:** organizations respond not simply to crisis magnitude, but to the joint prospects of being judged with the field and being believed when they defend it.

### Five acts

- **Exposition:** A railroad crisis becomes media scrutiny of systemic field failure, exposing organizations not directly involved in the accidents.
- **Rising action:** Institutional theory supplies defensive institutional work, but a competing avoidance logic establishes why field defense can backfire; scrutiny and similarity determine exposure and credibility.
- **Climax:** Others' accidents reduce defensive statements on average, establishing that visible defense is not an automatic response to field crisis.
- **Falling action:** High fieldwide issue scrutiny reverses the baseline pattern; core similarity suppresses defense while externally inducible accident similarity increases it, and supplemental analysis shows reduced later scrutiny following defense.
- **Denouement:** The field is recast as an object organizations may rhetorically preserve after crisis, though field defense is an option selected under conditions rather than an inevitable collective action.

### Tension

- **Source:** An uninvolved organization may need to protect the field's legitimacy from contagion, but publicly defending the field can itself appear manipulative and intensify suspicion.
- **Construction:** The paper does not eliminate that contradiction; it turns it into a selection problem in which scrutiny governs the cost of silence and similarity governs the credibility of defense.

### Alternative readings

- **author-signaled-alternative:** The paper treats defensive work and avoidance as competing responses. It also identifies exit from a stigmatized core group as an alternative to field defense rather than a failed instance of it.
- **analyst_counterfactual:** Defensive statements may follow latent industry coordination or unobserved safety conditions rather than organizations' estimated spillover and credibility calculations. The patterned interactions fit the argument, but those calculations are not directly observed.

## Learning Affordances

### Introduction and Theory

Use this paper when an event at one organization plausibly threatens a collective category, field, or stakeholder judgment, and nonfocal organizations face a real strategic choice between visible response and avoidance. The transferable move is to separate exposure to spillover from credibility of response. It is not a template for labeling every industry communication effort as institutional defense.

### Methods, Results, and Discussion

The negative baseline makes the conditional mechanism legible: crises can suppress visible defense unless the risk of silence or the credibility of response changes. Its empirical calibration is `partly_probed`: the paper observes defensive statements and a field-scrutiny outcome, not the predicted beliefs or legitimacy judgments inside the process.
