## Story Reading

### Theme question

How can institutional investors that own multiple firms in one industry use corporate social responsibility to manage the systematic risk that one portfolio firm's actions impose on its peers and on the investor's aggregate returns?

### Whole-story synopsis

The paper begins with a paradox in the rise of common ownership. The same ownership structure commonly criticized for softening competition also creates an investor problem that conventional diversification cannot solve: when the investor owns several firms in an industry, one firm's irresponsible conduct can damage the whole portfolio through stakeholder and regulatory spillovers. The authors call this the common-owner dilemma and make CSR the potential risk-management instrument. A focal firm's CSR can improve the industry's perceived legitimacy, attract stakeholders, and reduce the likelihood of costly fieldwide regulation; irresponsibility can do the reverse. Because common owners capture gains and losses across multiple peer firms, they can rationally favor CSR even where its standalone benefit to the focal firm is modest. The theory becomes more disciplined by asking when this portfolio logic should be strongest: owners need time for spillovers to emerge, stakeholder-sensitive industries make peer spillovers economically consequential, and financially material CSR offers the returns that a financially motivated owner has reason to pursue. The results show a positive common-ownership–CSR association, concentrated in long-term common ownership, stakeholder-sensitive industries, and material rather than immaterial CSR. A merger-based shock supports the association. The Discussion returns to the opening reversal: common ownership may change corporate behavior not only by reducing competitive rivalry but also by making firms' positive externalities portfolio-relevant.

### Characters and storylines

- **Structural character:** common institutional ownership across multiple firms in one industry, because it creates portfolio-wide exposure to peer spillovers.
- **Problem character:** systematic industry risk, which cannot be diversified away simply by owning more same-industry firms and is amplified by one firm's harmful behavior.
- **Strategic character:** corporate social responsibility, which can alter stakeholder perceptions and regulatory responses across industry peers.
- **Resolution-bearing character:** portfolio-level returns, because peer gains and reduced fieldwide losses make CSR economically meaningful to the common owner beyond a focal firm's isolated payoff.
- **Boundary characters:** long investment horizons allow spillovers to arrive; stakeholder-sensitive industries increase their economic force; material CSR identifies the financially consequential form of responsibility.
- **Storyline 1:** common ownership → heightened exposure to industry spillovers → incentive to raise portfolio firms' CSR.
- **Storyline 2:** focal CSR → stronger industry legitimacy and lower regulatory/reputational spillover risk → improved aggregate portfolio value.
- **Intersection:** a structural condition often read as an anticompetitive incentive becomes a distinct incentive to create positive externalities among firms the investor jointly owns.

### Five acts

- **Exposition:** Common ownership is rising and is largely viewed through a competition-harm lens, while its systematic-risk problem remains undertheorized.
- **Rising action:** CSR is recast as a spillover-producing strategy that can change stakeholder and regulatory responses across an industry; horizon, stakeholder sensitivity, and materiality establish when the portfolio logic matters.
- **Climax:** Common ownership is positively associated with firm CSR performance, primarily through increased CSR strengths.
- **Falling action:** Long-term owners, stakeholder-sensitive industries, and financially material CSR supply the predicted concentration; merger-based analyses reinforce the proposed structural relation.
- **Denouement:** The paper reopens the policy meaning of common ownership by adding a portfolio positive-externality possibility to the dominant concern about reduced competition.

### Tension

- **Source:** Owning multiple industry peers can create incentives to soften competition, but it also leaves the investor unusually exposed to harmful peer spillovers that no within-industry diversification can remove.
- **Construction:** The paper resolves neither side away; it identifies CSR as a separate portfolio strategy through which common owners may reduce shared risk and capture shared gains.

### Alternative readings

- **author-signaled-alternative:** The paper explicitly positions systematic-risk management as an alternative to, not a denial of, accounts in which common owners influence firms to compete less aggressively.
- **analyst_counterfactual:** Common owners might select firms already improving CSR rather than cause change. The natural-experiment analysis narrows this alternative, but the actual engagement and peer-spillover processes remain unobserved.

## Learning Affordances

### Introduction and Theory

Use this card where the same structural arrangement creates a neglected optimization problem that is distinct from, rather than opposite to, the established concern. The transferable move is to identify a cross-unit externality and show why the actor captures it at the portfolio level. It is not a generic argument that diversified investors always promote socially responsible action.

### Methods, Results, and Discussion

The card illustrates how diagnostic contingencies can make an alternative mechanism more credible than a broad main effect alone. Its empirical calibration is `not_directly_tested`: the evidence establishes the ownership–CSR pattern and its predicted locations, not the investor engagement or peer-spillover chain itself.
