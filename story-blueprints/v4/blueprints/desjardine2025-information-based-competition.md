## Story Reading

### Theme question

When institutional investors own both an ESG rating agency and firms that rival a target company, can those investors use the agency to lower the target's ESG rating, and when do changing reputational threats and opportunities make this indirect attack more likely?

### Whole-story synopsis

The paper begins with direct information-based competition: firms sometimes disclose unfavorable information about rivals, but visible attacks expose them to legal and reputational costs. Information intermediaries offer a less visible route because their ratings, coverage, and certifications shape stakeholders' perceptions while their own economic ties can leave room for outside influence. The authors make common ownership the bridge between competition and the intermediary, but the crucial topology is asymmetric: an investor holds ownership in both MSCI and firms that compete with the target, not simply in the target and its rater. That owner can benefit if MSCI's ESG coverage disadvantages the target relative to its portfolio rivals. The paper then rejects a static account of intermediary bias. Reputational controversies at rival firms create motivation to shift the information landscape against targets, while target controversies reduce the need for an additional attack. Rival ESG awards create an opportunity that makes raters more receptive to a favorable contrast; target awards protect against negative information. Analyses of MSCI ratings show that higher rival–MSCI common ownership is associated with lower target ESG ratings and that the relationship shifts with the predicted threat and opportunity windows. Robustness analyses examine score boundaries, alternative estimators, and quasi-natural shocks. The Discussion returns to the core contribution: competitive dynamics can be conducted not only by firms' own market actions but by outside owners shaping the intermediaries that determine how competitors are publicly evaluated.

### Characters and storylines

- **Target character:** the firm receiving an ESG rating from MSCI, because it bears the information disadvantage without necessarily being a party to the ownership tie.
- **Attacker-side characters:** rivals in the common owner's portfolio and their shared institutional investors, who benefit if the target's relative information position worsens.
- **Intermediary character:** MSCI ESG Research, whose authoritative ratings translate information into stakeholder perceptions and whose public ownership offers an influence channel.
- **Attack mechanism:** rival–MSCI common ownership, the specific bridge joining the target's rivals to the rating agency rather than a general measure of common ownership.
- **Dynamic boundaries:** rival controversies motivate attack, target controversies reduce its marginal value; rival awards make a favorable comparison more feasible, target awards reduce rater receptivity to negative information.
- **Storyline 1:** investor ownership in rival and MSCI → influence over information intermediary → lower ESG rating for target → relative competitive advantage for the rival portfolio.
- **Storyline 2:** changing reputational threats and opportunities create windows in which an indirect information attack is more attractive or more likely to gain traction.
- **Intersection:** the paper moves competition outside the direct firm-to-firm arena while retaining a precise account of who benefits, who is targeted, and through which intermediary channel.

### Five acts

- **Exposition:** Firms compete through direct actions and sometimes direct negative information, but information intermediaries can shape market hierarchy from outside the focal rivalry.
- **Rising action:** Ownership influence over a rating agency is joined to rivals' portfolio interests; reputational threats and opportunities explain dynamic motivation and intermediary receptivity.
- **Climax:** Targets receive less favorable MSCI ESG ratings when their rivals' institutional investors have greater ownership in MSCI.
- **Falling action:** Rival and target media controversies and ESG awards respectively strengthen or weaken the rating discount; threshold, robustness, and quasi-natural analyses bound the proposed effect.
- **Denouement:** The paper labels the resulting mechanism information-based competition and extends competitive dynamics to outsiders who shape the information sources governing firm reputations.

### Tension

- **Source:** Rating agencies are expected to serve as relatively authoritative evaluators, yet ownership by economically interested outsiders can make their coverage a channel for competition among firms they do not directly rate as owners.
- **Construction:** The paper makes the concern specific by separating the rater's investor, that investor's rival portfolio firms, and the exposed target, then using reputational windows to explain why influence need not be constant.

### Alternative readings

- **author-signaled-alternative:** The authors acknowledge that investor influence over rating agencies, executives catering to investor interests, or both could produce the pattern; their analyses do not adjudicate the internal route.
- **analyst_counterfactual:** Rival–MSCI ownership might correlate with information about target ESG quality that investors already possess. The quasi-natural and boundary analyses narrow some alternatives, but direct manipulation or communication is not observed.

## Story Assessment

- **Theme coherence:** `works` — the ownership topology, rating intermediary, target disadvantage, and reputational windows all serve one indirect-competition claim.
- **Character discipline:** `works` — the paper carefully separates target, rival, owner, and rater; that separation is necessary for the mechanism to remain intelligible.
- **Knot integrity:** `works` — the study reveals a consequential tension between trusted intermediary authority and outside economic influence.
- **Plot emergence:** `works` — the dynamic moderators arise from the motivation-and-receptivity logic rather than functioning as a generic ESG heterogeneity list.
- **Tie–unravel alignment:** `partly_works` — ratings and their predicted contingencies fit the information-based-competition claim, although the influence process inside the rating agency is unobserved.
- **Ending quality:** `works` — the Discussion returns to how competitive dynamics are changed when outsiders shape information rather than launch direct attacks.
- **Boundary:** This evaluates storytelling only; it is not a judgment about causal identification or research quality.

## Learning Affordances

### Introduction and Theory

Use this card when a competitive mechanism runs through an intermediary rather than a direct action by the focal firm, and the actor–intermediary–target topology can be specified clearly. The transferable move is to show why the outside actor benefits from shaping the intermediary's assessment. It is not a basis for assuming every evaluator with investors is biased.

### Methods, Results, and Discussion

The results are useful for learning how to keep a complex network mechanism legible by measuring and controlling each ownership side separately, then adding dynamic windows only after the main channel is established. Its empirical calibration is `not_directly_tested`: the rating pattern is observed, but the investor-to-rater influence and rater decision process are not.

## Comparison prompt

Which node is the target, which is the beneficiary, and which is the information conduit?
