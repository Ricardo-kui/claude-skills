## Story Reading

### Theme question

How does a peer audience evaluate a candidate after a non-peer audience has already valued that candidate, when the external evaluation can indicate ability but also possible deviation from the peer audience's expected identity?

### Whole-story synopsis

The paper opens with a familiar social-valuation inference: under uncertainty, evaluators rely on positive evaluations already conferred by others, producing reinforcing advantage among peers. It then makes that familiar mechanism unstable by moving from homogeneous peers to multiple audiences. An industry contract is not simply a positive signal transferred intact into academic peer review. For academic peers, it is simultaneously an external endorsement of a scientist's ability to execute and an increasingly conspicuous cue that the scientist may not conform to the identity of a proper academic. These meanings cannot be collapsed into a single common yardstick, but they are not wholly incommensurable either: industry recognition retains some ability relevance. The paper therefore predicts an inverted U, not because there are two audiences in the abstract, but because the positive ability reading is roughly linear while the identity-deviance concern grows at higher levels of external recognition. Identity proximity between audiences reduces the second reading; strong endogenous peer certification reduces reliance on the ambiguous external signal. The quantitative analyses establish the curve, turning point, and boundary patterns; matching and specification checks test robustness; interviews then show that evaluators themselves articulate both ability recognition and identity concerns. The Discussion returns to the opening Matthew-effect intuition and changes it: external valuation is an asset only up to the point at which it becomes identity liability to the focal audience.

### Characters and storylines

- **Main characters:** external (industry) evaluation, peer (academic) evaluation, imputed ability, and identity conformance.
- **Audience characters:** industry confers the observable exogenous index; academic peers interpret it and control the focal resource; the candidate is evaluated across both audiences.
- **Certification characters:** prior peer grants and publication quality/regularity are endogenous indices that make the ambiguous external signal less necessary and less damaging.
- **Boundary character:** audience identity proximity changes whether external success is read as deviance from the peer identity.
- **Storyline 1:** more external evaluation indicates competence to execute and initially increases peer valuation.
- **Storyline 2:** accumulating external evaluation increasingly signals departure from the peer audience's identity template and eventually decreases peer valuation.
- **Intersection:** one observable external signal carries two nonreducible meanings to the focal peer audience, yielding a bounded benefit rather than universal social reinforcement.

### Five acts

- **Exposition:** Social valuation research shows that positive prior evaluations reinforce later evaluations among homogeneous peers, but candidates commonly seek resources from multiple audiences.
- **Rising action:** The paper distinguishes endogenous from exogenous indices and separates ability from identity-conformance information. Industry contracts make the divergence observable; audience proximity and peer certification specify when it should matter less.
- **Climax:** Peer valuation rises and then falls with industry evaluation, with a turning point inside the observed range; the curve is flatter and shifts when identity proximity or endogenous academic standing reduces the identity concern or signal dependence.
- **Falling action:** Matching, alternative specifications, selection and endogeneity checks support the pattern; interviews articulate the two interpretive readings that the curve represents.
- **Denouement:** External appreciation becomes a bounded social-approval asset: it can validate ability without full identity compatibility, but excessive external recognition can undermine standing with the core peer audience.

### Tension

- **Source:** The dominant socially endogenous-inference account predicts reinforcing value from prior positive evaluation, yet its peer-homogeneity assumption leaves unclear what survives when recognition comes from an external audience with a different identity standard.
- **Construction:** The Introduction first establishes why existing peer reinforcement is plausible, then uses external audience evaluation to reveal a signal with two meanings. The Theory does not declare one audience right and the other wrong; it specifies why the focal peer evaluator can read the same signal as both ability and identity deviation.

### Alternative readings

- **author-stated:** The authors explicitly delimit their theory to cases with a moderate overlap in ability criteria. With radically distant audiences, external recognition may not signal ability at all, or may be read negatively from the outset.
- **analyst_counterfactual:** A generic “multiple audiences impose a trade-off” story would predict tension but would not explain why initial external recognition helps, why the penalty accelerates, or why peer certification and identity proximity flatten the curve.

## Story Assessment

- **Theme coherence:** `works` — the portability of prior evaluation across audiences stays central from the opening inference through the curve, moderators, interviews, and closure.
- **Character discipline:** `works` — ability and identity conformance remain analytically separate, while proximity and endogenous indices serve clear boundary roles.
- **Knot integrity:** `works` — the paper challenges a real but bounded peer-homogeneity assumption in socially endogenous inference, not merely a generic lack of multi-audience research.
- **Plot emergence:** `works` — the nonlinear prediction follows from distinct readings of one signal with different marginal effects; it is not imposed by the desire for a curve.
- **Tie–unravel alignment:** `partly_works` — the observed curve, moderations, and interview accounts align strongly with the promise, but interviews provide interpretive corroboration rather than direct process observation and some interaction components are marginal.
- **Ending quality:** `partly_works` — the ending changes the meaning of external approval from universally reinforcing asset to bounded asset/liability, though its long contribution inventory delays that return.
- **Boundary:** This evaluates storytelling only; it does not assess the causal identification, operational validity, or publication value of the study.

## Learning Affordances

### Introduction and Theory

Use Fini et al. for a cross-audience evaluation problem with partial, not total, criterion overlap: the focal audience can learn something about ability from another audience's evaluation but sees that evaluation as possible evidence of identity misfit. The transferable move is to derive the outcome from the two meanings of one signal, not to label all audience differences as a gap.

### Results

The results serve as a model only when a curvilinear claim has two specified mechanisms and observable bounds. The sequence—shape and turning point, boundary patterns, then evaluator accounts—helps the reader see why the curve represents the theory rather than an after-the-fact coefficient.

### Methods and Discussion

The multi-source evidence design is useful as a story-alignment standard: separately observe cross-audience recognition, focal valuation, and endogenous focal-audience standing. The paper closes most effectively when it turns a familiar reinforcement claim into a bounded cross-audience rule.

## Comparison prompt

Is the current study about one audience interpreting another audience's positive evaluation through both a shared ability baseline and a misaligned identity standard? If yes, Fini et al. is potentially relevant. If the same focal attribute is valued oppositely by two different audiences, consider the legacy Pontikes contrast; if two theories govern different ranges of a single X–Y relation, consider Ridge. Do not retrieve Fini merely because a study has several stakeholders or a curvilinear result.
