## Story Reading

### Theme question

Do geographical distance and geographical dispersion in a manufacturer's direct supply base create product quality risks through different mechanisms, and does the Chinese emerging-market context change the familiar presumption that distance is harmful and vertical integration protective?

### Whole-story synopsis

The paper opens with global supply bases as a source of quality risk and argues that past work has blurred two different spatial properties. Distance is the separation between buyer and supplier; dispersion is the spread of suppliers across heterogeneous places. The distinction is made consequential through two mechanisms: distance should increase dyadic monitoring and information-asymmetry problems, whereas dispersion should make it harder to coordinate quality standards and responses across a portfolio of supplier environments. The paper adds a contextual challenge: much prior evidence comes from WEIRD settings in which firms often source down an institutional gradient, but Chinese manufacturers may also source to higher-regulatory-quality countries. Vertical integration is initially proposed as a common remedy because ownership should improve both transparency and coordination. A Chinese automobile model-year-component panel measures system-level consumer complaints and models supplier selection and endogeneity. The main result sharply changes the initial symmetry: distance is not significantly associated with quality risk, while dispersion is positively associated. Vertical integration does not attenuate dispersion; it amplifies it. Post hoc analyses give the divergent paths content: strong supplier-country regulatory quality weakens the distance relationship, and supplier certifications weaken dispersion risk. The Discussion reframes the finding as a context-bounded warning against both a closer-is-safer heuristic and a generic integration remedy.

### Characters and storylines

- **Main characters:** geographical distance and geographical dispersion, because their conceptual separation is the paper's principal theoretical intervention.
- **Outcome character:** product quality risk, operationalized as cumulative consumer complaints for a car-model, model-year, and component-system combination; it captures realized system-level problems rather than verified supplier defects.
- **Distance mechanism character:** monitoring and information asymmetry in the buyer–supplier dyad.
- **Dispersion mechanism character:** coordination of quality standards and corrective action across multiple institutional locations.
- **Governance character:** vertical integration, initially framed as a common control remedy but empirically revealed to worsen the dispersion pathway.
- **Context character:** supplier-country regulatory quality, which can provide external quality infrastructure and reduce the need for direct buyer monitoring.
- **Storyline 1:** spatial distance may make a supplier harder to inspect and assist, yet the institutional quality of distant sourcing can offset that risk in China.
- **Storyline 2:** dispersion across heterogeneous locations increases quality-standard alignment and coordination burdens, and ownership can add rather than remove that burden.
- **Intersection:** geographical complexity is not one construct; its quality consequences depend on which spatial relationship is present and which institutional setting gives it meaning.

### Five acts

- **Exposition:** Global supply bases create visible quality failures, but the field often treats distance and dispersion as one general geographic-complexity problem and generalizes predominantly WEIRD evidence.
- **Rising action:** Transaction-cost reasoning assigns dyadic monitoring to distance and portfolio coordination to dispersion; vertical integration is proposed to mitigate both, while the Chinese setting makes institutional direction of sourcing relevant.
- **Climax:** The main models show no significant distance effect but a positive association of supply-base dispersion with quality risks.
- **Falling action:** Vertical integration unexpectedly strengthens dispersion risk; supplier-country regulatory quality weakens the distance pathway and quality certifications weaken the dispersion pathway. Alternative samples, measures, models, selection correction, and control functions probe robustness.
- **Denouement:** The Discussion turns the results into a bounded supply-base design lesson: neither proximity nor ownership is a universal quality safeguard, and non-WEIRD sourcing contexts can change the meaning of distance.

### Tension

- **Source:** The literature's common spatial-complexity intuition predicts that more distant and more dispersed suppliers should both be risky, yet their managerial problems and the institutional direction of sourcing are not the same.
- **Construction:** The paper separates the constructs before estimating them jointly, then allows null and reversal results to revise the initial intuition rather than hiding them behind a composite complexity label.

### Alternative readings

- **author-signaled-alternative:** The null distance finding may reflect Chinese firms' access to higher-quality suppliers and stronger external regulatory infrastructures at distant locations, rather than an absence of monitoring difficulty. The post hoc regulatory-quality results are consistent with this account but do not directly observe the proposed institutional learning or monitoring process.

## Learning Affordances

### Introduction and Theory

This card is useful when a field uses adjacent measures interchangeably even though they describe different organizational problems, and when the research setting changes a theory's causal environment. It is not a licence to manufacture two constructs merely by splitting a composite index.

### Methods and Results

The reusable action is to let separate constructs earn different empirical fates and to use targeted tests to interpret, not erase, a null main effect. The card also warns that a system-level quality measure cannot prove the proposed supplier-level mechanism.

### Discussion

The ending shows how an unexpected moderation result can revise a practical default—here, integration as a risk remedy—without claiming that the opposite prescription holds everywhere.
