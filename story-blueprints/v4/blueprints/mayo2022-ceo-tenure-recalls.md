## Story Reading

### Theme question

How does a CEO's tenure shape the hazard of voluntary product recalls when a new CEO can plausibly attribute a bad event to a predecessor but a long-tenured CEO may have more reason and discretion to avoid announcing it?

### Whole-story synopsis

The paper begins with recall as a feared C-suite event whose costs create incentives either to avoid a problem or to avoid blame for it. It transfers a finance-and-accounting account of tenure-dependent bad-news management into consumer-product recalls: incoming CEOs may disclose negative events while a predecessor can absorb blame, whereas later CEOs may avoid disclosing events once that attribution opportunity fades. Consumer-product recalls make that move plausible because they are rare, subject to comparatively weak CPSC intervention, and therefore likely to reach senior managerial discretion. Attribution theory anchors the early-tenure account; a forced departure makes the predecessor a more credible blame target. The later-tenure account centers on discretion: recalls omitted from financial reports are treated as more avoidable than those requiring disclosure. A recurrent-event hazard design finds a higher recall hazard early in tenure and a lower one late in tenure. The early pattern is stronger after a forced predecessor exit; the late pattern is present only among high-discretion recalls. Robustness checks address alternative tenure measures, selection, reverse causality, and operational-performance explanations. The Discussion returns to the safety cost of discretion, but the conclusion acknowledges that the study does not observe blame or concealment directly.

### Characters and storylines

- **Main character:** CEO tenure, because its early and late phases reorganize the possible handling of the same costly operational event.
- **Decision character:** voluntary recall hazard, an incidence-based operationalization of whether a firm initiates a recall, not a measure of post-awareness response duration.
- **Early-phase boundary character:** forced departure of the prior CEO, which makes attribution to a predecessor more plausible.
- **Late-phase boundary character:** recall discretion, proxied by whether the recall appears in a 10-K filing and therefore whether it seems difficult to leave unannounced.
- **Storyline 1:** early CEOs may bring bad news forward while responsibility can still be assigned to an already-discredited predecessor.
- **Storyline 2:** later CEOs may face stronger personal association with the firm's problems and have scope to avoid an announcement when the recall is discretionary.
- **Intersection:** the paper turns tenure from a background CEO attribute into a changing opportunity structure for handling a negative operational event.

### Five acts

- **Exposition:** Recall costs make a C-suite actor relevant; finance and accounting studies establish that CEO tenure can alter bad-news management.
- **Rising action:** Attribution theory distinguishes early opportunity to shift blame from later scope to avoid it; forced exit and discretion define the two boundary tests.
- **Climax:** Recurrent-event hazard estimates show a 77% higher recall hazard in early tenure and a 52% lower hazard in late tenure relative to middle tenure.
- **Falling action:** Forced predecessor exit strengthens the early pattern; late tenure predicts fewer high-discretion, but not low-discretion, recalls. Alternative models and operational-performance checks probe competing accounts.
- **Denouement:** The paper recasts recall incidence as a governance-sensitive decision and links reduced discretion to board and regulatory transparency, while acknowledging unobserved motives and time-varying factors.

### Tension

- **Source:** The same CEO tenure trajectory can make recalling more attractive at the beginning and less attractive later, even though the operational problem has not become inherently less consequential.
- **Construction:** The paper makes the reversal credible by assigning different opportunities to the two tenure phases rather than treating tenure as a linear measure of experience or competence.

### Alternative readings

- **author-signaled-alternative:** Later-tenure firms may simply produce higher-quality goods or retain better leaders, rather than strategically avoid recalls. Operational-performance measures make that explanation less consistent with the main pattern but cannot fully separate recall need from decision willingness.

## Learning Affordances

### Introduction and Theory

This card is useful when one changing actor position can plausibly produce opposite actions at different stages, and each action has a distinct, testable opportunity condition. It is not a generic invitation to give every nonlinear tenure result two psychological explanations.

### Methods and Results

The card distinguishes recall incidence from awareness-to-initiation timing. Its useful lesson is to make boundary tests bear the weight of a proposed temporal explanation while clearly labeling what remains inferred.

### Discussion

The ending offers a bounded way to translate a discretion finding into governance implications: reduce the unobserved decision space rather than claim to have diagnosed individual intent.
