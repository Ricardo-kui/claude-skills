## Story Reading

### Theme question

When the law makes a firm's trade secrets safer, do firms actually do more to turn those secrets into market outcomes—specifically, do they advertise more—or does protection simply relax competitive pressure and let them advertise less?

### Whole-story synopsis

The paper opens with a genuine economic puzzle: trade secrets are worth trillions and their theft costs hundreds of billions, yet protection is only valuable if firms convert secrets into commercial outcomes—and almost nothing is known about whether stronger protection changes firms' marketing actions. The question is made real by two opposed, well-cited predictions: protection lowers competitive pressure (pointing to less advertising, per Klasa et al. 2018) or it raises capital-market growth expectations that redirect managerial attention toward leveraging secrets (pointing to more). The attention-based view supplies the plot engine: attention is scarce, IDD recognition frees attention previously spent guarding secrets, and salient investor and analyst growth expectations pull that freed attention toward advertising as the value-appropriation lever. The staggered recognition of the Inevitable Disclosure Doctrine by U.S. state courts provides the natural experiment. The evidence answers the front-end question decisively in the attention direction: advertising rises about 13.5% after recognition, falls again when GA and OH reject the doctrine, and the effect is strongest exactly where the attention logic says it should be—weaker under CEO duality, stronger for younger firms and in high-peer-advertising industries. Post hoc probes in the Discussion (more brand talk in 10-Ks, higher analyst revenue forecasts) triangulate the attention mechanism, and a sales analysis closes the value loop: protection translates into higher sales only for firms that increase advertising. The ending returns to the opening premise and upgrades it—trade secret law is not merely an anti-theft device but an institution that enables leveraging, with state-level growth and job implications.

### Characters and storylines

- **Main character(s):** Advertising spending—the firm action whose journey the whole paper tracks (from unexamined outcome to demonstrated response, moderator-sensitive, and finally sales-linked); and IDD recognition—the institutional event whose staggered arrival drives the plot. Managerial attention is the story engine that connects them.
- **Supporting character(s):** Capital-market expectations (investors/analysts), which supply the motive for the attention shift; CEO duality, firm age, and peer advertising spending, which modulate how much attention the shock captures; the prior "reduced employee mobility" mechanism, which plays the incumbent explanation the paper complements rather than defeats.
- **Storyline(s):** The legal storyline (trade secret law from anti-theft to leverage-enabling) and the managerial storyline (attention from protection to leveraging) run in parallel and intersect at IDD recognition; the moderator storyline (who pays attention) branches off and rejoins in the Discussion's contingency and policy implications. The sales storyline provides the closing proof that the attention shift is consequential.

### Five acts

- **Exposition:** Trade secrets are enormously valuable, theft is rampant, firms protect via law—but protection without leverage has limited value, and the marketing side of leverage is unknown (with prior aggregated-measure studies finding null effects).
- **Rising action:** The bidirectional prediction is staged; ABV reframes the shock as an attention event driven by capital-market growth expectations; H1 asserts the increase, and three attention contingencies (CEO duality, firm age, peer advertising) become H2-H4.
- **Climax:** The staggered DiD reveals a statistically and economically significant ~13.5% advertising increase after IDD recognition, with parallel trends supported—and the GA/OH rejection counterfactual moves the outcome the other way.
- **Falling action:** All three moderators confirm the attention contingencies with large economic contrasts; a robustness battery (UTSA, DTSA window, stacked DiD, placebo DVs) holds the line; post hoc probes show more brand emphasis in 10-Ks and higher analyst revenue forecasts; sales rise only for firms that increase advertising.
- **Denouement:** The paper returns to its opening and transforms it: trade secret protection's purpose is recast as enabling leverage, not merely preventing theft, with implications for young-firm dynamism, state-level advertising jobs, and a corrective to the "protection suppresses innovation" view.

### Tension (optional)

- **Source:** Two literatures with credible evidence point in opposite directions from the same legal change—competitive-pressure relief says advertising falls; attention and capital-market expectations say it rises. A secondary knot is empirical: prior studies bundling advertising into R&D+SG&A aggregates found null effects, leaving the true marketing response obscured by measurement.
- **Construction:** The introduction voices both predictions explicitly ("on the one hand… on the other hand") before the ABV resolves them, and the literature review (with Table 1's four-domain map) shows the null-effect aggregates as a solvable measurement problem, making the contribution feel earned rather than asserted.

### Alternative readings (optional)

- **analyst_counterfactual:** One could read the paper as a measurement-correction story first (disaggregating advertising from R&D/SG&A bundles) with the ABV mechanism as framing; the authors instead foreground the attention theory, which is what gives the moderators and the Discussion's policy reframe their coherence.

## Story Assessment

- **Theme coherence:** `works` — "does protection change firms' efforts to leverage trade secrets through advertising?" organizes every section: theory derives it, design identifies it, moderators refine it, and the Discussion returns to it as a policy claim.
- **Character discipline:** `works` — the shock, the action, and the attention engine stay distinct; the three moderators are explicitly subordinated to one attention scheme (individual/organizational/contextual); no orphan storyline, and the incumbent mobility mechanism is kept in a complementary supporting role.
- **Tie–unravel alignment:** `partly_works` — the evidence answers the directional question the front end made salient, including a reversal counterfactual; however, the promised attention mechanism is only probed post hoc in the Discussion (10-K brand words, analyst forecasts), never designed for in Methods, so the unraveling of the "why" lags the tie.
- **Ending quality:** `works` — the Discussion returns to the opening premise and changes its meaning (protection as leverage-enabling rather than anti-theft), adds a sales-conditional payoff and state-level stakes, and corrects prior aggregated-measure conclusions rather than merely restating findings.
- **Boundary:** This evaluates storytelling, not research quality or identification.

## Learning Affordances

### Introduction

- **Suitable:** `yes`
- **Learn:** (1) Make the empirical question real by staging two opposed, literature-backed predictions before letting theory resolve them. (2) Preview three contributions aimed at three distinct audiences (theory mechanism, contingency/policy, measurement correction), so the paper reads as doing three jobs at once.
- **Do not copy:** The heavy result preview (effect size and mechanism findings stated in the introduction) and the $5-trillion/$225-600-billion stakes framing are specific to a policy-salient legal shock; without genuinely contested directional priors, the bidirectional staging becomes rhetorical theater.

### Theory

- **Suitable:** `yes`
- **Learn:** (1) Use attention scarcity to convert an institutional event into a resource-allocation prediction: the shock both frees attention (from protection) and redirects it (via capital-market expectations)—two why-chains converging on one hypothesis. (2) Organize three moderators under one explicit scheme of attention determinants so the contingency framework is a single argument.
- **Do not copy:** The mechanism is narratively carried by H1 alone, with no mechanism hypothesis and no attention measure in the design; writers whose journals expect designed mechanism tests should not imitate this load-bearing single-hypothesis structure.

### Methods

- **Suitable:** `partial`
- **Learn:** Match staggered court rulings to headquarters-state exposure and address outcome-disclosure selection with a two-stage IMR built on sector- and auditor-peer disclosure exclusions.
- **Do not copy:** The design-to-story alignment gap—no pre-announcement of the mechanism tests the theory promises—is the card's flagged seam (L2 C3 flag), not a feature to emulate.

### Results

- **Suitable:** `yes`
- **Learn:** (1) Sequence evidence as raw descriptives → parallel-trends dynamics → focal DiD → rejection counterfactual → augmented/stacked DiD → placebo DVs, so validity reads as escalation rather than an appendix list. (2) Give moderators economic-magnitude contrasts (53%-100% gaps) instead of significance-only reporting.
- **Do not copy:** The results arc is hypothesis-only by design here; deferring all mechanism evidence to the Discussion worked for this JM paper but leaves Results unable to pay off the theory's central promise on its own.

### Discussion

- **Suitable:** `partial`
- **Learn:** (1) Close by transforming the opening premise—recast the legal institution's purpose from protection to leverage—rather than summarizing coefficients. (2) Embed post hoc mechanism probes as "additional analyses" inside the implications narrative, an observed JM convention for late-arriving evidence.
- **Do not copy:** This is the imperfect-paper learning signal: post hoc mechanism placement reads smoothly here, but it cannot be cited as exemplary mechanism-testing practice, and the sales-conditional performance claim rests on a covariate-absorption argument that should not be generalized.
