## Story Reading

### Theme question

Why do reputation and celebrity—two favorable but often conflated social-approval assets—make firms differently prone to material earnings surprises and make investors read positive and negative surprises differently?

### Whole-story synopsis

The paper begins with a mature intangible-assets literature whose labels and measures have obscured whether its social-approval assets are the same thing. It separates high reputation, collective recognition of demonstrated quality and value, from celebrity, public attention coupled with positive emotional resonance. The theoretical middle makes this more than a definitional exercise. Reputation is built through consistent valued performance, so it should produce predictable outcomes and provide investors an analytical ability frame. Celebrity is built through dramatic, nonconforming action and affective media narratives, so it makes surprises more likely and gives investors an affective potential frame. Material earnings surprises become the shared evidence arena: they are consequential deviations that require investors to make sense of both good and bad news. The panel and event-study evidence supports the expected divergence for positive surprises—reputable firms are less likely and celebrity firms more likely to generate them; both receive higher rewards, with celebrity rewarded most. Both assets also buffer negative-surprise reactions relative to firms with neither asset, but neither predicted asset effect on generating negative surprises nor reputation's predicted stronger negative buffer is supported. The Discussion therefore preserves the main lesson, that social approval is not one asset or simple visibility, while narrowing the claimed differences to the conditions in which they materialize.

### Characters and storylines

- **Main characters:** high reputation and celebrity, because the paper asks readers to stop treating favorable collective perception as one interchangeable asset.
- **Formation characters:** consistent value delivery builds reputation; dramatic nonconforming action plus media narrative builds celebrity.
- **Interpretive characters:** an analytical ability frame and an affective potential frame explain why investors do not read equal surprises identically.
- **Event character:** material positive and negative earnings surprises, which expose predictability expectations and make the frames consequential.
- **Storyline 1:** reputation → reliable performance and predictability → fewer positive surprises; an ability frame partly cushions surprise reactions.
- **Storyline 2:** celebrity → dramatic, less predictable action → more positive surprises; affective resonance magnifies reward for good news and cushions bad news.
- **Intersection:** two assets can both be favorable yet generate different outcome propensities and different forms of investor sensemaking.

### Five acts

- **Exposition:** Social approval assets matter, but proliferating labels and shared proxies have fragmented theory about their distinctive effects.
- **Rising action:** Reputation and celebrity are defined by different formation processes and then recast as analytical versus affective frames for interpreting unexpected outcomes.
- **Climax:** Reputation predicts fewer and celebrity predicts more positive material surprises; both assets produce more favorable market reactions, with celebrity receiving the largest reward for positive news.
- **Falling action:** The negative-surprise tests show buffering by both assets but do not support the predicted differences in surprise propensity or reputation's stronger buffer.
- **Denouement:** The paper concludes that researchers and managers must distinguish social approval assets and their components instead of equating either with visibility, supporting this with untabulated checks in which visibility-only and affect-only firms underperform celebrity in both valences, and warning that absent recognition of ability or positive emotional resonance, visibility alone may not be beneficial.

### Tension

- **Source:** Favorable social approval is commonly treated as one performance-enhancing resource, yet the underlying recognition of demonstrated ability and the emotional pull of celebrity are generated differently and can make an unexpected event mean different things.
- **Construction:** The paper turns a construct problem into a consequential test by using positive and negative earnings surprises, where predictability, performance, diagnosticity, and affect have different roles.

### Alternative readings

- **author-signaled-alternative:** The authors note that either asset could plausibly worsen reactions to a negative surprise because it raises expectations; their prediction instead relies on ability-based diagnosticity for reputation and affective attenuation for celebrity.
- **analyst_counterfactual:** High reputation and celebrity may partly reflect unmeasured firm quality, visibility, or investor attention rather than separate frames that cause behavior and interpretation. The measured components distinguish the assets, but the proposed firm and investor processes are inferred.

## Learning Affordances

### Introduction and Theory

Use this card when a literature has genuinely collapsed conceptually distinct collective evaluations and the proposed constructs can be distinguished by both their generation and their audience meaning. The transferable action is to let the distinction make a later event interpretable in two different ways. Do not copy its paired labels, positive/negative earnings setting, or the premise that all favorable perceptions are social-approval assets.

### Methods, Results, and Discussion

The results provide a useful model of retaining nulls that limit a symmetric prediction matrix. The empirical calibration is `not_directly_tested`: it observes asset classifications, surprises, and returns, not the hypothesized behavioral formation or investor-processing mechanisms.
