## Story Reading

### Theme question

How does CEO–TMT pay disparity affect firm performance when social comparison and tournament theories appear to make opposite predictions but may apply at different levels of the same pay gap?

### Whole-story synopsis

The paper begins from a genuine conflict: economics and finance work associates greater pay disparity with higher performance, whereas organizational and management work associates it with lower performance. It rejects two weak exits—treating the relationship as meaningless or merely saying that context matters—and returns to what each theory actually assumes. Social comparison depends on a credible similar other, which very large pay gaps undermine; tournament theory depends on a prize large enough to motivate competition, which only very large gaps supply. The paper therefore makes pay disparity not only the antecedent but also the condition that changes which mechanism is relevant. The resulting theory predicts lower performance as disparity moves from low to moderate levels, followed by higher performance as disparity becomes large enough for tournament incentives: a U-shaped relationship. Its design then makes heir apparent and CEO power mechanism tests because both should suppress the high-gap tournament arm, while TMT eligibility should strengthen both comparison and tournament effects. Results reveal the U-shaped relationship and then use these moderating patterns to support the proposed domain shift. The Discussion returns to the original theoretical split and concludes that the theories are complementary across ranges rather than globally contradictory.

### Characters and storylines

- **Main characters:** CEO–TMT pay disparity and firm performance. The paper's central question is whether the same disparity has a stable relationship with performance.
- **Mechanism characters:** social comparison and tournament incentives. They do not govern different outcomes; they compete to explain the same X-to-Y relationship at different levels of X.
- **Supporting characters:** heir apparent and CEO power weaken the succession tournament; TMT eligibility strengthens both comparison and tournament participation.
- **Storyline 1:** at low-to-moderate disparity, a sufficiently similar CEO is a comparison target, so relative deprivation harms team functioning and performance.
- **Storyline 2:** at high disparity, similarity-based comparison weakens while the CEO role becomes a sufficiently large tournament prize, so competition improves performance.
- **Intersection:** the change in pay-disparity magnitude shifts the relevant mechanism, producing the U-shaped resolution.

### Five acts

- **Exposition:** Opposing theoretical traditions and empirical findings make the pay-disparity–performance relationship unsettled.
- **Rising action:** The paper recovers the similarity and prize-size assumptions, redefines each theory's relevant range, derives the U-shape, and identifies moderators that should verify the proposed mechanism switch.
- **Climax:** Results show the predicted U-shaped association: performance falls as disparity first rises, then improves at meaningfully high disparity.
- **Falling action:** Heir apparent and CEO power suppress the high-disparity arm, while TMT eligibility steepens both arms; these analyses test the tournament and comparison explanations rather than merely decorate the curve.
- **Denouement:** The Discussion resolves the original dispute by locating both theories within different ranges of pay disparity and states bounded compensation implications.

### Tension

- **Source:** Two established literatures offer opposite predictions and reported findings about the same pay-disparity–performance relation.
- **Construction:** The Introduction presents both traditions, rejects the null and generic-contingency exits, and makes the neglected assumptions of each theory the route to resolution.

### Alternative readings

- **analyst_counterfactual:** A generic “pay disparity has a U-shaped effect” paper would reproduce the empirical shape without explaining why the left and right arms belong to different mechanisms. This is an analyst counterfactual, not an author-stated alternative.

## Learning Affordances

### Introduction and Theory

Ridge is useful when a paper needs to ask whether apparently rival theories are globally misapplied, not when it simply needs a nonlinear prediction. The transferable action is recovering what makes each theory operative and then determining whether changes in the focal X alter that applicability.

### Methods and Results

The paper's strongest evidence move is to select moderators that interrogate the claimed mechanism switch. The curve alone would not establish its story.

### Discussion

The ending preserves both theories' validity but bounds each theory's domain. It should be read with Zhou and Wowak before being used as a learning reference.

## Comparison prompt

If a paper reaches a nonlinear result, does it arise because two mechanisms operate on facets of one process (Zhou), because different outcomes require different judgments (Wowak), or because changes in X alter which theory is operative (Ridge)?
