## Story Reading

### Theme question

How can lobbying raise firm value through regulatory advantage while simultaneously lowering customer satisfaction by redirecting the firm's limited attention away from customers, and when can that customer cost be contained?

### Whole-story synopsis

The paper opens with a one-sided success story: lobbying is widespread and commonly associated with striking firm returns, yet prior research has asked almost entirely what firms gain from regulators rather than what customers lose. Regulatory capture supplies the direct-benefit branch—lobbying can secure favorable rules, access, protection, or influence that financial markets value. The attention-based view then gives the omission a mechanism. Because regulators and customers are distinct stakeholder environments that demand different resources, routines, and priorities, lobbying can redirect limited firm attention away from customer focus. Customer satisfaction becomes the resolution-bearing consequence of that diversion, and its decline creates a negative indirect path back to firm value. The paper then prevents the theory from becoming an anti-lobbying morality tale. A marketing-background CEO and product-market lobbying can align regulatory and customer priorities; advertising and R&D can counterbalance the attention drawn to regulators by maintaining customer-facing priorities. Empirical analyses show the positive direct lobbying–firm-value association alongside the negative customer-satisfaction path, test the proposed attenuation conditions, and use shareholder communications as a trace of reduced customer focus. The Discussion returns to the opening accounting problem: lobbying's return is incomplete if it ignores the customer value lost while the firm pursues regulatory advantage.

### Characters and storylines

- **Benefit character:** firm lobbying, because it produces the regulatory advantages whose direct value has dominated prior research.
- **Middle character:** customer focus, the limited attentional priority that can be displaced when the firm concentrates on regulators and political influence.
- **Resolution-bearing character:** customer satisfaction, which converts an internal attentional reallocation into a customer consequence and indirect firm-value cost.
- **Direct-outcome character:** firm value, which receives both the established positive lobbying path and the negative counteracting satisfaction path.
- **Boundary characters:** CEO marketing background and product-market lobbying align regulatory activity with customer priorities; advertising and R&D counterbalance the reallocation by keeping customer priorities salient.
- **Storyline 1:** lobbying → regulatory capture/direct advantage → higher firm value.
- **Storyline 2:** lobbying → less customer focus → lower customer satisfaction → counteracting loss in firm value.
- **Intersection:** the paper turns a known political-strategy benefit into a mixed value calculation, not by denying the benefit but by identifying its customer-facing opportunity cost.

### Five acts

- **Exposition:** Lobbying appears highly profitable and has been studied primarily as a firm-value-enhancing political strategy.
- **Rising action:** Regulatory capture establishes the direct return, while attention-based theory explains why success with regulators can displace attention to customers; alignment and counterbalance make the loss conditional.
- **Climax:** Lobbying is positively associated with firm value but negatively associated with customer satisfaction, creating the promised competing paths.
- **Falling action:** Customer satisfaction mediates a counteracting cost; CEO background, customer-facing investment, and product-market lobbying attenuate the satisfaction loss, while text analysis provides a customer-focus trace.
- **Denouement:** The Discussion reframes lobbying evaluation as a balance between regulatory returns and the customer value that may be quietly depleted in obtaining them.

### Tension

- **Source:** Lobbying can create firm-specific advantages without having to satisfy customers, yet customer satisfaction remains a source of firm value that the firm cannot ignore without cost.
- **Construction:** The paper sustains both sides by using distinct theories for regulatory benefit and attentional diversion, then letting customer satisfaction connect the latter to the same firm-value endpoint.

### Alternative readings

- **author-signaled-alternative:** Product-market lobbying is not treated as simply less lobbying; it is proposed to orient regulatory activity toward customers and therefore weaken the customer-focus loss.
- **analyst_counterfactual:** Firms with falling customer satisfaction could lobby more in response to weakening product-market prospects. The identification design addresses this concern, while the actual managerial redistribution of attention is still inferred from outcomes and communication traces.

## Learning Affordances

### Introduction and Theory

Use this card when a strategy has a documented direct benefit but plausibly imposes a cost on a stakeholder whose outcome feeds back into the same higher-order value question. The reusable move is to build two nonredundant paths and name the mechanism that makes them compete. It is not a license to attach a generic customer downside to any profitable corporate action.

### Methods, Results, and Discussion

The paper shows how a customer outcome can be essential rather than decorative when it changes the interpretation of the established firm-value outcome. Its empirical calibration is `partly_probed`: it measures a communication trace of customer focus and the mediated path, not managers' complete attention allocation process.

## Comparison prompt

Is customer satisfaction falling because a firm redirects attention from customers toward a different stakeholder arena (this paper), or because financial commitments restrict the resources available for customer-facing investment? Does the direct firm-value benefit arise from the same mechanism as the customer cost?
