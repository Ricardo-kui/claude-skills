## Story Reading

### Theme question

When top managers adopt governance reforms they privately oppose, does the mere appearance of reform — a decoupled adoption plus socially legitimate agency language — have real, measurable consequences, and who exactly is the audience that rewards it?

### Whole-story synopsis

The paper opens amid shareholder unrest: agency theory names incentives and boards as the mechanisms of managerial accountability, yet extensive evidence shows both are underused, and institutional investors demand reform. Financial economics even reports positive stock-market reactions when firms adopt reforms such as long-term incentive plans (LTIPs). But the behavioral literature insists CEOs privately resist such reforms — they add compensation risk and cost autonomy. The latent puzzle: why would managers adopt reforms they oppose, and what does the market's approving reaction actually measure? The authors' own prior work supplied the antecedents — firms adopt-but-do-not-implement (decoupling) and wrap adoptions in socially legitimate "agency explanations" — but stopped before the audience and the consequences. This study completes that arc by recasting the agency problem itself: not substantive cost reduction but the reduction of social uncertainty about managerial motives, under which market reactions are "soft numbers" — the aggregated perceptions of a heterogeneous, boundedly rational audience — rather than "hard numbers" reflecting real savings. Two forms of symbolic action (decoupled adoption; agency explanation) are crossed with two consequences (external market reward; internal preemption of board-control reforms), with institutional investors as the discriminating audience whose voice-based pressure should also be dampened. Evidence from over 400 firms, 1982–1992, delivers the verdict: the market rewards unimplemented plans and rewards agency language most; symbolic adoption also forestalls CEO/chair separation and outsider-ratio increases, especially where institutional ownership is high. The ending reinterprets the neighboring puzzle of institutional investors' weak substantive influence and closes with a social theory of agency: the agency problem can be resolved in social beliefs while persisting in substantive policy.

### Characters and storylines

- **Main character — symbolic LTIP adoption (decoupling):** the protagonist action; its "whether or not the plan is implemented" clause is the engine of every hypothesis and of the story's adjudication.
- **Main character — the agency explanation:** the language protagonist; converts a structural event into a symbolic message, and turns a binary adoption story into a 2×2.
- **Judge character — the stock market as heterogeneous audience:** passive-to-active investors with uneven information, reacting almost instantly under imperfect communication; its verdict is the external consequence, and the "soft numbers" reframe is what makes it susceptible to symbolism.
- **Supporting character — institutional investors:** large-stake owners with constrained exit and strong voice; both the source of reform pressure and the moderator that tests whether symbolic substitution works even on the most demanding audience.
- **Supporting characters — the board-control reforms (CEO/chair separation; outsider-ratio increases):** the internal stakes; the substantive reforms that symbolic action preempts.
- **Offstage motive character — the self-interested CEO:** risk-averse and autonomy-seeking; never a measured construct, but the motive that makes symbolic substitution "an important and subtle entrenchment device."
- **Counter-reader — the conventional finance interpretation:** treats market reactions as hard evidence of agency-cost reduction; the decoupling split exists to defeat this reading from within its own evidence.
- **Storyline 1 (external reward):** adoption (+ explanation) → positive excess returns regardless of implementation; the appearance of agency-cost reduction is monetized.
- **Storyline 2 (internal preemption):** adoption (+ explanation) → lower likelihood of subsequent board-control changes; "symbolic substitution" coined at the payoff.
- **Storyline 3 (audience discrimination):** institutional ownership interacts with symbolic action; the shareholders with the most at stake are also placated.
- **Intersection:** all three storylines hinge on one event carrying two incompatible readings — economic signal vs. symbolic substitute — which the decoupling split adjudicates empirically.

### Five acts

- **Exposition:** A governance-confidence crisis; mechanisms deemed inadequate; reforms gain legitimacy; financial economics certifies that markets reward adoption; behavioral research insists CEOs resist.
- **Rising action:** The symbolic management perspective explains resistance-without-defiance; the self-named gap (antecedents only) becomes a two-part question; the agency problem is reframed as social uncertainty; the 2×2 promise (two forms × two consequences) plus the institutional-investor moderator yields H1–H4a.
- **Climax:** Table 2: symbolic adopters alone earn significant positive excess returns (H1), and the agency explanation dramatically amplifies the reaction — t = 8.69–11.98, most striking for plans never implemented; the market's "hard numbers" respond to pure symbolism.
- **Falling action:** Tables 3–4: symbolic adoption reduces the likelihood of separation and outsider-ratio increases (H3); agency explanations amplify and the no-explanation cells are null — confirming the language mechanism (H4); institutional ownership's reform pressure is dampened by symbolic action (H3a/H4a); "symbolic substitution effect" is named.
- **Denouement:** The Discussion reinterprets institutional investors' weak substantive influence as a consequence of symbolic preemption, defends the interpretation against a role-symbolism alternative, and converts the opening question into a research program: a social theory of agency in which the agency problem is resolved in beliefs yet perpetuated in policy.

### Tension

- **Source:** One event — the announced adoption of an LTIP — carries two incompatible readings: an economic signal that agency costs will fall, or a symbolic substitute that merely reduces social uncertainty. The market's approving reaction is conventionally read as the former; the behavioral reality of managerial resistance makes the latter live.
- **Construction:** The paper never lets the reader resolve the ambiguity by fiat. It builds the empirical fork into the phenomenon itself (implemented vs. unimplemented; agency language vs. none), so the data — a positive reward for plans never implemented — must choose between the readings. The rival reading is quoted generously (Kumar and Sopariwala; the Business Week "Report Card") before being turned.

### Alternative readings

- **author_signaled_alternative:** Combined CEO/chair roles could themselves carry symbolic value (unified-control symbolism); the authors answer that separation is the reform most demanded by institutional investors and that the combined role would symbolize something different (e.g., the romance of leadership) that does not contradict their framework.
- **analyst_counterfactual:** Agency explanations might proxy for unobserved plan or firm quality rather than causing favorable reactions; the authors partially pre-empt this by citing Zajac and Westphal (1995) that explanation type did not reflect actual plan differences, and by footnote evidence that non-agency explanations yielded no significant excess returns — but the perceptual mechanism itself remains unobserved.

## Story Assessment

- **Theme coherence:** `works` — one question (does symbolic action have real consequences, and for whom) organizes the hook, the reframed agency problem, both dependent variables, and the moderator; nothing in the paper is off-theme.
- **Character discipline:** `works` — symbolic adoption and agency explanation have structurally distinct roles (form vs. language), institutional investors are a genuine discriminating character rather than a generic moderator, and the CEO motive stays offstage without hijacking the story.
- **Tie–unravel alignment:** `works` — every hypothesis H1–H4a is adjudicated with adjudication criteria pre-announced in Methods (the four-cell test), and the promised test — rewarding appearance without substance — is literally delivered in the symbolic-adopter columns; the unmeasured perceptual mechanism is the one looseness (hence `partly_probed` calibration), but the story-level promise is kept.
- **Ending quality:** `works` — the ending does not repeat findings: it reinterprets an external literature's puzzle, absorbs the strongest alternative reading, and upgrades the opening question into a named research program (social theory of agency).
- **Boundary:** This evaluates storytelling only — not causal identification, research quality, or journal value. The PDM era flags (no AME beat, escalated causal language, Analysis/Results separation) are craft-boundary notes carried in section caveats, not story failures.

## Learning Affordances

### Introduction

- **Suitable:** `yes`
- **Learn:** (1) The self-inheriting gap — name your own prior study's scope boundary as the gap and convert it into a two-part question (real impact? which audience?) so gap and design coincide. (2) The contribution paragraph as hypothesis matrix — two forms × two consequences previewed cell by cell, each cell later cashed as a numbered hypothesis.
- **Do not copy:** The move requires already owning the antecedent study; the 1998 stakes-free preview style and the two-part question's phrasing belong to this paper's institutional moment, not to a modern template.

### Theory

- **Suitable:** `yes`
- **Learn:** (1) The alternating reframe — take the rival tradition's headline evidence and re-read it under a different ontology ("hard numbers" → "soft numbers") so identical facts motivate your hypotheses. (2) Decoupling as built-in discriminating test — write "whether or not implemented" into each hypothesis so the rival reading is refuted by construction, with null cells reserved as confirmation.
- **Do not copy:** Requires a setting where appearance and substance are separately codable; the uncertainty-reduction mechanism is asserted, and a modern version must add perceptual or heterogeneity evidence rather than rest on the decoupling split alone.

### Methods

- **Suitable:** `partial`
- **Learn:** Design-to-story alignment — coding (adoption/implementation), content-analyzed language, and four-category dummies exist to make the narrative's adjudication estimable; anticipated rival-reading contaminations (event-date choice, non-agency explanations) are defused inside the design chapter.
- **Do not copy:** The era's presentation (Analysis/Results split, no magnitude reporting, no exhaustive coding rulebook) reflects 1998 norms; borrow the alignment logic, not the presentation.

### Results

- **Suitable:** `yes`
- **Learn:** (1) Stage the climax on the appearance-only cell — report the expected baseline first, then let the symbolic-adopter significance land as the headline the rival reading treats as impossible. (2) Double-confirm moderation — subgroup t-tests plus Wald tests on coefficient differences, with null cells narrated as part of confirmation.
- **Do not copy:** No AME or economic-significance beat and mildly escalated causal verbs ("leads to", "elicits") are era artifacts; a modern staging needs a magnitude interpretation and disciplined causal language.

### Discussion

- **Suitable:** `yes`
- **Learn:** (1) Outward reinterpretation — use your mechanism to explain a neighboring literature's surprising finding (weak institutional-investor influence) so the discussion itself becomes new evidence. (2) Transform the opening question into a named research program rather than restating results.
- **Do not copy:** The single-paragraph deflection of the role-symbolism alternative relies on author judgment about what a governance form "would symbolize"; modern versions need stronger grounds for ruling the alternative out.
