## Story Reading

### Theme question

When a major recall lowers a rival's perceived quality, do competitors increase promotions to capture demand, and why do those promotions benefit premium brands more reliably than nonpremium brands?

### Whole-story synopsis

The paper opens with a major recall as a public negative quality shock whose consequences do not end with the recalled firm. It asks a more active question than the recall-spillover literature: how do competitors use a familiar marketing instrument when their rival's failure creates both an opening and a reputational hazard? Promotion can make substitutes more affordable and draw reconsidering customers, but it can also look opportunistic or become a low-quality cue precisely when safety and vertical quality are salient. Brand tier gives this uncertainty a theoretical resolution. Premium brands should have more to gain from making their superior vertical quality affordable and less to lose from the quality signal of a discount; nonpremium brands have weaker protection on both fronts. The Toyota 2009–2010 crisis provides an external shock. A matched difference-in-differences design first shows that competitors in recalled Toyota categories increased promotions, especially premium-brand competitors. A second, instrumented sales analysis then reveals the initially surprising average null: promotional reactions do not improve sales on average. Brand-tier estimates supply the payoff: most premium brands benefit, whereas nonpremium effects are generally null or negative. The Discussion returns to the opening ambiguity: a rival recall is neither a universal chance to discount nor merely a category-wide threat; it changes the meaning of promotion conditional on perceived vertical quality.

### Characters and storylines

- **Shock character:** Toyota's major recall, which makes safety and vertical quality salient in a relevant automobile category.
- **Action character:** a competitor's effective promotion, distinguished from routine programmatic promotion and observed before its sales consequence.
- **Interpretive characters:** demand-capture opportunity, opportunism concern, and low-quality signaling risk.
- **Boundary character:** premium versus nonpremium brand tier, which represents different perceived vertical-quality positions.
- **Payoff character:** post-recall sales, which determines whether a promotional reaction earns its strategic role.
- **Storyline 1:** competitors can use promotion to make an alternative attractive when recall weakens loyalty to the defective rival.
- **Storyline 2:** heightened safety concern can make discounting seem opportunistic or signal low quality, especially for nonpremium brands.
- **Intersection:** vertical quality is meant to resolve both whether a brand promotes and whether that promotion converts into sales.

### Five acts

- **Exposition:** Major recalls damage the focal firm, yet existing recall and crisis research says little about competitors' actual promotional responses.
- **Rising action:** Promotion becomes a mixed competitive move—demand capture versus opportunism and quality-signal risk—and vertical quality predicts which side should dominate.
- **Climax:** Toyota recalls produce an average increase in competitors' promotions, with stronger and more frequent increases among premium-brand competitors.
- **Falling action:** The average promotion-to-sales effect is null; manufacturer-tier results reveal predominantly positive effects for premium brands and null or negative effects for nonpremium brands.
- **Denouement:** The Discussion reframes conventional promotion effectiveness as contingent on the quality-salient recall context and the rival's brand tier.

### Tension

- **Source:** A competitor's safety failure creates demand to be won, but a visible discount can itself be read as opportunistic or as evidence of inferior quality.
- **Construction:** The paper makes this a decision-and-payoff problem: it separates promotional response from promotion effectiveness, then uses brand tier to explain why the two are not uniform.

### Alternative readings

- **author-signaled-alternative:** The authors note that actual firm intentions cannot be identified from the observational design; a promotion may be a strategic reaction without proving that managers aimed to exploit a rival's recall.
- **analyst_counterfactual:** The tier asymmetry may also reflect differences in dealer networks, product portfolios, or unobserved demand elasticity. The paper's matching, controls, and instrumental strategy support the staged outcome patterns but do not observe the proposed consumer interpretations.

## Learning Affordances

### Introduction and Theory

Use this card when an event at a rival creates a real choice for the focal firm and the same action has a credible benefit and reputational cost. It is not a license to label every response to a competitor as a mixed opportunity-threat story.

### Methods and Results

The key learning is the evidence sequence: establish that the shock changes action, then ask whether the action produces its promised payoff. Do not use response intensity as a proxy for managers' motives or for consumer perception.

### Discussion

The ending shows how a contingency can change the meaning of an ordinary marketing tool. Its policy extrapolation should remain more modest than its theoretical conclusion.
