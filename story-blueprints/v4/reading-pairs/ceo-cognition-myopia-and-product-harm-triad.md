# CEO Cognition, Marketing Myopia, and Product-Harm Risk — Three-Card Contrast

## Scope

This second-reading record compares `chunglow2022`, `chunglowrust2023`, and `kashmiri2017`. All three make a CEO psychological characteristic relevant to marketing or product-harm outcomes. They do not support a single category called "CEO traits cause short-termism."

| Card | Focal decision or process | Middle theoretical mechanism | Consequence structure | Organizational boundary |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Chung and Low (2022) | A marketing/R&D budget cut under earnings pressure | Regulatory focus reweights immediate stock-price gains versus larger long-term value losses, and action versus status-quo concerns | One intertemporal marketing-myopia process with later performance cost | Compensation, analyst scrutiny, and turbulence alter named gains or losses |
| Chung, Low, and Rust (2023) | The same budget-cut decision | CEO confidence makes present cuts appear repayable by expected future performance | One intertemporal process, made into a CEO–CMO–board contest | CMO confidence supplies counter-persuasion; board independence can intensify performance pressure |
| Kashmiri, Nicol, and Arora (2017) | Firm-level innovation posture | Narcissism induces competitive aggressiveness | Separate innovation benefits and product-harm-risk costs from one shared posture | Marketing power increases customer orientation and selectively weakens the safety branch |

## Central comparative finding

The first two cards concern **one decision whose temporal valuation changes**. Regulatory focus changes which gains and losses become salient; confidence changes whether a future loss appears recoverable. Their outcomes should therefore remain within one marketing-myopia sequence.

The third card concerns **a posture producing two distinct processes**. Competitive aggressiveness can accelerate innovation and increase exposure to product harm. Its benefit and cost are not merely two measurements of the same budget decision, and marketing power is a selective safety boundary rather than a general moderator of CEO influence.
