# Liu and Shankar 2015 × Chen, Ganesan, and Liu 2009 — Crisis Damage Process versus Response-as-Signal

## Scope

This record compares `liu2015` and `chen2009`. Both are product-harm crisis studies, but they locate the story's focal causal object at different points in the event sequence.

| Card | Focal object | Audience and observed outcome | Middle mechanism | Evidence boundary |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Liu and Shankar (2015) | The recall event and subsequent advertising allocation | Consumers; monthly nameplate demand and inferred brand preference | Negative recall information directly damages preference and indirectly reduces advertising effectiveness, with persistence and same-parent spillovers | The model estimates dynamic paths but does not observe trust updating, advertising content, or all event-newsworthiness confounding |
| Chen, Ganesan, and Liu (2009) | The firm's proactive versus passive recall strategy | Investors; announcement-window abnormal returns | Investors may read a visible proactive response as a signal of hidden crisis severity | The event study observes returns, not investor beliefs, true severity, or which bundled response component carries the signal |

## Central comparative finding

Liu and Shankar ask what the product-harm event does to the customer-side demand state and to the efficacy of repair-oriented advertising. Chen et al. ask what an observable response does to investor valuation under information asymmetry. One paper's recall is the damage-producing shock; the other's response to a recall is the potentially damaging signal.

The papers should not be combined into a generic claim that recalls harm all audiences through one mechanism. Their audiences, temporal positions, focal objects, and evidence differ.

## Learning boundary for future retrieval

Retrieve `liu2015` softly for a genuine dynamic direct-and-indirect consequence process with a specified intervention whose effectiveness changes. Retrieve `chen2009` only when an external audience has reason to interpret a visible organizational response as a cue to a hidden state. A customer-demand study does not establish investor severity inference, and an event study of market returns does not establish dynamic consumer preference damage.

## Next comparison question

Is the paper tracking the consequences of the adverse event itself, evaluating a post-event response choice, or explaining an audience's interpretation of that response? What observed outcome actually corresponds to that part of the sequence?
