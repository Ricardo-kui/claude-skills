# CEO Cognition Under Pressure — Contextual Manifestation versus Intertemporal Valuation

## Scope

This comparison reads `ridge2024-ceo-paranoia-stakeholder-engagement` alongside `chunglow2022`. Both papers make a CEO psychological characteristic consequential for strategy, but they do not describe one generic category of “CEO cognition changes behavior.”

| Card | Focal strategic problem | Middle theoretical mechanism | What makes behavior change | Empirical boundary |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Ridge et al. (2024) | Whether and how to engage powerful stakeholders | Paranoia produces safety seeking; a cue that avoidance failed redirects its manifestation from avoidance to aggression | A regulatory ruling or rival attack is interpreted as targeting and failed protection | Government sequence is supported; competitor baseline is not, and its cue interaction is practically tiny |
| Chung and Low (2022) | Whether to cut marketing/R&D under earnings pressure | Regulatory focus changes the salience of immediate gains and future losses, plus action versus status-quo concerns | The decision's pre-existing time pressure and its gain/loss structure | The predicted behavioral pattern and later performance payoff are observed; cognitive valuation itself is not |

## Central comparative finding

Ridge et al. need an **activation-and-redirection** story: one safety motive first generates avoidance, then a cue makes that very response untenable and produces aggression. Chung and Low need a **valuation** story: the same budget decision carries visible near-term and long-term consequences, and regulatory focus changes which of those consequences dominates.

The distinction is consequential for learning. A project should not borrow Ridge's reversal structure merely because a leader trait has a moderator. It must establish that the moderator is a cue that changes the trait's manifestation, not a variable that changes incentives, payoff salience, or the outcome's feasibility. Conversely, a paper with an intertemporal trade-off should not call the decision a trait-activation story unless a cue plausibly redirects a trait's usual behavioral response.

## Comparison question

Before drafting the theory middle, ask: does the proposed condition reveal that the actor's ordinary safety response has failed, or does it change the gain/loss calculus of a choice that the actor already faces? What behavioral baseline must be visible in the results for that answer to be credible?
