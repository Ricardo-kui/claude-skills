# Recall as Rival Shock or Safety Trace — Zhou 2019 × Jeon 2026

## Scope

These cards share the automobile setting and product-safety language, but they place recall-related information on opposite sides of a causal story. This is a corpus distinction, not a claim that one paper's theory explains the other.

| Card | What the adverse event represents | Focal decision or process | Story payoff | Mechanism evidence check |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Zhou 2019 | A rival's public quality shock | Competitors' promotions and their sales effect | Premium versus nonpremium brands face different competitive returns to the same action | `not_directly_tested` — consumer quality and opportunism interpretations are theoretical. |
| Jeon 2026 | A trace of upstream safety failure, corroborated by recall counts | Regulatory pressure, MPG-related technology bundles, and defects | A sustainability-oriented design goal carries a bounded safety cost | `partly_probed` — engine technology, MPG, and defects are linked, though integration and coordination are not observed. |

## Central comparative finding

Do not treat both as studies of "the effect of recalls." Zhou starts after a rival's failure has become public and asks how a competitor acts in the market. Jeon treats defects and recalls as outcomes that reveal an earlier product-design and quality-production process. The appropriate narrative clock is therefore different:

```text
upstream policy and technology choices → defects / recalls → Jeon 2026
public rival recall → competitor promotion → sales response → Zhou 2019
```

## Retrieval boundary

- Retrieve `zhou2019` only for a real rival-response question in which an external event changes both the attraction and reputational meaning of a focal action.
- Retrieve `jeon2026` only when the outcome is a credible trace of an upstream production or design process and theory names a concrete transformation that can produce it.

## Next comparison question

Before borrowing a recall exemplar, is the study explaining what produced the safety failure, what a firm does after recognizing it, or how a different firm responds once it becomes public?
