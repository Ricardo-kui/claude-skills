# activism_risk_and_corporate_self_regulation_investigating_ho 句子库存

## introduction
<!-- para 2 -->
Stakeholder activism imposes external governance pressures on firms to consider stakeholder interests when developing policy (Aguilera et al., [2015](#smj70031-bib-0002); Dorobantu & Odziemkowska, [2017](#smj70031-bib-0024)).[1](#smj70031-note-0001_note_0 "Link to note") Examples of such activism include protests, boycotts, and other social-movement tactics that raise public awareness of both the issues and the targeted firms (Briscoe & Gupta, [2016](#smj70031-bib-0012)).
Scholarship on stakeholder activism indicates that firms perceive stakeholder activism as a nonmarket risk (Oh et al., [2020](#smj70031-bib-0064)) that can prevent firms from obtaining critical resources (Hiatt & Park, [2013](#smj70031-bib-0038)), provoke elite employee turnover (McDonnell & Cobb, [2020](#smj70031-bib-0053)), disrupt corporate political activities (McDonnell & Werner, [2016](#smj70031-bib-0057)), and ultimately damage firm performance (Vasi & King, [2012](#smj70031-bib-0081)).
Accordingly, a substantial body of research has examined how firms respond once they become targets of activism, with particular attention to the types of responses firms adopt and how effective those responses are in mitigating reputational and material harm (Wu & Liu, [2024](#smj70031-bib-0085)).
<!-- para 3 -->
Parker et al. ([2019](#smj70031-bib-0067): 254), however, note that “the everyday reality for most firms consists not of responding to such crises but, rather, *avoiding* and preventing them in the first place” (emphasis original).
Firms and their managers are strategic actors that can and should anticipate threats and pursue strategic moves to avoid problems (Chen & Hambrick, [1995](#smj70031-bib-0015)).
Yet, despite some noteworthy exceptions (e.g., Odziemkowska & Dorobantu, [2021](#smj70031-bib-0062); Yue et al., [2013](#smj70031-bib-0087)), how firms respond in anticipation of, rather than in reaction to, stakeholder activism remains poorly understood.
<!-- para 4 -->
Drawing on the literature on corporate preemptive self-regulation (Baron, [2001](#smj70031-bib-0007); King & Lenox, [2000](#smj70031-bib-0046); Maxwell et al., [2000](#smj70031-bib-0052)), we argue that firms will respond to an increased risk of stakeholder activism by increasing their institutional corporate social performance (CSP), defined as the tangible outcomes of firms' activities and initiatives in “promoting social welfare outside firms' formal boundaries” (Acharya et al., [2022](#smj70031-bib-0001): 312).
Specifically, we propose that when the *environmental* risk of stakeholder activism increases, institutional CSP will rise as an attempt to satisfy stakeholder activists' interests proactively; we further predict that this effect becomes stronger as the *firm-level* risk of activism increases.
<!-- para 5 -->
Investigating the impact of stakeholder activism risk on firms' institutional CSP faces two non-trivial empirical challenges.
First, the counterfactual likelihood of stakeholder activism that could have materialized had it not been preempted is often difficult to determine.
Second, the risk of would-be activism and corporate self-regulatory engagement in institutional CSP is likely endogenously formed and can be driven by the unobservable characteristics of the firm.
This concern is particularly acute as organizational values are likely to drive not only the likelihood of being targeted by activists (McDonnell & Cobb, [2020](#smj70031-bib-0053)) but also the propensity toward preemptive self-regulation.
We address these challenges by exploiting a natural experiment from the staggered enactment of laws prohibiting retaliatory lawsuits against critics across US states between 1991 and 2018.
<!-- para 6 -->
A Strategic Lawsuit Against Public Participation (SLAPP) is a retaliatory lawsuit filed against critics who have spoken against the plaintiff in a public forum.
Large firms often use such lawsuits to deter stakeholder-led protests and boycotts (Pring & Canan, [1996](#smj70031-bib-0069)).
In response, US states began enacting anti-SLAPP laws to prevent the use of litigation threats to silence critics.
Activists view these laws as protection from costly legal proceedings, making them a key enabler of activism.
The staggered enactment of anti-SLAPP laws thus creates an opportunity structure favorable for activism (Briscoe & Gupta, [2016](#smj70031-bib-0012)).
By exploiting this variation, we capture changes in activism risk that might otherwise remain unobservable.
Second, the staggered rollout renders activism risk plausibly exogenous to firm behavior, addressing endogeneity concerns.
Using a difference-in-differences (DiD) design, we find that the enactment of anti-SLAPP laws leads to a significant increase in institutional CSP for firms headquartered in adopting states relative to those in non-adopting states.
Further, this law-induced effect is stronger when the firm-specific risk of activism is higher, as reflected in greater media coverage of corporate social irresponsibility (CSI).
<!-- para 7 -->
Our study makes three contributions to the literature.
First, the findings in this study contribute to the literature on corporate responses to social activism (Wu & Liu, [2024](#smj70031-bib-0085)) by shifting the focus from the prevalent backward-looking perspective, wherein firms react to ongoing activism, to a more forward-looking view in which firms proactively seek to prevent activism.
Unlike most research that limits itself to reactive strategies, our study, in line with pioneering work by Yue et al. ([2013](#smj70031-bib-0087)) and Odziemkowska and Dorobantu ([2021](#smj70031-bib-0062)), explores how firms anticipate and proactively address long-term activism risks.
While Yue et al. ([2013](#smj70031-bib-0087)) and Odziemkowska and Dorobantu ([2021](#smj70031-bib-0062)) focus on particular industries where location matters to the firm's potential activism risk, emphasizing location choices (Yue et al., [2013](#smj70031-bib-0087)) and community contracts (Odziemkowska & Dorobantu, [2021](#smj70031-bib-0062)) as risk mitigation strategies, our research extends this analysis to a wider industrial spectrum, exploring how firms attempt to use institutional CSP as a self-regulatory tool to try to preempt the risk of future activism.
This expansion of focus enriches the underexplored area of corporate preemptive strategies against stakeholder activism, shedding light on firms' strategic actions before the onset of activism (Wu & Liu, [2024](#smj70031-bib-0085)).
<!-- para 8 -->
Second, our research enriches scholarly understanding of activism opportunity structures by examining their multi-level interactions, a dimension underexplored in prior work.
Extant research has largely examined how firm- or environment-level conditions independently render firms more or less attractive targets for activists (e.g., Briscoe et al., [2014](#smj70031-bib-0011); Walker et al., [2008](#smj70031-bib-0083)).
However, little attention has been paid to how different levels of activism opportunity structures interact to shape firm adaptation, beyond the isolated effects of any single opportunity structure.
We address this gap by demonstrating how environment-level opportunity structures (e.g., the enactment of anti-SLAPP laws) intersect with firm-level opportunity structures (e.g., media coverage of CSI) to jointly influence firm adaptation.
In doing so, our work underscores the *interdependence* of multi-level opportunity structures, offering a more nuanced understanding of how these structural interactions create layered vulnerabilities for firms facing activism in complex institutional environments.
<!-- para 9 -->
Third, our research contributes to the literature on how institutions affect firms' CSP (Ioannou & Serafeim, [2012](#smj70031-bib-0042)).
To date, the literature has focused on how cross-national variation in institutions explains differences in firms' CSP across countries (Odziemkowska & Henisz, [2021](#smj70031-bib-0063)).
We add richness to this line of research by investigating how subnational institutions (e.g., state-level variation in laws) can affect firms' CSP.
The finding that enactment of anti-SLAPP laws can nudge firms' CSP improvement speaks to the importance of institutional contexts in understanding firms' CSR/CSI decisions (Mohliver, [2019](#smj70031-bib-0060)), heeding Ioannou and Serafeim's ([2022](#smj70031-bib-0043): 15) call for more “discussion of how institutions themselves could be reformed … to further promote and ultimately achieve social goals.”

## theory
<!-- para 2 -->
Research on corporate responses to stakeholder activism has historically focused on post hoc reactions to activism that are causing damage to the firm (e.g., King, [2008](#smj70031-bib-0047); Luo et al., [2016](#smj70031-bib-0051); McDonnell et al., [2015](#smj70031-bib-0055)).
Less attention has been paid to whether and how firms engage in preemptive efforts to mitigate activism risk before it materializes into a threat (Wu & Liu, [2024](#smj70031-bib-0085)).
The few studies examining preemptive mechanisms have typically focused on firms' location choices in response to activism risk.
For example, Yue et al. ([2013](#smj70031-bib-0087)) investigated how mass-market retailer Target observed the protests against Walmart and avoided entering markets where activism risk was high.
As another example, Odziemkowska and Dorobantu ([2021](#smj70031-bib-0062)) found that firms anticipate future activism and seek to create contracts that govern the relationships between firms and nonmarket stakeholders in an effort to mitigate activism risk.
These two pioneering studies demonstrate two preemptive strategies to activism risk: location avoidance (Yue et al., [2013](#smj70031-bib-0087)) and strategic contracting (Odziemkowska & Dorobantu, [2021](#smj70031-bib-0062)).
We extend the understanding of preemptive mechanisms further by proposing corporate self-regulatory improvement in institutional CSP as another strategy for firms to mitigate activism risk before the risk materializes into an active threat.
Given the unobserved nature of activism risk that would have materialized, we situate our theory development in the context of staggered enactment of anti-SLAPP laws across US states.
<!-- para 4 -->
A SLAPP is a civil claim filed by businesses, government entities, or elected officials against an individual or an organization that opposes the individual or the organization on issues of public concern (Pring & Canan, [1996](#smj70031-bib-0069)).
SLAPPs, often civil suits for defamation, have become a tool firms use to silence critics by burdening them with the high cost of legal proceedings (Donson, [2000](#smj70031-bib-0023)).
While most SLAPPs are legally meritless, firms often aim not to win such lawsuits, but simply to use them to dissuade present and future critics.
<!-- para 5 -->
SLAPPs initially gained prominence in the late 1980s and early 1990s, especially in real estate (Stetson, [1995](#smj70031-bib-0079)).
One landmark case involved Brenda Hill, a Washington State resident who, in 1984, sought to report Robert John Real Estate Co. for extensive tax fraud.
In retaliation, the company filed a defamation suit against her, seeking $100,000 in damages and an injunction.
In response, the state passed the “Brenda Hill Bill,” granting civil immunity to individuals who raise concerns in good faith (Pring & Canan, [1996](#smj70031-bib-0069)).
This legislation became the first modern anti-SLAPP law in the United States (Wyrich, [2011](#smj70031-bib-0086)).
<!-- para 6 -->
The most active period of anti-SLAPP law enactment occurred in the late 1990s and 2000s (see Table [^1]), coinciding with the rise of the internet as a platform for public criticism (Furman, [2001](#smj70031-bib-0031)).
In response, firms increasingly used SLAPPs to silence critics (Spencer, [2000](#smj70031-bib-0077)).
For instance, a Texas couple posted a one-star review of Prestigious Pets, a pet-sitting company, citing a poor experience.
After the couple refused to remove the review, the company filed a $1 million defamation lawsuit for reputational harm (Sherwin, [2016](#smj70031-bib-0076)).
This case was not unique.
Many firms have used SLAPPs to intimidate critics in cyberspace.

Anti-SLAPP laws aim to protect public participation by deterring lawsuits intended to silence critics.
For example, California's law cites “a disturbing increase in lawsuits brought primarily to chill the valid exercise of the constitutional rights of freedom of speech and petition,” affirming that “continued participation in matters of public significance… should not be chilled through abuse of the judicial process” (Code of Civil Procedure – Section 425.16; California Anti-SLAPP Project, [2023](#smj70031-bib-0014)).
By safeguarding these rights, anti-SLAPP laws encourage activists, particularly those mobilizing social movements against corporate entities, to speak out without fear of legal retaliation.
<!-- para 10 -->
Activist campaigns often rely on public criticism to attract attention and pressure firms, a strategy Eesley et al. ([2016](#smj70031-bib-0025): 2427) describe as “dragging companies publicly through the mud.” To maximize impact, activists selectively target firms whose actions are likely to spark strong public reactions, using public sentiment as a lever for influence (Briscoe & Gupta, [2016](#smj70031-bib-0012)).
Media platforms play a central role in this process, enabling activists to amplify their message, raise awareness, and mobilize broader support (King, [2008](#smj70031-bib-0047)).
<!-- para 11 -->
Before anti-SLAPP laws, firms could often use SLAPPs, typically alleging defamation, to intimidate and silence activists that mobilize social movements against them (Pring & Canan, [1996](#smj70031-bib-0069)).
With the introduction of anti-SLAPP laws, however, there has been a pivotal shift in the institutional context that governs the interactions between activists and firms.
These laws have redefined the legal landscape, significantly curtailing the ability of firms to use litigation as a means of silencing activism (Pring & Canan, [1996](#smj70031-bib-0069)).
As a result, they lower the perceived legal risks and costs for activists when speaking out against firms (Stetson, [1995](#smj70031-bib-0079)).
Recognizing this, Verza ([2018](#smj70031-bib-0082)) comments that “anti-SLAPP regulations are a step forward for guaranteeing…activists and whoever wants to contribute to the public debate not to be intimidated by eventual vexatious lawsuits.”
<!-- para 12 -->
Institutional context influences how disruptive activists will seek to be (Walker et al., [2008](#smj70031-bib-0083)).
In the current institutional landscape shaped by anti-SLAPP laws, activists are emboldened, facing reduced fear of legal retribution (Golden, [2014](#smj70031-bib-0034)).
This increased sense of security likely encourages more vigorous activism, as stakeholders can campaign against firms without the dread of costly legal disputes (Wyrich, [2011](#smj70031-bib-0086)).
Consequently, with these laws providing a safeguard for activists, firms face an increased risk of activism in their stakeholder environment (Wu & Liu, [2024](#smj70031-bib-0085)).
There is evidence to support this claim.
For example, Ashenmiller and Norman ([2011](#smj70031-bib-0006)) found that anti-SLAPP laws in the United States encouraged whistleblowing on environmental violations, resulting in a 50% increase in Environmental Protection Agency inspections.
As another example, Schaufele ([2022](#smj70031-bib-0074)) found that anti-SLAPP laws in Canada empowered local communities to oppose housing developments, resulting in an $80 million monthly decline in construction investment and 120 fewer single-family housing units per month.
<!-- para 13 -->
Anecdotal evidence further illustrates how activists actively invoke anti-SLAPP protections when threatened with retaliatory lawsuits.
For example, when O'Neill Properties Group sought to build 228 homes on a contaminated brownfield site near Philadelphia (StateImpact Pennsylvania, [2017](#smj70031-bib-0078)), the Delaware Riverkeeper Network (DRN) petitioned state regulators to block the project due to public health concerns (Delaware Riverkeeper Network, [2018](#smj70031-bib-0017)).
In retaliation, O'Neill filed a defamation-based SLAPP against DRN.
DRN responded by invoking Pennsylvania's anti-SLAPP statute to seek dismissal of the case, and the court agreed, ruling that DRN's advocacy was constitutionally protected speech and that the organization was immune from tort claims (Delaware Riverkeeper Network, [2018](#smj70031-bib-0017); StateImpact Pennsylvania, [2017](#smj70031-bib-0078)).
This case demonstrates how activists can actively mobilize anti-SLAPP laws to defend their campaigns against firms' legal intimidation.
<!-- para 15 -->
Baron and Diermeier's ([2007](#smj70031-bib-0009)) game-theoretic models suggest firms may proactively adopt self-regulatory practices to mitigate increased activism risk.
Although direct empirical evidence is lacking, observed trends in corporate self-regulation, undertaken to preempt regulatory risks, corroborate their theory.
For instance, Maxwell et al. ([2000](#smj70031-bib-0052)) observed that firms improved their environmental performance preemptively in response to stricter regulations.
Analogously, we argue that firms anticipating higher activism risk may similarly enhance self-regulatory practices to prevent such risk from escalating into active campaigns.
<!-- para 16 -->
As stakeholder activism is generally focused on advocating for firms to consider issues outside their basic economic obligations (Briscoe & Gupta, [2016](#smj70031-bib-0012)), institutional CSP stands out as a likely mechanism for proactively assuaging the interests of stakeholder activists.
Unlike other types of CSP which serve primary stakeholders engaged in direct economic exchange with the firm, institutional CSP signals alignment with broader societal expectations beyond immediate business interests (Acharya et al., [2022](#smj70031-bib-0001)).
Institutional CSP strategically benefits firms by helping them build moral capital, an intangible resource accumulated when firms support societal causes without direct financial benefits (Godfrey, [2005](#smj70031-bib-0032)).
This moral capital fosters stakeholder trust, goodwill, and positive evaluations, which can reinforce firms' normative legitimacy, a perception that firms' actions align with broader societal expectations (Suchman, [1995](#smj70031-bib-0080)).
This legitimacy can help “protect firms against possible \[activism\] threats” (Acharya et al., [2022](#smj70031-bib-0001): 312), because activists often struggle to portray a demonstrably responsible firm as belonging to a morally disfavored category (Den Hond & De Bakker, [2007](#smj70031-bib-0020)).
Consistent with this logic, Acharya et al. ([2022](#smj70031-bib-0001)) provide robust evidence that the likelihood of stakeholder activism declines significantly as a firm's institutional CSP increases.
<!-- para 17 -->
We acknowledge, however, that prior research also presents a competing perspective.
For instance, King and McDonnell ([2015](#smj70031-bib-0048)) suggest that structural signals of CSR commitments, such as establishing dedicated committees, may paradoxically increase a firm's vulnerability to activist targeting.
Their logic is not that such efforts are ineffective, but that activists interpret these structural signals as evidence of managerial receptiveness to stakeholder demands (McDonnell et al., [2015](#smj70031-bib-0055)).
Since activists intentionally target firms where they anticipate greater chances of successful engagement, robust CSR structures, while socially beneficial, may signal organizational openness to activist influences (Briscoe et al., [2014](#smj70031-bib-0011)).
As a result, structural signals of CSR commitments can paradoxically increase a firm's attractiveness as an activist target.
<!-- para 18 -->
Taken together, while prior research presents mixed findings on whether firm CSR engagement attenuates or amplifies activism risk, we interpret these inconsistencies as evidence that not all forms of CSR engagement are perceived equally by activists.
Whereas signaling CSR commitment may indicate managers' openness to accommodate stakeholder activists, institutional CSP represents actual performance along the dimensions of interest to activists.
Structural commitments, such as the creation of sustainability committees, communicate *intent* but do not necessarily reflect *actual* social performance.
In contrast, institutional CSP captures tangible performance outcomes of firm CSR engagement that align with broader societal values.
As such, we speculate that structural commitments signal openness to activist demands, and thus may attract activism; institutional CSP demonstrates performance that may dissuade activism.
<!-- para 19 -->
This distinction is important because CSR-related structural commitments do not always translate into actual CSP improvements.
Well-intentioned firms may adopt formal structures, such as board-level sustainability committees, as a signal of responsibility yet fail in translating these structural mechanisms into performance outcomes.
For instance, Orazalin et al. ([2023](#smj70031-bib-0065)) show that establishing a board-level sustainability committee does not reliably lead to improved environmental performance.
Similarly, Rodrigue et al. ([2012](#smj70031-bib-0073)) find no association between the presence of board environmental committees and actual environmental outcomes.
These findings highlight a potential disconnect between structural CSR commitments and institutional CSP, suggesting that the former may not offer the same reputational protection as the latter in the context of activist targeting, as structural commitments often lack the protective legitimacy that stems from *demonstrable* societal contributions reflected in institutional CSP.
<!-- para 20 -->
Accordingly, activists are more likely to target firms with weak institutional CSP, regardless of their structural CSR commitments, because poor performance offers a clearer basis for mobilizing public support and launching effective campaigns (Lenox & Eesley, [2009](#smj70031-bib-0050)).
As Den Hond and De Bakker ([2007](#smj70031-bib-0020): 910) emphasize, activists must persuade external audiences “that the firm belongs to some morally disfavored taxonomic category,” a judgment that hinges more on actual CSP outcomes than structural CSR commitments.
This logic is further supported by Baron ([2016](#smj70031-bib-0008)), who argues that activists strategically target firms based on the perceived alignment of their CSP with stakeholder interests.
Because activist campaigns depend on generating public support, activists are more likely to focus on “safe bets”—firms whose actual CSP is widely viewed as violating stakeholder expectations—rather than “long shots,” where any perceived misalignment is more ambiguous (Holburn et al., [2024](#smj70031-bib-0039): 7443).
<!-- para 21 -->
Given our focus on how firms attempt to preempt activism risk, we propose that firms seek to enhance their institutional CSP in response to the elevated risk of stakeholder activism, particularly following the enactment of anti-SLAPP laws.
Institutional CSP, which reflects actual improvements in stakeholder-related outcomes beyond the firm's formal boundaries, demonstrates substantial alignment with societal expectations.
This form of CSP builds moral capital and normative legitimacy (Godfrey, [2005](#smj70031-bib-0032)), both of which can help insulate firms from activist targeting by making it more difficult for activists to frame the firm as morally culpable (Den Hond & De Bakker, [2007](#smj70031-bib-0020)).
Empirical evidence supports this view: Acharya et al. ([2022](#smj70031-bib-0001)) find that higher institutional CSP significantly reduces the likelihood of being targeted by activists.
<!-- para 22 -->
Anecdotal evidence further suggests the plausibility of firms intending to self-regulate by improving their institutional CSP following the enactment of anti-SLAPP laws.
For instance, following Texas' anti-SLAPP law enactment in 2011, Dell initiated a “stakeholder mapping process” to evaluate and improve stakeholder relations (Dell, [2012](#smj70031-bib-0018): 4).
In 2013, they launched a major initiative that focused on stakeholder benefits beyond the firm's direct financial interests: the Dell 2020 Legacy of Good Plan, focusing on environmental and community improvements, resulting in a successful reduction in greenhouse gas emissions and the establishment of an e-waste recycling hub in East Africa (Dell, [2014](#smj70031-bib-0019)).
These actual CSP improvements by Dell suggest that firms, facing increased activism risk after anti-SLAPP legislation, may intentionally improve institutional CSP as a proactive strategy to deter future activism.
We therefore propose:
<!-- para 23 -->
Hypothesis 1.Following the enactment of anti-SLAPP laws, institutional CSP increases.
<!-- para 25 -->
As discussed earlier, activists strategically select targets that perform poorly on stakeholder-related issues, as such firms provide a stronger basis for effective campaigns (King, [2008](#smj70031-bib-0047)).
Scholars refer to this condition as a firm's vulnerability to activism, defined as its susceptibility to activist attacks due to verifiable misalignments with societal expectations (Walker et al., [2008](#smj70031-bib-0083)).
A firm's vulnerability influences whether activists can credibly frame it as belonging to a morally disfavored category, a framing that is critical for garnering external support (Den Hond & De Bakker, [2007](#smj70031-bib-0020)).
Because activist campaigns depend heavily on mobilizing public support, they prioritize targets whose missteps are clear, credible, and communicable (Briscoe et al., [2015](#smj70031-bib-0013)).
In this context, well-documented evidence of corporate social irresponsibility (CSI), such as human rights violations and environmental damage, provides activists with persuasive evidence to legitimize their claims, galvanize public support, and “prosecute” the firm in the court of public opinion (Lenox & Eesley, [2009](#smj70031-bib-0050)).
<!-- para 26 -->
Building on this logic, we argue that media coverage of CSI amplifies a firm's vulnerability to activism risk, particularly in the context of anti-SLAPP laws, which increase the likelihood of stakeholder mobilization.
When a firm's irresponsible practices are widely reported in the media, it becomes harder for the firm to manage its public image (McDonnell & King, [2013](#smj70031-bib-0054)), and easier for activists to credibly frame it as morally culpable (Den Hond & De Bakker, [2007](#smj70031-bib-0020)).
As a result, firms receiving extensive media coverage of CSI are more likely to be perceived as credible targets by activists (Eesley et al., [2016](#smj70031-bib-0025); King, [2008](#smj70031-bib-0047)).
<!-- para 27 -->
Firms facing such heightened vulnerability to activism are especially in need of restoring or bolstering their legitimacy (Zavyalova et al., [2012](#smj70031-bib-0088)).
To counter the negative narratives amplified by CSI-related media coverage, these firms are more likely to enhance their institutional CSP, delivering tangible outcomes that signal alignment with broader societal expectations.
Such improvements help rebuild stakeholder trust by generating moral capital and strengthening normative legitimacy (Acharya et al., [2022](#smj70031-bib-0001); Godfrey, [2005](#smj70031-bib-0032)).
While prior CSI remains part of the public record, demonstrable improvements in institutional CSP can provide counter-evidence that complicates activists' efforts to portray the firm as persistently harmful (McDonnell & King, [2013](#smj70031-bib-0054)).
In this way, institutional CSP becomes a strategic tool to reduce the firm's vulnerability by mitigating the moral critique that activists rely on to mobilize support (Baron, [2016](#smj70031-bib-0008)).
<!-- para 28 -->
Taken together, these arguments suggest that media coverage of CSI conditions how firms respond to the increased activism risk triggered by the enactment of anti-SLAPP laws.
When firms are publicly associated with irresponsible records, anti-SLAPP laws make it easier for activists to mobilize against them, as well-documented CSI evidence covered by media ensures that these firms are morally contestable (Zavyalova et al., [2012](#smj70031-bib-0088)).
In such cases, improving institutional CSP becomes a strategic means of risk mitigation (Baron, [2016](#smj70031-bib-0008)).
These firms are thus more likely to respond to anti-SLAPP legislation by enhancing institutional CSP, seeking to build moral capital and restore legitimacy (Acharya et al., [2022](#smj70031-bib-0001); Godfrey, [2005](#smj70031-bib-0032)), hopefully counterbalancing the negative public narratives and therefore reducing their vulnerability to activist targeting (McDonnell & King, [2013](#smj70031-bib-0054)).
In contrast, firms with little or no media exposure for CSI face lower perceived vulnerability, as the relative absence of publicized misconduct makes it more difficult for activists to frame them as socially irresponsible or galvanize public support against them (Briscoe & Gupta, [2016](#smj70031-bib-0012)).
As a result, these firms are less likely to view institutional CSP as a necessary improvement, even in the presence of activist-friendly legislation.
We therefore propose:
<!-- para 29 -->
Hypothesis 2.The positive relationship between anti-SLAPP laws and institutional CSP will be stronger for firms with greater CSI coverage in the media.

## methods
<!-- para 3 -->
Our sample starts with the universe of US public firms covered in the MSCI (formerly KLD) database from 1991 to 2018, which we match against firm-year observations available in Compustat.
MSCI contains firm-level CSP data, and Compustat contains accounting data and additional firm-level information including headquarters, state, and industry classification.
We obtain data on media coverage of CSI from the RepRisk database,[2](#smj70031-note-0002_note_1 "Link to note") which is available only from 2007 onward.
Thus, our empirical analysis examining the moderating effect of media coverage of CSI is confined to the period 2007–2018.
We obtain state-level GDP data from the US Bureau of Economic Analysis, state-level voting data in presidential elections from The American Presidency Project,[3](#smj70031-note-0003_note_2 "Link to note") and state-level natural disaster data from the Spatial Hazard Events and Losses Database for the United States,[4](#smj70031-note-0004_note_3 "Link to note") all of which are available for 1991–2018.
After excluding observations with missing data, our final sample includes 3488 firms (28,353 firm-year observations) for testing Hypothesis [^2], and 3005 firms (19,363 firm-year observations) for testing Hypothesis [^3].
<!-- para 4 -->
For supplemental analyses supporting our theoretical assumptions, we use two additional datasets.
BoardEx provides data on the presence of board-level CSR committees, which we use to assess the discriminant validity of institutional CSP as conceptually distinct from CSR (governance) structures.
We employ SigWatch to obtain data on NGO activist campaigns.
Since 2011, SigWatch has tracked roughly 13,000 activist groups involved in over 75,000 campaigns targeting more than 27,000 firms worldwide, compiling data from public sources such as NGO websites, press releases, and reports.
For each campaign, SigWatch documents the activist group, the targeted firm, the nature of the campaign, and other relevant attributes.
We use this data to identify whether and how often firms in our sample are targeted by NGO activism.
Due to data availability, these analyses are restricted to the 2011–2018 period.
<!-- para 7 -->
Our theory focuses on firms' actual performance improvements in stakeholder-related outcomes.
Following prior research, we use MSCI (formerly KLD) data to measure institutional CSP (e.g., Acharya et al., [2022](#smj70031-bib-0001); Godfrey et al., [2009](#smj70031-bib-0033)).
As noted in its handbook, MSCI ESG Research ([2019](#smj70031-bib-0061): 11; emphasis in italics) provides “positive and negative environmental, social, and governance (ESG) *performance* indicators applied to a universe of publicly traded companies.”
<!-- para 8 -->
MSCI evaluates firms using seven categories: Community, Corporate Governance, Diversity, Environment, Employee Relations, Human Rights, and Product.
Consistent with prior research (e.g., Cronqvist & Yu, [2017](#smj70031-bib-0016); Jia et al., [2020](#smj70031-bib-0045)), we exclude the Corporate Governance category because it reflects firms' engagement with shareholders rather than with broader stakeholders.
Within each category, MSCI rates a number of CSP strengths as well as CSP concerns, and scholars commonly compute the CSP score as the number of strengths net of the number of concerns (e.g., Acharya et al., [2022](#smj70031-bib-0001); Jia et al., [2020](#smj70031-bib-0045)).[5](#smj70031-note-0005_note_4 "Link to note") A higher score indicates more socially responsible performance.
<!-- para 9 -->
We operationalize *institutional CSP* as the additive score (strengths minus concerns) in the Community, Environment, and Human Rights categories.
These domains primarily involve stakeholders such as local communities and society at large, who generally do not engage in regular economic exchange with firms.
Providing social goods in these areas, often beyond the firm's formal boundaries, is central to institutional CSP (Godfrey et al., [2009](#smj70031-bib-0033)).
<!-- para 10 -->
To demonstrate discriminant validity, we separately operationalize what prior work refers to as technical CSP (Godfrey et al., [2009](#smj70031-bib-0033)), using the additive score in the Diversity,[6](#smj70031-note-0006_note_5 "Link to note") Employee Relations, and Product categories.
These domains primarily benefit stakeholders like employees and customers, who have direct economic relationships with the firm.
<!-- para 11 -->
Finally, our theory distinguishes between institutional CSP and CSR structures.
To verify this distinction, we include CSR structures as a placebo outcome variable.
Following McDonnell et al. ([2015](#smj70031-bib-0055)), we measure the presence of a *board CSR committee* using a binary indicator, which equals 1 if the firm has a CSR-related committee in a given year and 0 otherwise.
We rely on BoardEx for this measure and identify relevant committees based on whether their names include any of the following terms: corporate social responsibility, CSR, corporate responsibility, social responsibility, sustainability, environmental, ESG, public responsibility, responsible business, citizenship, or social impact.[7](#smj70031-note-0007_note_6 "Link to note") Firms with committees including any of these terms are coded as having a CSR committee.
<!-- para 13 -->
*Anti-SLAPP laws* are measured as a dummy variable, which equals 1 if the state in which the firm is headquartered has enacted such laws by year t, and 0 otherwise.
Following prior research (e.g., Heider & Ljungqvist, [2015](#smj70031-bib-0037)).
We use historical headquarters information.
During our sample period, 169 of 3488 firms (4.8%) relocated their headquarters.
<!-- para 15 -->
To measure the *media coverage of CSI*, we follow Kölbel et al. ([2017](#smj70031-bib-0049)) and count the number of news articles mentioning a firm's CSI each year, using data from the RepRisk database.
While RepRisk covers a wide range of environmental, social, and governance issues, we focus only on environmental and social dimensions to align with our interest in stakeholder rather than shareholder activism.
RepRisk also rates the severity of CSI coverage (high, medium, low) and the reach of each article (high: global; medium: national or regional; low: local).
For our analysis, we include all articles regardless of severity or reach, assuming that even local or low-severity coverage can influence firms' adaptation (Kölbel et al., [2017](#smj70031-bib-0049)).[8](#smj70031-note-0008_note_7 "Link to note") We standardize this moderating variable prior to analysis to facilitate interpretation, following the approach recommended by Aiken and West ([1991](#smj70031-bib-0003)).
<!-- para 17 -->
We control for a set of firm-level, industry-level, and state-level characteristics that may affect institutional CSP.
At the firm level, since larger firms often have more resources to improve institutional CSP, we control for *firm size*, measured as total assets, expressed in billions.
Since financial performance could influence CSP (Hubbard et al., [2017](#smj70031-bib-0040)), we control for *return on assets* (ROA), measured as the ratio of net income to total book value of assets.
Since slack resources can be deployed for CSP improvement (Arora & Dharwadkar, [2011](#smj70031-bib-0005)), we control for *leverage*, measured as the ratio of long-term debt to total assets.
<!-- para 18 -->
At the industry level, since competitive dynamics can affect CSP (Flammer, [2015](#smj70031-bib-0026)), we control for *industry competition*, measured as the reverse-coded Herfindahl–Hirschman Index (HHI) of total assets within each two-digit SIC industry.
<!-- para 19 -->
At the state level, since economic conditions may impact firms' willingness and ability to improve CSP (Flammer & Luo, [2017](#smj70031-bib-0029)), we control for *state GDP*, measured as the log-transformed value of GDP (in millions) in the firms' state of headquarters.
Since time-varying socio-political factors may influence commitment to improve CSP (Di Giuli & Kostovetsky, [2014](#smj70031-bib-0022)), we control for *state liberalism*, measured as the ratio of Democratic to Republican votes in the most recent presidential election in the firms' state of headquarters (Gupta et al., [2017](#smj70031-bib-0035)).
Since natural disasters may nudge corporate CSP improvement (Luo et al., [2016](#smj70031-bib-0051)), we control for *state disasters*, measured as the log-transformed number of earthquakes, flooding, and wildfires in the firms' state of headquarters.
<!-- para 20 -->
To account for time-invariant factors that may impact corporate responses to the enactment of anti-SLAPP laws, we include firm, state,[9](#smj70031-note-0009_note_8 "Link to note") and year fixed effects.
Following prior DiD studies (e.g., Jia et al., [2020](#smj70031-bib-0045); Qian et al., [2019](#smj70031-bib-0070)), we lag the moderating and control variables by 1 year to better account for their effect on subsequent CSP improvement.[10](#smj70031-note-0010_note_9 "Link to note") Consistent with prior CSR research that uses DiD design (e.g., Flammer, [2015](#smj70031-bib-0026); Flammer & Kacperczyk, [2019](#smj70031-bib-0028)), we do not lag the treatment variable (i.e., anti-SLAPP laws) because doing so will lead to the identification of the treatment effect before the treatment is onset.

## discussion
<!-- para 2 -->
This study examines whether and how firms adapt their institutional CSP to mitigate the risk of stakeholder activism.
Empirically, we exploit a quasi-natural experiment provided by the staggered enactment of anti-SLAPP laws in the United States and examine how they affect firms' institutional CSP from 1991 to 2018.
The anti-SLAPP laws protect stakeholder activists by preventing firms from using costly lawsuits to intimidate and silence critics.
Therefore, the enactments of anti-SLAPP laws provide exogenous variation in the risk of stakeholder activism.
Using a DiD research design, we find that firms improve their institutional CSP in response to the enactment of the anti-SLAPP laws, consistent with our prediction.
The increase in institutional CSP is larger for firms with higher firm-specific activism risk, such as those with greater media coverage of CSI.
<!-- para 4 -->
This work makes three contributions to the literature.
First, the insights this study provides advance nascent scholarship on corporate strategies to preempt activism risk.
While research on stakeholder activism has predominantly taken a backward-looking view of firm responses to stakeholder activism, wherein firms adopt defensive measures to address active activism threats (e.g., King, [2008](#smj70031-bib-0047); McDonnell & King, [2013](#smj70031-bib-0054); McDonnell & Werner, [2016](#smj70031-bib-0057)), less attention has been given to the plausibility of a forward-looking view, wherein firms take preemptive actions to avoid activism risks before such risks later evolve into active threats (Wu & Liu, [2024](#smj70031-bib-0085)).
Odziemkowska and Dorobantu ([2021](#smj70031-bib-0062)) and Yue et al. ([2013](#smj70031-bib-0087)) are two exceptional studies in this area, wherein they focus on industries where location choices are critical and point out two preemptive mechanisms that firms use to avoid activism: avoidance (Yue et al., [2013](#smj70031-bib-0087)) and contracting (Odziemkowska & Dorobantu, [2021](#smj70031-bib-0062)).
We add momentum to this nascent scholarship by looking at a different mechanism to mitigate activism risk: institutional CSP as corporate self-regulation.
Departing from prior work focused on location-sensitive sectors, we show that firms across a broader range of industries adopt institutional CSP as a preemptive strategy to manage elevated activism risk.
This moves the scholarly conversation beyond context-specific tactics toward a more generalizable approach and positions institutional CSP as a broadly relevant tool for proactively managing activism risk.
<!-- para 5 -->
Second, this research advances the scholarly understanding of opportunity structures in stakeholder activism by emphasizing the theoretical importance of *interdependence* across multiple levels.
Prior studies have typically examined firm-, industry-, or state-level conditions in isolation, treating each as an independent driver of a firm's exposure to activism (e.g., Briscoe et al., [2014](#smj70031-bib-0011); Schurman, [2004](#smj70031-bib-0075); Walker et al., [2008](#smj70031-bib-0083)).
What has been largely missing is attention to how these levels interact to influence firm behavior.
We address this gap by showing how environment-level opportunity structures (e.g., anti-SLAPP laws) interact with firm-level conditions (e.g., media coverage of CSI) to shape how firms adapt.
This perspective underscores the promise of exploring how opportunity structures interact with each other in enacting (or constraining) stakeholder activism.
<!-- para 6 -->
Third, this work contributes to research on how institutions affect firm CSP (Ioannou & Serafeim, [2022](#smj70031-bib-0043)).
Extant work often takes a comparative approach, investigating how cross-national institutions help explain the heterogeneity in firm CSP across countries (Odziemkowska & Henisz, [2021](#smj70031-bib-0063)).
We attempt to advance this research stream by examining the role of subnational institutions (e.g., state-level variation in laws) in encouraging or constraining firm CSP improvement (for an exception, see Park et al., [2025](#smj70031-bib-0066)).
By showcasing that the enactment of anti-SLAPP laws encourages institutional CSP but not technical CSP, our research also heeds Ioannou and Serafeim's ([2022](#smj70031-bib-0043): 859) call to understand why “institutions are likely to affect certain types of CSR actions and strategies more so than others.” Our findings, showing that anti-SLAPP laws nudge firms to improve their institutional CSP, suggest that it might be fruitful for future research to explore the effects of other activism-friendly legislations, such as whistleblower protection laws (Peffer et al., [2015](#smj70031-bib-0068)), on firms' adaptation within their stakeholder environments.
This direction of research holds the potential to elucidate the nuanced ways in which various activism-friendly legislations can nudge firms' stakeholder-friendly adaptation.
<!-- para 8 -->
This study has its limitations that indicate the direction of potential future investigations.
First, while we view institutional CSP as a self-regulatory tool to mitigate negative stakeholder assessments, there's a potential variability in how stakeholders receive these efforts.
Future studies could enrich our understanding by integrating stakeholder receptivity into models of preemptive strategies.
<!-- para 9 -->
Second, our empirical analysis does not delve into the nuanced decision-making processes of firms as they respond to anti-SLAPP law enactments.
The complexity of obtaining a deeper understanding through executive interviews is compounded by the anticipated social desirability bias.
This bias significantly impedes the ability to accurately gauge the strategic motivations driving firms' institutional CSP improvement in the wake of anti-SLAPP legislation, as executives may prefer to frame their CSP improvement as altruistically motivated rather than strategic maneuvers to counteract activism risks.
Thus, future research may find it fruitful to explore innovative methods that can effectively navigate around the social desirability bias in executive reporting, thereby unveiling the true strategic underpinnings of CSP improvement within the context of anti-SLAPP legislation.
Such research could employ indirect approaches aimed at securing more authentic qualitative insights into corporate decision-making processes.
<!-- para 10 -->
Finally, while our research aims to examine the possibility of whether and how firms preempt the risk of stakeholder activism that would have materialized, this does not mean institutional CSP would be the *only* preemptive tactic.
There remains significant room for future research detailing other types of preemptive actions that firms may take with the intent of mitigating the risk of would-be activism.
<!-- para 12 -->
This research shows that activists-friendly legal reforms like anti-SLAPP laws can drive meaningful improvements in firms' institutional CSP, not by mandating changes, but by altering the stakeholder environment firms operate in.
When firms lose the ability to suppress critics through litigation, they become more likely to improve their institutional CSP as a way to avoid becoming targets of stakeholder activism.
For policymakers, this suggests that safeguarding civic freedoms and protecting public dissent go beyond upholding democratic principles; it intentionally or unintentionally incentivizes firms to elevate their institutional CSP, creating broader societal benefits.
<!-- para 14 -->
Whereas research on corporate responses to materialized stakeholder activism has proliferated, theoretical understanding of how firms may attempt to reduce the risk of *would-be* activism remains underdeveloped.
To address this gap, we exploit the staggered enactment of anti-SLAPP laws in the United States, which constrain firms' ability to use strategic litigation to deter critics, thereby providing exogenous variation in stakeholder activism risk.
Employing a DiD research design, we find that firms improve their institutional CSP in response to the enactments of anti-SLAPP laws, especially when firms are subject to a higher risk of being targeted.
These findings suggest that firms try to use self-regulatory adaptation to mitigate the risk of potential activism.
We hope this research offers a base to continue to explore the *pre-activism* strategies that firms employ to co-opt their stakeholder environments.
