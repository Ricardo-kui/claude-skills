# anand_mukherjee_2024_learning_from_failures_di 句子库存

## introduction
<!-- para 2 -->
Organizational learning theory (Cyert and March 1963; Argyris and Schön 1978, 1996; Argote 2013) suggests that failures can activate searches for solutions and result in improvements (Sitkin 1992, Dahlin et al. 2018).
We define product failures as undesirable outcomes for users (Cannon and Edmondson 2001).
We add nuance to the perspective of learning from failures by dividing product failures into two types based on their characteristics and by studying whether learning from failures occurs for both types of failures (Muehlfeld et al. 2012, Dahlin et al. 2018).
Based on related literature (Rasmussen 1983, Frese and Keith 2015), we categorize failures into those that result from slip-ups in production processes versus those that occur because of knowledge gaps in product designs.
Given that organizational learning requires multiple mechanisms (Popper and Lipshitz 1998) including reinforcement of frequently repeated established production practices and experimentation for less frequent changes to product designs (Anand et al. 2016, Eggers and Suh 2019), it is valuable to study the extent to which learning happens in organizations from different types of failures (Park et al. 2023).
<!-- para 3 -->
Thus, in this research, we conceptually develop the two-way categorization of failures mentioned earlier and use it to empirically address the question of whether learning occurs from both, slip-up failures and knowledge gap failures.
Furthermore, we delve deeper into the differences in these learning mechanisms by including the enhancing effects of the absorptive capacity coming from innovation capabilities (Cohen and Levinthal 1990).
Absorptive capacity theory posits that firms have different capacities to absorb and use information, and that such capacities are more prevalent and stronger in firms with a history of innovation outcomes and efforts (Cohen and Levinthal 1990).
Although most research on absorptive capacity focuses on absorbing knowledge from external sources such as alliance partners (Vasudeva and Anand 2011), acquired firms (Sears and Hoetker 2014), external researchers (Cockburn and Henderson 1998), suppliers (Zacharia et al. 2011), and information technology providers (Setia and Patel 2013), we apply the absorptive capacity perspective to learning from product failures, absorbing knowledge from the firms' ongoing innovation efforts and past innovations (Dahlin et al. 2018).
<!-- para 4 -->
Based on organizational learning and absorptive capacity theories, we develop a set of hypotheses, and empirically test them using data on product recalls, which are widely used as indicators of product failures (Haunschild and Rhee 2004, Thirumalai and Sinha 2011, Kalaignanam et al. 2013, Shah et al. 2017, Gao et al. 2022, Astvansh et al. 2022a).
Applying our categorization of slip-up and knowledge gap failures, we divide product recalls into two types, process-related and design-related recalls, to represent each failure type.
Innovation capabilities are measured using accumulated patents and lagged research and development (R&D) intensity (DeCarolis and Deeds 1999, Pennetier et al. 2019).
<!-- para 5 -->
We offer two examples of firm learning from product recalls to clarify the context of our research.
First, consider the following instance of product failure and the subsequent actions described to us by a production manager of a large medical device manufacturing firm.
The product is a medical implant device that is used to prevent blood clot formation (left atrial appendage occlusion) for certain types of cardiac patients.
After reports of a few instances of failures of the device, the executives at the firm decided to issue a recall.
A quality management team was put in charge of the recall, and the team appraised the appropriate regulatory agency about the recall, after which the agency took responsibility for oversight of the recovery and returns of the product and the subsequent steps for proper termination of the recall.
The quality management team investigated the manufacturing and delivery processes and the design of the device.
Members of the team collaborated with surgeons who had experience in implanting this device and consulted with other teams in their own firm working on the design of related products.
The team found that the root cause of the failure was inverted implantations of the device, which were traced to difficulty in distinguishing the top and bottom of the device.
This was a design-related recall that occurred because of the failure to gather knowledge about use, and the failure to recognize potential modes of errors during implantations of the product, before launching the product.
The quality management team completed the steps for the product recall and worked with the product design team to redesign the device, which was subsequently reintroduced to users after supplementary approvals and clearance from the regulatory agency.
The discovery of the potential failure mode in this way resulted in the inclusion of a defect prevention feature in that version and all other versions of the device and in other products designed by the manufacturer since then.
<!-- para 6 -->
Our second example of learning from recalls comes from our data on product recalls combined with information from past interactions of one of the coauthors with executives at the manufacturing firm making the product and surgeons at a hospital using the product.
This product is a medical device called the da Vinci surgical robot manufactured by Intuitive Surgical.
In Figure 1, we plot reports of adverse events (solid plot), incidents of recalls (dashed lines), and supplemental approvals that signify design changes (dotted lines) during 2001–2018.
The adverse events (solid plot) spiked after about 144 months, in 2013, and there were several recalls (dashed lines) related to the surgical tip and the robotic “EndoWrist” portions of the robot in a 24-month period.
There were several supplemental design approval applications (dotted lines) throughout the 2001–2018 period related to new developments in the surgical tip and the robotic EndoWrist.
From our patent data, we note that Intuitive Surgical also received several patents related to those features of the robot, starting from 1999 and continuing up to the period immediately following recalls (dashed lines).
Evidently, the tradition of deep innovation at Intuitive Surgical, as indicated by prior patents, and the intensity of ongoing innovation efforts, as evidenced by the supplemental approvals and patents immediately after the recalls, resulted in high capabilities for investigating the root-causes of the failures and redesigning the product for improved future performance, which resulted in the drop in subsequent adverse events.
Overall, this plot depicts an example of the learning effect of recalls supported by innovations, resulting in design changes and subsequent reduction in adverse events and recalls after 175 months, around 2015.
<!-- para 7 -->
For our empirical analysis of learning from product failures, we source and compile data on product recalls for publicly traded firms in the medical device and pharmaceutical industries in the United States, which are regulated by the Food and Drug Administration (FDA).
Selecting this context gives us the ability to find reliable sources of data to measure the different constructs needed for our analyses.
We use text analysis of recall announcements to identify process-related and design-related recalls, and negative binomial generalized linear models (GLMs) to study firm learning from recalls and the influence of accumulated patents and lagged R&D intensity on firm learning.
Patent and Trademark Office (USPTO) and R&D investment data from the Wharton Research Data Services (WRDS) database.
<!-- para 8 -->
Our results indicate that (1) firms learn more from design-related than from process-related recalls, (2) innovation capabilities, that is, accumulated patents and lagged R&D intensity, enhance learning from recalls, and (3) the enhancement in learning supported by innovation capabilities is greater for design-related recalls than for process-related recalls.
<!-- para 11 -->
From a theoretical perspective, our results support the notion that learning from failures is dependent on two aspects of the failure context: reasons for failures, and innovation efforts of firms.
We demonstrate how these two aspects interact leading to within-firm (reasons for failure) and across-firm (innovation efforts) heterogeneity in learning from failures.
<!-- para 12 -->
We discuss our main results, supplemented by results from a post hoc analysis, supporting the idea that the two types of recalls invoke different learning mechanisms.
Our results suggest that learning from failures takes place more readily for knowledge gap failures, which call for greater use of focused root cause analysis, experimentation, and implementation of design changes.
The same result is not as strong for learning from slip-up failures, which call for more deliberate actions to improve the sustainment of process compliance.
<!-- para 13 -->
Overall, we make three contributions from this research.
First, we develop a two-way categorization of failures and study the impacts on learning in the context of each type.
Second, we provide evidence of the connection between learning from failures and innovation capabilities.
Third, we generate implications for more comprehensive learning from failures including slip-up failures.
This reinforces the importance of organizational culture for sustained efforts toward process improvement (Sitkin et al. 1994, Repenning and Sterman 2001) and deliberate efforts toward maintaining the dual focus on innovation and improvement (Adler and Borys 1996, Chandrasekaran et al. 2012).
<!-- para 14 -->
In Section 2, we describe our categorization of product failures and connect it to the context of product recalls.
In Section 3, we develop our hypotheses based on organizational learning and absorptive capacity theories, following which we describe our sample and analytical methods in Section 4.
In Section 5, we report the results of the main analyses, robustness checks, and post hoc analysis.
We conclude the paper with Section 6, in which we discuss the implications of our results, state the contributions and limitations of this study, and identify opportunities for further research.

## theory
<!-- para 3 -->
Failures are defined as the outcomes of errors resulting in departures from anticipated outcomes (Cannon and Edmondson 2005, Dahlin et al. 2018).
Product failures are distinct from managerial failures in terms of the possibilities and the mechanisms for learning from each (Sitkin 1992, Goodman et al. 2011).
Managerial failures are related to broader decisions with more strategic and greater firm-wide implications such as investing in a technology or a new product that fails to capture the market due to errors in competitive positioning and market segmentation (Eggers 2012a, b), overextending the product portfolio (Barnett and Freeman 2001), or engaging in misaligned acquisitions (Muehlfeld et al. 2012).
The causes of such failures are related to judgment-based choices of managers or management teams (Sitkin 1992, Cannon and Edmondson 2005).
Product failures, in contrast, are technical failures in the design, manufacturing, or use of products.
As such, product failures are conducive to gathering information about them followed by investigation of their causes and experimentation with alternatives to remedy the causes for failures (Khanna et al. 2016).
<!-- para 4 -->
Product failures are unavoidable to some extent because of the increasing complexities of processes and the continuous development of new products (Thirumalai and Sinha 2011, Shah et al. 2017, Ball et al. 2018, Zou et al. 2023).
Reactions to product failures can result in experiential learning though the existence of such learning is not automatic and warrants deliberate organizational efforts (Edmondson 2011, 2023).
Such efforts include creating the "opportunity, motivation, and ability to integrate and discuss learning factors at the individual, group, and organizational levels" (Dahlin et al. 2018, p. 253) The mechanisms for learning consist of the allocation of attention to causes for failures (Ocasio 1997, Park et al. 2023) leading to search for solutions to avoid future failures and followed by actions to implement those solutions (Cyert and March 1963).
<!-- para 5 -->
Although there is support for the prevalence of learning from product failures (Haunschild and Rhee 2004, Thirumalai and Sinha 2011, Kalaignanam et al. 2013), there have been repeated calls for further studies of learning from different types of failures and identifying conditions conducive to such learning (Wowak and Boone 2015, Dahlin et al. 2018, Park et al. 2023).
Furthermore, related to the searches and implementations associated with learning from product failures, the ability to make sense of errors and handle the uncertainty and complexity of developing solutions is important for deriving lessons from failure experiences (Park et al. 2023).
Innovation capabilities, with their focus on deriving solutions in similarly uncertain and complex conditions, can enhance the capability of learning from failures (Greve and Taylor 2000, Maslach 2016).
Thus, in this research, we add contextual specificity to the phenomenon of learning from failures by categorizing failures into two types and by including the impact of innovation capabilities of firms in learning from them.
<!-- para 6 -->
We characterize product failures as two types: (1) those related to the execution of prescribed established processes that we label slip-up failures, and (2) those related to missing functionalities or malfunctions that we call knowledge gap failures (Rasmussen 1983, Goodman et al. 2011, Frese and Keith 2015).
Whereas slip-up failures are caused by the inappropriate application of established rules, knowledge gap failures are caused by the nonexistence of complete rules owing to incomplete models of the performance contexts or the development of novel performance contexts (Reason 2016).
Slip-up and knowledge gap failures mirror “avoidable errors and the unavoidable outcomes of experiments and risk taking” (Cannon and Edmondson 2005, p. 300).
There is less ambiguity about the causes of the failures in the case of slip-up failures than about the causes of knowledge gap failures.
<!-- para 7 -->
Slip-up failures occur, for instance, because an operator neglects to follow instructions for the production or the handling of materials or misinterprets instructions.
Such failures can be traced to a lack of training, distraction, fatigue, multiple goals, inadequate instructions, or laxity in oversight.
Two examples of slip-up product failures are product contamination due to the violation of cleanliness protocols, and faulty product due to the miscalibration of a measurement scale used to measure ingredients.
Knowledge gap product failures occur, for instance, because of problems in the performance or functionality of a product originating from inadequate conceptualization, incorrect assumptions about use contexts, unforeseen modes of interactions with environmental conditions, missing features expected by customers, or errors in the creative or technical aspects of the product design.
Two examples of knowledge gap failures are unexpected interactions of two medications and failure of a device to perform its intended function for reasons that are not clear.
<!-- para 9 -->
Recalls are the correction or removal of products following failures after the manufacturer has made the products available to customers (FDA 2020b).
Recalls for consumer products are common, and their numbers have been increasing steadily, as evidenced by the regularly updated list at recalls.gov, which is jointly maintained by six federal agencies in the United States.
There were more than 850 recall events in the second quarter of 2023, and the total number of units recalled over the year 2023 was expected to be greater than one billion. [^1] Recalls can result in significant direct and indirect costs to manufacturers.
For instance, Johnson and Johnson's 2010 recall of Children's Tylenol, [^2] owing to the discovery of higher than allowed concentration of active ingredients, substandard raw materials, and contamination, cost the company an estimated 665 million in lost revenue.
In 2012, Pfizer recalled about one million packets of birth control pills due to an error in packaging that caused hormone-containing pills to be placed in slots for placebos and resulted in unexpected pregnancies (Natof and Pellegrini 2023).
These instances point to the significance of this phenomenon and emphasize the importance of informing practitioners about learning from them.
<!-- para 10 -->
Recall counts not only provide a large amount of independently collected and longitudinal data that can be used to study learning based on experience, but the texts of their announcements also provide information that can be used to derive rich insights into the reasons for recalls and to make implications about the attention that they generate (Ocasio 1997, Park et al. 2023) for slip-up versus knowledge gap failures.
Although companies and regulatory agencies strive to reduce recalls, there is a recognition that the risk of failures that result in recalls cannot be eliminated, especially in the face of evolving technologies and products and changing regulations (Shah et al. 2017, Ball et al. 2018, Zou et al. 2023).
A strategic approach to managing recalls is to not only resolve the failures that caused them but also derive lessons for the development and production of future products and processes (Sitkin 1992, Craig and Thomas 1996).
<!-- para 11 -->
Extant literature has pointed to manufacturing processes and product designs as two main sources for product recalls (Hora et al. 2011, Shah et al. 2017).
Process-related recalls originate from defects that can be traced to shortfalls in process compliance (Ball et al. 2018).
These defects would not have occurred but for missteps in processes such as incorrect settings on machines, improper procedures conducted by operators, and imperfect coordination and monitoring of supply chains.
Design-related recalls originate from causes related to the development or enhancement of products, overlooking failure modes and effects, underestimating the likelihood of failure-occurrences although in use, and unsuitable choices of design parameters (Thirumalai and Sinha 2011, Ball et al. 2018).
The interdependencies of components and the complexities of their interactions are sometimes observable only as the products are in use (Mukherjee and Sinha 2018).
Process-related recalls and design-related recalls fit well into the descriptions of slip-up failures and knowledge gap failures.
As such, we develop our hypotheses using the empirical context of product recalls, which are the phenomena that we observe and examine.
Subsequently, based on our results, we derive implications for the larger context of product failures.
<!-- para 12 -->
We use data on recalls issued by FDA-regulated firms in the medical device and pharmaceutical industries.
The lessons from our research using these recalls generally apply to learning from failures in all consumer products, albeit with one caveat: Although recalls in some other industries such as automobiles and household products are more substantially divided between voluntary and involuntary recalls (Chen et al. 2009, Astvansh et al. 2022b), most of the FDA-related recalls are voluntarily issued by the manufacturer and subsequently overseen by the agency (Williams 2010, Nagaich and Sadhna 2015).
Thus, recalls in our context are expected to be more of authentic firm-choices that lead to learning from the underlying failures than the superficial firm-reactions that are associated with mandated recalls (Haunschild and Rhee 2004).

Starting as a compliance investigator at the agency, this expert held several management positions for over two decades before retiring from the FDA; job responsibilities included determining policies and procedures for overseeing compliance and manufacturing and heading regulatory functions.
During their career with the FDA, this expert oversaw the recall management group at the agency's Center for Devices and Radiological Health where they were also instrumental in developing current recall policy and procedures, and then went on to oversee the FDA's Recall and Policy group, responsible for recalls affecting all industries that the agency regulates.

<!-- para 18 -->
In addition, we studied the official guidance manuals published by the FDA for pharmaceuticals (FDA 2020b) and medical devices (FDA 2014).
Our conceptualization is also based on contextual knowledge gained from our formal interactions with FDA officials on two past projects funded by the agency and completed in 2021.
We reference concepts from organizational learning theory as the foundations for our hypotheses.
<!-- para 19 -->
The FDA regulates the manufacture of medical devices and pharmaceuticals.
The agency approves the usage of devices and pharmaceuticals and sets guidelines for their manufacturing and distribution (Figure 3, Step 1).
The manufacturing firms monitor user reports and gather market intelligence (Ball et al. 2018) (Step 2).
When a product failure or potential for failure is detected, a quality management team, usually comprised of cross-functional representatives, analyzes it (Step 3) to determine whether the failure is isolated or systematic (Step 4).
In the case of an isolated failure (Step 4a), action is taken to resolve it.
Such an action of detection and correction is described in organizational learning theory as single-loop learning (Argyris 1977, Argote and Miron-Spektor 2011).
In the case of a determination of systematic causes of failure (Step 4b), the quality management team escalates the incident, raises the attention of higher-level executives, and makes a recommendation to them for issuing a recall (Wowak et al. 2021, Darby et al. 2023).
<!-- para 20 -->
When the higher-level executives decide, the firm issues the recall in coordination with the FDA (Step 5).
Although in some cases, it is required that units of the recalled product be removed from the market, in others, the recalled units are required to be corrected either in the field or at a facility.
The FDA oversees such removals and corrections to ensure the due diligence of the firm and the effectiveness of the steps for completing the requirements of the recall and terminating it.
Next, the quality management team assigned to the recall relies on sources of knowledge inside the firm such as marketing and sales personnel and outside the firm such as suppliers and technology experts, searches for the root causes, and develops ways of reducing the chances of failures of that product and related products in the future.
Collections of failures have been shown to be useful sources of learning (Argote and Greve 2007, Maslach et al. 2018, Park et al. 2023).
<!-- para 22 -->
One perspective applying organizational learning theory to failures is that failure experiences are more effective than success experiences at generating learning because they activate managerial attention (Ocasio 1997, Ocasio et al. 2020) and result in more focused searches for solutions to prevent repetitions (Madsen and Desai 2010, Desai and Madsen 2022).
The opposite view is that firms do not learn from failure experiences because their reactions are myopic in only focusing on the recent failure, there is too much uncertainty and complexity to be able to pinpoint underlying causes, and the results of any changes are not sustained (Cannon and Edmondson 2001, Edmondson 2011).
The contingency view of learning from failures reconciles these opposing views by focusing on conditions under which failures may or may not lead to learning (Park et al. 2023).
<!-- para 23 -->
Existing research on learning from failures resulting in product recalls has pointed to the existence of learning (Thirumalai and Sinha 2011, Kalaignanam et al. 2013).
Research in the automotive industry revealed that voluntary recalls had a greater impact than mandatory recalls (Haunschild and Rhee 2004) and recalls exclusively focusing on internal process failures, disregarding recalls due to supplier failures and design failures from the sample, were impacted by product complexity and capacity utilization (Shah et al. 2017).
In this current research, we seek to add contextual specificity by studying learning from recalls (depicted as Step 6 in Figure 3) implicit in the two different sets of steps (depicted following Steps 6a and 6b in Figure 3) required for slip-up failures manifested in processrelated recalls and knowledge gap failures manifested in design-related recalls.
We also study the contingency role played by the innovation capabilities of firms, represented by accumulated patents and lagged R&D intensities (depicted in dotted line boxes in Figure 3), on such learning.
<!-- para 25 -->
Referring to Figure 3, when the product recall is based on an error of nonadherence or negligence, that is, slip-up, (Figure 3: Step 6a(i)) the corrections need to focus on updating standard operating procedures (Figure 3: Step 6a(ii)) and on training and motivating employees to better adhere to the procedures (Figure 3: Step 6a(iii)).
After the recall is terminated in consultation with the FDA and depending on the substance of the change in work practices, sometimes the firm may need to seek FDA approval for the changes (Figure 3: Step 6a(iv)).
<!-- para 26 -->
Turning to the mechanisms of learning from process-related recalls, research in quality management has emphasized the need to focus attention on process control along with learning (Sitkin et al. 1994) and highlighted the importance of better engagement of employees and teamwork to achieve process control (Anderson et al. 1994).
Previous researchers have studied contexts like those of process-related recalls in which the right way of doing work is mostly known but there is inattention and negligence or shortfall in adherence (Anand et al. 2012, Shah et al. 2017).
The challenge for learning and improving in these contexts is that of combining attention to compliance and efficiency with autonomy and innovation in the attitudes and actions of employees (Adler et al. 1999, Spear and Bowen 1999).
In addition, there are the challenges of preventing deterioration of attention to process compliance over time (Anand et al. 2012) and of needing to change organizational cultures that mostly encourage first-order problem-solving without generating lessons from them to prevent future problems (Tucker and Edmondson 2003).
We posit that the steps in issuing and terminating process-related recalls provide impetus for creating organizational environments (Choo et al. 2007) conducive to developing better processes by bringing attention (Ocasio 1997, Ocasio and Wohlgezogen 2010) to process control.
Such learning and attention should lead to a reduction in subsequent process-related recalls, leading us to hypothesize the following.
<!-- para 27 -->
Accumulated process-related recalls are negatively associated with subsequent process-related recalls.
<!-- para 29 -->
Referring to Figure 3, a design-related recall is based on a defect in an existing product feature or deficiencies in features required by users.
Fulfilling the knowledge gap that resulted in the recall requires searching for knowledge from internal (Figure 3: Step 6b(ii)) and external (Figure 3: Step 6b(iii)) sources and combining it (Figure 3: Step 6b(iv)) into new knowledge to redesign the product (Figure 3: Step 6b(v)).
The quality management team uses and analyzes data and drawings for root cause analysis and development of new designs (Figure 3: Step 6b(vi)).
In addition to terminating the recall in consultation with the FDA, in most cases, the firm needs some approvals from the FDA for the new designs, and, depending on the extent of the change, the approval can be supplemental for an existing product or a fresh additional approval for a new product.
<!-- para 30 -->
A design-related recall has a higher level of ambiguity than a process-related recall (Kalaignanam et al. 2013).
This makes it challenging to nail down causal attributions, which play a crucial role in preventing similar failures in the future (Dahlin et al. 2018, Park et al. 2023).
Research in organizational learning has emphasized the value of structural mechanisms like templates (Jensen and Szulanski 2007), repositories (Maslach et al. 2018), and “transactive memory systems” for “creating, coordinating, retaining, and transferring knowledge in organizations” (Argote and Guo 2016).
The learning “is constructed by firm members as they assemble specific failure events they had not considered” (Maslach et al. 2018, p. 226).
Such learning should lead to a reduction in subsequent design-related recalls, leading us to hypothesize the following.
<!-- para 31 -->
Accumulated design-related recalls are negatively associated with subsequent design-related recalls.
<!-- para 33 -->
Referring to Figure 3, the quality management team investigating the root causes of the recall examines the existing process and product design and consults with the teams that were responsible for the designs.
The quality management team also has access to previous designs and the ability to collaborate with the teams that are developing future products and designing new manufacturing and delivery processes.
This is intended for the teams working on innovations and on recalls to share knowledge and learn from each other.
The firms that do this well deliberately look out for similar issues and for groups of related products.
The ability of firms to act on failures underlying recalls is impacted by their available resources, including the knowledge base from innovation activities.
As the FDA oversees both recalls and approvals for new products and processes, such coordination is even more important because of its impact on future products.
The context expert we interviewed noted that the FDA may choose to increase the frequency of inspections for facilities that have repeat recalls related to similar failures.
<!-- para 34 -->
From a theoretical perspective, organizational mechanisms that support learning have similarities with mechanisms for innovation employed by firms (Brown and Duguid 1991, Laursen and Salter 2006, Lugovoi et al. 2022).
Such mechanisms are aimed at creating new knowledge by discovering and integrating knowledge from external and internal sources.
Thus, the innovation capabilities of firms should enhance learning from failures.
In this current research, we apply the lens of absorptive capacity—the firm's ability to recognize, absorb, and use external knowledge toward organizational goals (Cohen and Levinthal 1990)—and study the impact of innovation capabilities on learning from failures in general followed by learning from the two types of failures.
<!-- para 35 -->
Overall, innovation capabilities enhance firms' understanding of product functionalities and enhance the proficiencies of firms for recovering from failures.
Thus, we propose a role for firm innovation capabilities in developing absorptive capacity for learning from recalls.
Although absorptive capacity is a multidimensional construct (Jansen et al. 2005, Volberda et al. 2010), we focus in this research on the innovation capabilities of firms because of the connection of innovation with learning (Adler and Borys 1996, Chandrasekaran et al. 2012, Zou et al. 2023).
<!-- para 36 -->
Firms' stocks of innovations and their innovation efforts represent two integral aspects of the innovation capabilities of firms (Cohen and Levinthal 1990, DeCarolis and Deeds 1999).
Accumulated stocks of innovations represent a firm's capabilities to identify relevant new knowledge, discern the value of the new knowledge, and assimilate it with existing knowledge, all of which can help firms to learn from failures.
The innovation efforts of a firm are an indication of its prevailing innovation culture, and infrastructure that support the search and identification of new and useful knowledge, which can facilitate the resolution of reasons for failures and reduction in future failures.
These two aspects of innovation capabilities can have different impacts on the two sets of mechanisms that differentiate learning from slip-up failures and learning from knowledge gap failures.
We express our hypotheses for the absorptive capacity effects of stocks of innovations and innovation efforts using the empirical context of accumulated patents and lagged R&D intensity (Pennetier et al. 2019).
<!-- para 38 -->
Stocks of innovations enhance learning from failures in three ways.
First, prior innovation experience provides firms with a greater ability to identify causal associations for failures with product design and processing steps, pointing to what needs to be fixed to prevent repetitions.
Second, stocks of innovations support wider internal searches for effective solutions to newly identified failure modes.
Finally, stocks of innovations help identification of valuable external knowledge, and integration of such knowledge with the existing knowledge base of the firm (Cohen and Levinthal 1990).
The example of learning from failures in the case of the da Vinci surgical robot described earlier (Figure 1) provides a practical illustration of such enhancement owing to a greater stock of innovations.
Patents represent the generation of new processes and products; as such, accumulated patents represent experience in translating ideas into innovations by assimilating added information from external sources and internal developments (Katila and Ahuja 2002, Durand et al. 2008).
Thus, we hypothesize the enhancing effect of accumulated patents on learning from recalls.
<!-- para 39 -->
The negative association of accumulated recalls with subsequent recalls is stronger when there are higher numbers of accumulated patents than when there are fewer accumulated patents.
<!-- para 40 -->
Stocks of innovations, represented by accumulated patents, are an indicator of a firm's orientation toward experimentation, exploration of new ways of doing things, and continuous improvement.
Such orientation is valuable when using process improvement artefacts such as process maps and standard operating procedures to share process knowledge across the firm.
Further, product designs and production technologies are intricately connected with manufacturing processes.
For example, better designs of products lend themselves to a greater appreciation of tolerances and specifications.
Accumulated patents provide evidence of a propensity to integrate and use new knowledge that is valuable for problem-solving in firms (Rothaermel and Deeds 2004, Lane et al. 2006).
This leads to the following hypothesis for the enhancing effect of accumulated patents on learning from experience with process-related recalls:
<!-- para 41 -->
The negative association of accumulated process-related recalls with subsequent process-related recalls is stronger when there are higher numbers of accumulated patents than when there are fewer accumulated patents.
<!-- para 42 -->
Accumulated patents provide evidence of innovative actions for developing or identifying and using new technologies (Van Den Bosch et al. 1999).
Thus, success in innovation, signaled by accumulated patents, better prepares firms to deal with and learn from accumulated design-related recalls.
Moreover, as seen in the case of the da Vinci surgical robot example (Figure 1), access to individuals and teams responsible for those patents provides an advantage in the search for root causes of design-related recalls based on the direct experiences of the firm with the designs being corrected.
Based on these reasons, we put forward the following hypothesis for the enhancing effect of accumulated patents on learning from experience with design-related recalls.
<!-- para 43 -->
The negative association of accumulated design-related recalls with subsequent design-related recalls is stronger when there are higher numbers of accumulated patents than when there are fewer accumulated patents.
<!-- para 45 -->
Finding remedies for failures that resulted in recalls, like innovation efforts, involves troubleshooting problems.
Learning from recalls requires overcoming the challenges of product and supply chain complexities (Zou et al. 2023) through appropriate infrastructure aimed at aspects such as improving traceability (Wowak and Boone 2015) and building technical capabilities (Zavyalova et al. 2012).
Similarly, the active involvement of firms in R&D implies a greater focus on integrating information from diverse sources for problem-solving (Teece et al. 1999).
An internal context of intense R&D efforts helps firms learn better from experience (Argote and Miron-Spektor 2011).
Whereas accumulated patents capture the stocks of innovation knowledge, recent R&D efforts, represented by lagged R&D intensity, reflect the extent of firms' efforts to capture knowledge for innovations (O'Brien 2003).
Based on the notion that the challenges and actions for learning from recalls are paralleled in R&D efforts, we hypothesize that learning from recalls is supported by lagged R&D intensity.
<!-- para 46 -->
The negative association of accumulated recalls with subsequent recalls is stronger when there is higher lagged R&D intensity than when there is lower lagged R&D intensity.
<!-- para 47 -->
Differences in the learning from experiences with process-related and design-related recalls suggest that they may be impacted differently by the intensity of R&D efforts.
Process-related recalls may, at times, generate superficial learning and expedited reactions such as greater reliance on inspections to detect quality problems instead of eliminating such problems through genuine in-depth root cause analyses (Tucker and Edmondson 2003).
Such learning would not be impacted much by the more in-depth R&D searches.
Conversely, process-related recalls may lead to the generation of knowledge about the process that may remain noncodified (Schneider and Sting 2020).
Such knowledge is distributed and absorbed through interactions among individuals and teams working on remedies for the recalls.
R&D efforts similarly include the assimilation of noncodified knowledge (Tatikonda and Montoya-Weiss 2001).
Also, process control and innovation efforts have been shown to be complementary (Sitkin et al. 1994).
Based on these associations, we hypothesize the enhancing effect of lagged R&D intensity on learning from process-related recalls.
<!-- para 48 -->
The negative association of accumulated process-related recalls with subsequent process-related recalls is stronger when there is higher lagged R&D intensity than when there is lower lagged R&D intensity.
<!-- para 49 -->
Design-related recalls call for actions that are close to R&D efforts as they consist of changes to product functionalities.
Correcting for design failures involves applying alternate technologies, relying on new scientific discoveries, and including new features.
A firm's R&D efforts indicate a propensity to tackle uncertainties and complexities in searching for new products, functionalities, mechanisms, and solutions (Jansen et al. 2006).
Such a propensity complements the firm's ability for learning from design-related recalls (Bailey et al. 2014).
Thus, we hypothesize the enhancing effect of lagged R&D intensity on learning from design-related recalls.
<!-- para 50 -->
The negative association of accumulated design-related recalls with subsequent design-related recalls is stronger when there is higher lagged R&D intensity than when there is lower lagged R&D intensity.
<!-- para 51 -->
Altogether, we posit that learning from failures accrues from a combination of identified opportunities provided by recalls, capabilities to learn as indicated by stocks of innovations, and actions toward learning, as provided by R&D efforts.

## methods
<!-- para 3 -->
We test our hypotheses using data on recalls in the U.S. medical device and pharmaceutical industries during 2000–2016.
All recalls in our data set are voluntary recalls.
Our counts of recalls include product withdrawals because they, like recalls, provide firms with opportunities to learn.
The FDA classifies recalls in medical devices and pharmaceuticals into three classes, which decrease in severity from Class I to Class III.
Class III recalls are issued due to minor errors such as misprints in labels on products.
Such failures have minimal chances of harmful effects and do not offer much in terms of learning.
For our main analysis, we removed Class III recalls, which accounted for less than 5% of the recalls in both industries.
Class II recalls account for approximately 95% of the remaining recalls in our sample.
We verified that our main results are robust to the inclusion of all recalls including Class III recalls.
<!-- para 4 -->
We compiled and cross-validated the data on recalls using two sources.
First, we noted all firms that had issued at least one recall in the FDA recalls database in the medical device and pharmaceutical industries.
Our FDA recalls sample started with 9,540 recalls from 1,320 firms.
Second, we downloaded the FDA's Significantly Regulated Organizations (SROs) list that contains details of manufacturers from food, medical device, and pharmaceutical industries.
A vast majority of the SROs are suppliers to OEM manufacturers and are of foreign origin.
From the 8,696 firms in the SRO list, we first selected firms that are listed in the U.S. stock exchanges.
This gave us a list of 1,269 firms, out of which we deleted 578 that operated solely in the food industry, thus retaining 691 firms.
<!-- para 5 -->
From these, we removed firm names that did not exist independently for at least 10 years within the observation period because of mergers and acquisitions (M&As).
An example of firms that merged in our sample is that of Guidant getting acquired by Boston Scientific in 2006, thus only leaving six years of observations.
In the medical device and pharmaceutical industries, M&As are frequent occurrences.
Therefore, identifying an observation unit over the entire sample observation period is complex.
Only accounting for a few years of a firm does not leave us with sufficient observation periods to estimate learning, which occurs over time and based on experience.
On the one hand, including firms with insufficient observation periods is likely to provide noisy and biased estimates of learning.
(2012) demonstrate, when a firm gets acquired, the disruption results in a short-term negative shock on product and process quality.
This effect is also likely to bias learning from product failure experience.
On the other hand, excluding these firms reduces the sample size and potentially eliminates information on some firms, which otherwise could have impacted the results.
Therefore, for the main analysis, we do not include firms with less than ten years of observations.
However, a supplementary analysis with all firms using an unbalanced panel data set gives comparable results as those for our main analyses.
Also, we deleted firms in the SRO database that are not in the FDA recalls database.
This data compilation leaves 116 medical devices and 95 pharmaceutical firms.
<!-- para 6 -->
Next, deleting firms that did not have at least 30 recalls during 2000–2016 (average of ~2 recalls per year), we are left with 67 medical device firms and 63 pharmaceutical firms.
Because learning is expected to result in a reduced number of product failures in the future years, a low base level of recalls is unlikely to provide a good estimate of learning, particularly because the number reduces further when we analyze the two types of failures separately.
However, in this case too, we confirm the results from our main analyses with supplementary analyses including firms that have less than 30 recalls over the entire observation period.
Finally, we deleted an additional fourteen medical device and eight pharmaceutical firms due to the nonavailability of financial data for all the years.
<!-- para 7 -->
Our final sample consists of 1,728 firm-years (108 firms over 16 years) for 53 medical devices and 55 pharmaceutical publicly traded firms in the United States, with 7,984 recalls.
These recalls represent about 84% of the 9,540 total recalls in our sample frame.
For the 1,728 firm-years, we collected product approvals from the FDA database.
We compiled data on patents from the USPTO, and financial data, consisting of revenue, profitability, R&D investment, and other control variables, from the WRDS database.
For the 108 firms, we compiled data from the FDA and the USPTO for an additional 10 years, 1990–1999, to get accumulated recalls and accumulated patents.
<!-- para 9 -->
We classified recalls based on the text of the description for each recall using a combination of manual and automatic text analysis, as described in Online Appendix C.
This resulted in 33% process-related and 67% design-related recalls in medical devices and 59% process-related and 41% design-related recalls in pharmaceuticals.
Cross-validating our classification with the self-classification by firms, which is available for a subset of recall announcements, resulted in a 95% match.
<!-- para 12 -->
Subsequent Recalls measures the total number of recalls in a firm-year.
Following extant research (Thirumalai and Sinha 2011), we use this variable to assess organizational learning from past recall experience.
We divide Subsequent Recalls into Subsequent Process-related Recalls and Subsequent Design-related Recalls in a firm-year.
<!-- para 14 -->
Accumulated Recalls of a firm for a specific year t is the cumulative count of recalls issued by the firm until the prior year $(t - 1)$.
To achieve homogeneity and consistency of this measure, we accumulate recall counts over an equal number of years, 10, for each year in the main models.
To illustrate, the Accumulated Recalls for a firm for the year 2016 is the sum of all recalls issued by the firm from 2006 to 2015.
We use time periods other than 10 years for Accumulated Recalls for a post hoc test.
We also divide Accumulated Recalls into Accumulated Process-related Recalls and Accumulated Design-related Recalls.
Each of these three variables is the primary predictor for learning from overall recalls, which is the base model based on extant research, and for learning from process-related and design-related recalls (Hypotheses 1a and 1b).
Accumulated Patents measures the stock of innovations that a firm has accumulated prior to a specific year t starting from the year $(t - 10)$.
By accumulating prior patent counts for 10 years, we also achieve congruence with our other count variables of accumulated recalls, which is helpful as we estimate interactions of pairs of independent variables, Accumulated Patents and Accumulated Recalls, for testing Hypothesis 2, and interactions with subcategories of Accumulated Process-related Recalls and Accumulated Design-related Recalls for Hypotheses 2a and 2b.
Lagged R&D Intensity for year t is measured using R&D investment as a percentage of net revenue of the firm for the preceding year $(t - 1)$.
Lagged R&D Intensity represents firms' strategic decisions to invest in R&D and serves as the moderating variable for Hypotheses 3a and 3b.
<!-- para 16 -->
Lagged Revenue measures the prior year's net revenue of a firm and is used to control for firm size.
Firm size has been shown to influence learning and response to failures (Attewell 1992).
We also control for profitability, as it can change firms' attention toward failures and their efforts toward learning (Greve 2003).
Lagged Profitability measures a firm's one-year lagged net operational profitability as a percentage of net revenue.
Lagged Product Approvals measures a firm's one-year lagged total number of new products as reported in the FDA database of product approvals (FDA 2019).
As aggregate recalls vary across firms, industries, and time, we control for fixed effects of all three.
Industry identifies whether each firm is in pharmaceutical or medical devices.
<!-- para 17 -->
Although there are some instances of firms operating in both industries, we only count each firm in its dominant industry, partly because the industry-specific financial data for firms operating in both industries is not available.
For example, Abbott has products in both medical devices and pharmaceutical industries.
However, most of their products and recalls are in the medical device industry, and they are registered as a medical device firm.
Therefore, we include Abbott Inc. in the medical device firms.
For similar reasons, we count Johnson and Johnson in the pharmaceutical industry.
As we are not interested in industry-wise comparisons, rather, we are interested in learning from past failure experience, we believe that this measurement, although not entirely perfect, would not significantly muddle the effects of interest.
Firm identifies each firm and is used to control for firm fixed effects.
Year is used to control for year fixed effects, accounting for variations in economic conditions that may influence firm efforts and outcomes related to learning.

Our analysis of learning from recalls and the enhancing effects of innovation capabilities consists of three progressive steps.
In Step 1, we include the effects of the control variables, lagged revenue, lagged profitability, lagged product approvals, and industry, on subsequent recalls.
We include the explanatory variables, accumulated recalls, accumulated patents, and lagged R&D intensity in Step 2 to assess the main effect of learning from accumulated recalls.
Although the main effect of learning from accumulated recalls is not a hypothesized relationship in our research, this step replicates the relationship analyzed in previous studies (Haunschild and Rhee 2004, Thirumalai and Sinha 2011).
We estimate similar models (not stated explicitly to avoid repetition) with subsequent process-related recalls and subsequent design-related recalls as dependent variables to test for Hypotheses 1a and 1b.
In Step 3, we test the moderation effects posited in Hypotheses 2 and 3 by incorporating relevant interaction terms.
The complete model is shown here, with subscripts for i representing firm, j representing industry, and t representing year.
<!-- para 9 -->
$$\text{Subsequent Recalls}_{ijt}=\beta_{0}+\beta_{1}\text{Lagged Revenue}_{ij(t-1)}+\beta_{2}\text{Lagged Profitability}_{ij(t-1)}+\beta_{3}\text{Lagged Product Approvals}_{ij(t-1)}+\beta_{4}(\text{Accumulated Recalls})_{ij(t-10\leq k<t)}+\beta_{5}(\text{Accumulated Patents})_{ij(t-10\leq k<t)}+\beta_{6}(\text{Lagged R\&D Intensity})_{ij(t-1)}+\beta_{45}(\text{Accumulated Recalls})_{ij(t-10\leq k<t)}\times(\text{Accumulated Patents})_{ij(t-10\leq k<t)}+\beta_{46}(\text{Accumulated Recalls})_{ij(t-10\leq k<t)}\times(\text{Lagged R\&D Intensity})_{ij(t-1)}+\text{Industry}_{j}+\text{Firm}_{i}+\text{Year}_{t}+\epsilon_{ijt}$$
<!-- para 10 -->
We estimate negative binomial generalized linear models (NB-GLMs) because our dependent variable in each case is recall count for a firm-year.
Although count data can also be modeled using Poisson models, they require the mean and the dispersion (variance) to be equal.
In our data, the distribution of the three count dependent variables, subsequent recalls, subsequent process-related recalls, and subsequent design-related recalls, are overdispersed.
Negative binomial models are well suited for such overdispersed data.
We check for serial correlations in our models using the Breusch-Godfrey test and the Durbin-Watson test (Cameron and Trivedi 2005).
The results are significant (p < 0.001), indicating that serial correlation is present due to unobserved firm-level and year-level heterogeneity beyond what is explicitly accounted for in the independent variables and the control variables in the models.
Therefore, we perform all model estimations with correction for serial correlation using the tsglm() function from the tscount package in the R software platform.
Furthermore, based on a significant result for a Hausman test (chi-square statistic: 143.28, p < 0.01), rejecting the null hypothesis that both fixed effects and random effects are consistent, we used fixed effects of firms in the models.
In the subsections that follow immediately, we present the results of our hypotheses tests for the main models.
Subsequently, we describe the results of supplementary analyses to test the robustness of our results and a post hoc analysis to assess the persistence of learning from recalls over time.

## discussion
<!-- para 2 -->
Our objectives in this research were to differentiate between two types of product failures attributed to slip-ups and knowledge gaps, study organizational learning from each type, and study the impacts of firms' innovation capabilities on learning from failures.
We based the relationships in our models on organizational learning and absorptive capacity theories.
Organizational learning theory provides arguments for failures serving as prompts, bringing attention, and providing direction related to occurrences and causes of failures, and resulting in efforts to fix current and anticipated problems.
Absorptive capacity theory provides the perspective of product failures being a source of information, for the absorption of which an existing capability of utilizing information for innovation is beneficial.
Overall, results of our analyses indicate that firms learn from failures to reduce subsequent failures, and this learning and consequent reduction in subsequent failures is greater for firms that have stronger innovation capabilities.
