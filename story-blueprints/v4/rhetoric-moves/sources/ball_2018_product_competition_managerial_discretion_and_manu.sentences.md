# ball_2018_product_competition_managerial_discretion_and_manu 句子库存

## introduction
<!-- para 2 -->
A fundamental principle of capitalism rests on the widely held notion that competition is predominately good, resulting in lower prices and higher quality (Friedman, 2009; Mazzeo, 2003).
Economic theory, however, suggests a more nuanced view.
While competition typically leads to lower prices, the relationship between competition and quality often depends on whether the price is regulated or set by firms (Gaynor, 2006).
When the price is regulated, the competition-quality relationship is clear: competition improves quality (Gaynor and Town, 2011; Gaynor, 2006).
However, in settings where prices are set by firms, the impact on quality is not as clear (Matsa, 2011; Gaynor, 2006; Jin, 2005).
Kamien and Vincent (1991) as well as Ma and Burgess (1993), for instance, showed that if prices are set by firms, increased competition results in lower quality while Allard et al. (2005) and Dranove and Satterthwaite (1992) found the opposite result.
Additionally, policy-makers in certain industries have leveraged regulations to increase competition with the goal of lowering prices for consumers, but the effect of such regulations on product quality remains uncertain.
<!-- para 3 -->
The Drug Price Competition and Patent Term Restoration Act of 1984 is a prime example of such competition-inducing regulation.
Commonly called the Hatch-Waxman Act, this legislation was intended to increase product competition in the pharmaceutical industry and lower drug prices by creating an expedited approval process for generic drugs.
Before this Act, every drug went through a long and an expensive New Drug Application (NDA) process that required extensive clinical tests and trials.
This Act introduced a second, expedited drug approval process: the Abbreviated New Drug Application (ANDA) process.
The ANDA process only requires firms to demonstrate evidence of a drug's bioequivalence (comparable in "dosage form, strength, route of administration, quality, performance characteristics, and intended use" - FDA, 2017a) to an original, pioneer drug rather than conduct lengthy clinical tests and trials themselves.
In other words, a drug is eligible for ANDA approval if there is an original version of the drug already on the market to which it can be compared.
<!-- para 4 -->
The ANDA process was designed to spur product competition in the pharmaceutical industry by making lower priced drugs available to consumers, while still maintaining high product quality standards.
As intended, the ANDA process has led to a considerable increase of bioequivalent drugs entering the market and in reduced drug prices (FDA, 2015).
Because drugs approved via the ANDA process need to be bioequivalent to pioneer drugs, firms are not allowed to change the design of the product (FDA, 2017a).
However, firms do have considerable leeway concerning manufacturing decisions as long as they abide by Current Good Manufacturing Practices (CGMP), which are broad guidelines dictating how work is done, not necessarily what work is done (AAM, 2013).
According to the Food and Drug Administration (FDA), CGMP regulations provide “minimum requirements for the methods, facilities and controls used in manufacturing, processing and packaging of a product” (FDA, 2014).
For example, CGMP dictates that suppliers are to be audited regularly, but not which suppliers to use, where they are located, or how many suppliers are included in the supply chain.
Additionally, CGMP requires firms document that they have trained their employees, but not what training techniques to use.
Decisions such as these are left to the purview of firm management, may be influenced by the level of product competition a firm faces, and may have product quality implications.

<!-- para 7 -->
In fact, evidence suggests that the Hatch-Waxman Act has fulfilled its intent to increase product competition through an increase in bioequivalent drugs, resulting in lower drug prices for consumers (Fig. 1).
However, the impact of this increased product competition on product quality, specifically product recalls, has to the best of our knowledge not been examined.
While lower drug prices are critical to consumer welfare, lower product quality can be very harmful.
Thus, one of the primary objectives of this study is to examine whether product competition influences product quality.
Because the ANDA process was created to increase product competition, we investigate how an increase in ANDA drugs as a proportion of all NDA and ANDA drugs marketed by the firm, influences future manufacturing-related product recalls.
<!-- para 8 -->
Our second objective is to examine if the product competition-quality relationship is contingent on contextual factors, as suggested in past research (Yayla-Küllü et al., 2013; Banker et al., 1998; Karmarkar and Pitbladdo, 1997).
Specifically, we examine if the relationship between product competition and manufacturing-related recalls is affected by managerial discretion (Hambrick and Finkelstein, 1987).
Managerial discretion surrounding pharmaceutical recalls varies greatly and is inversely related to the severity of the recall – that is, high severity recalls have less managerial discretion as consumer lives are often at stake and the need for a recall is more apparent, while low severity recalls have more managerial discretion, as there is, by definition, no health risk to consumers (Ball et al., 2017). [^1] We argue that high severity, low discretion recalls represent more objective product quality issues whereas low severity, high discretion recalls represent more subjective product quality issues and thus provides an ideal means to determine if the relationship between product competition and product recalls changes with managerial discretion.
<!-- para 9 -->
We test these relationships with 939 pharmaceutical recalls across 64 firms from 2002 to 2014, using fixed effects and random effects negative binomial panel models.
Our results show that firms that face more product competition have significantly more manufacturing-related recalls.
In other words, high product competition is associated with lower quality manufactured products.
Our results also suggest that the product competition-recalls relationship is contingent on managerial discretion.
Specifically, product competition is positively associated with high severity, low discretion manufacturing-related recalls while it is negatively associated with low severity, high discretion manufacturing-related recalls.
We also conduct robustness checks to substantiate these results, and to explore the mechanism underlying the relationship between product competition and manufacturing-related recalls.
In doing so, we find that compliance with CGMP guidelines acts as a mediator in our model, which indicates that high product competition may encourage firms to relax their attentiveness to manufacturing quality standards, which in turn may lead to an increase in manufacturing-related recalls.

## theory
<!-- para 3 -->
A significant amount of research has found that when competition intensifies, firms modify their actions to improve performance (Lumpkin and Dess, 1995; Vilcassim et al., 1999).
Most of the research on how competition changes a firm's actions has been analytical in nature (Banker et al., 1998; Kranton, 2003; Lenox et al., 2006), has focused on industry rather than product competition at the firm-level (Ferrier, 2001; Dranove et al., 1992), or has been conducted in service rather than manufacturing industries (Bloom et al., 2015; Berry and Waldfogel, 2010; Mazzeo, 2003).
We briefly review relevant literature below and provide a detailed summary of these studies in Table 1.

<!-- para 6 -->
From an analytical perspective, Gans (2002) found that quality increases with more competitors in the market.
Other scholars that examine the competition-quality relationship analytically found that the relationship is contingent on various factors.
For example, Banker et al. (1998) modeled three competitive settings to investigate how product quality performance relates to industry competition and found that it depends on how competition is interpreted as well as the industry's cost and demand structure, but in the most general case, competition leads to higher quality.
Karmarkar and Pitbladdo (1997) found that highly competitive markets are associated with increases in both conformance quality and quality costs.
Similarly, Yayla-Külli et al. (2013) discovered that the relationship between competition and product offerings may be contingent on the cost-to-quality ratio.
<!-- para 7 -->
The majority of empirical work linking competition and quality has been conducted in service industries where quality measures vary greatly.
Specifically, the airline industry measures quality using on-time performance (Mazzeo, 2003), the restaurant industry measures quality via consumer ratings (Berry and Waldfogel, 2010), and the banking sector measures quality as the number of branches (Cohen and Mazzeo, 2004).
Additionally, healthcare studies often operationalize quality as the number of services provided (Joskow, 1980) or mortality rates (Bloom et al., 2015; Propper et al., 2008; Propper et al., 2004).
Collectively, these studies show inconclusive results as competition is found to both enhance and hinder service quality.
There is limited empirical work that explores the effect of product competition on product quality in manufacturing (Olivares and Cachon, 2009), and none that we are aware of in the pharmaceutical industry (Table 1).
We therefore contribute to this literature stream by being the first to empirically examine how product competition influences product recalls, postulating that in the pharmaceutical industry, product competition is associated with more manufacturing-related recalls.
<!-- para 9 -->
This study rests on the premise that NDA drugs face less competition than ANDA drugs.
This is reasonable because at the time of product approval, NDA drugs have no bioequivalent products on the market (FDA, 2016).
In the absence of direct competition, NDA products allow firms to charge a price premium, reducing the incentive to cut costs and focus on manufacturing efficiency (Farber et al., 2009).
Further, NDA product competition is restricted until the patent of the drug expires, which is generally 20 years after approval (Brennan, 2016).
Once ANDA drugs begin to enter the marketplace, firms often withdraw their original NDA product because they simply cannot compete with the numerous, less expensive ANDA alternatives that quickly flood the market (Morton and Boller, 2017; FDA, 2015).
In contrast, ANDA drugs must have a bioequivalent product in the market, which implies that there are similar drugs that directly and closely compete with ANDA drugs from the moment they enter the market.
This is precisely the reason why the ANDA product approval method was developed: to enhance product competition in the marketplace.
Thus, the more ANDA products a firm manufactures as a proportion of their product portfolio, the greater product competition the firm faces (Horner, 2002; Martin, 2015).
<!-- para 10 -->
Product competition of this sort is likely to influence the culture that guides how the firm manufactures their products.
As firms cannot compete via the design of ANDA products, competing on manufacturing is one of the few options in this setting.
Further, literature indicates that firms establish holistic manufacturing and product quality strategies that affect their broader product portfolio, as opposed to individualized, product-specific manufacturing and product quality strategies (Wowak et al., 2015; Flynn et al., 1994).
This perspective is shared by the FDA, and is reflected by how the FDA enforces pharmaceutical product quality standards.
The FDA routinely conducts audits that examine the entire manufacturing and quality control management system to measure the product quality risk levels for all products originating from a facility or firm (Anand et al., 2012; Gray et al., 2011).
Therefore, it is reasonable to assume that a firm's response to heightened product competition may influence product quality outcomes for all products the firm produces.
<!-- para 11 -->
One potential response to heightened product competition may be to manufacture higher quality products.
However, we believe that this is less likely in our setting for two reasons.
First, literature indicates that one of the prerequisites for a firm to compete on quality is the consumer's ability to distinguish quality differences among products (Martin, 2015).
Research has shown that in the pharmaceutical industry, all products, and particularly NDA and ANDA comparables, are perceived as having equivalent quality by consumers, providing little incentive for pharmaceutical companies to compete on quality (Woodcock and Wosinska, 2013).
Second, a strong emphasis on cost-competition in this setting (Walker, 2017), coupled with the knowledge that high-quality is costly in the short-term (Nair, 2006; Sousa and Voss, 2002; Hendricks and Singhal, 2001), makes it a suboptimal strategy for a firm to manufacture high-quality products and simultaneously compete on cost.
We therefore posit that instead of leading to higher quality products, product competition will be associated with lower quality products and hence an increase in manufacturing-related recalls in the pharmaceutical industry in two alternative ways; one purposeful and intentional, the other inadvertent and less deliberate.
<!-- para 12 -->
To become more competitive, firms may intentionally seek to shave costs out of their manufacturing and quality control processes by consciously reducing labor costs, hiring less experienced employees or lowering maintenance costs by servicing their manufacturing equipment less frequently (Lewis, 2017; Mulligan, 2017).
Firms could also lower material costs by purchasing lower cost ingredients from cheaper suppliers (Lewis, 2017; Mulligan, 2017) or may find cheaper, alternative ways of making their products (Kirzner, 2015; Mazzeo, 2003; Joskow, 1980) by replacing expensive steps in the manufacturing process with low-cost substitutes and reducing non-value added activities such as quality assurance checkpoints (IMEC, 2017).
These activities may seem attractive as efficiency improving, cost-reduction efforts when product competition is high and firms are relying on their manufacturing processes to compete.
<!-- para 13 -->
Alternately, as opposed to consciously cutting corners in manufacturing and quality control, firms may simply relax their attentiveness to quality when product competition is high (Woodcock and Wosinska, 2013; Anand et al., 2012).
In fact, research indicates that in pharmaceutical manufacturing, attentiveness to quality can easily decay (Gray et al., 2015a; Argote, 2015; Anand et al., 2012) and such quality decay may be accelerated by “destabilizing forces in the environment” (Anand et al., 2012: pg 1704).
Literature in other contexts can be interpreted through this lens to suggest that high competition is a form of a destabilizing force that may accelerate inadvertent decay in quality systems (Beck et al., 2006; Park and Russo, 1996).
Whether the response to high product competition is a conscious choice to cut corners, or an inadvertent relaxation of attention to quality, either may lead to more manufacturing-related recalls.
Thus, we hypothesize:
<!-- para 14 -->
> [!hypothesis]
> Hypothesis 1.
Product competition is positively associated with manufacturing-related recalls.
<!-- para 16 -->
Next, we stipulate that the positive relationship between product competition and manufacturing-related recalls will be contingent upon the level of managerial discretion surrounding the recall decision, such that it will be stronger for low discretion recalls, but weaker for high discretion recalls.
To do so, we first explain how we view managerial discretion in the product recall decision, and then discuss how this may affect the product competition-recalls relationship.
<!-- para 17 -->
We use two examples of low discretion recalls and two examples of high discretion product problems that should have led to recalls, but did not, in explaining our conceptualization of managerial discretion as it pertains to recall severity.
First, we contend that recalls are low discretion when they are high severity.
For example, in 2008, Actavis Corporation initiated a recall of its Digitek drug after several customers complained of severe side effects including stroke and kidney failure.
Actavis admitted that these deadly side effects stemmed from a manufacturing error that resulted in an oversized heart medication, leading patients to consume more of the medication than was safe (Zevelloff, 2017).
Similarly, in 2013, Johnson & Johnson recalled their popular Risperdahl schizophrenia drug because mold, originating from a poorly maintained manufacturing facility, was discovered on the product, leading to potentially serious patient harm or death (Reuters, 2013).
Given the severity of the problems, managers of these firms had little discretion in making the recall decision.
We contend that such low discretion, high severity recalls are more objective manifestations of poor product quality, and because of this, if product competition does in fact lead to more manufacturing-related recalls as posited in Hypothesis 1, the relationship should be even stronger for low discretion, high severity recalls.
Thus, we hypothesize:
<!-- para 18 -->
> [!hypothesis]
> Hypothesis 2a.
Managerial discretion will moderate the positive association between product competition and manufacturing-related recalls such that the relationship will be stronger for low discretion, high severity recalls.
<!-- para 19 -->
Alternately, some product defects are less evident, making the recall decision more subjective and more discretionary.
Identifying actual examples of low severity, high discretion potential recalls that did not occur is more challenging, as these events are not in the public domain because the recalls did not actually occur.
As such, we spoke with a manufacturing manager at a large, publicly traded, FDA regulated firm.
The manager chronicled several product problem scenarios that did not result in recalls, although the manager admitted that according to FDA recall guidelines, the products should have been recalled.
We describe two such examples.
In one instance, a quality engineer at the plant noticed that the height of a regulatory symbol on the product packaging was printed smaller than the FDA minimum required height of 5 mm.
FDA regulations require that in such cases, the product in question be recalled and re-packaged.
Hence, the responsible quality engineer recommended a recall.
However, the manufacturing manager and the quality manager at the plant jointly decided to refrain from recalling the product due to the problem's low severity, even though it was in clear violation of FDA guidelines.
In another instance, there was a product labeling mismatch, where the product quantity listed on the outside of the box was inconsistent with the quantity printed on the product pouch inside the box.
Again, management opted to forgo the recall because the quantity printed on the outer box, which might be used by doctors, pharmacists, and patients to make decisions, was the correct quantity.
<!-- para 20 -->
While these problems may appear trivial and insufficiently serious to warrant a recall, the FDA clearly stipulates a recall in both of these situations (FDA, 2009).
In fact, nearly 20% of all pharmaceutical recalls are due to low severity mistakes (Dillon, 2017; Massey, 2016).
If the FDA instructs firms to recall in these cases, why would firm management not recall?
While there are many possible answers to this question, we contend that high product competition may provide one possible explanation.
<!-- para 21 -->
Recalls often result in substantial tangible and intangible repercussions including lower market share, lost sales, lower stock price, and tarnished firm reputation (Nagaich and Sadhna, 2015; Rockoff, 2010; Berenson, 2007; Kirmani and Rao, 2000; Davidson and Worrell, 1992; Jarrell and Peltzman, 1985).
More critically, however, research indicates that this is worse under high competition because competition magnifies negative performance consequences (Horner, 2002).
In other words, firms suffer greater penalties for mistakes when they operate in highly competitive environments (Horner, 2002; Karmarkar and Pitbladdo, 1997).
Thus, for low severity problems, managers may be tempted to look the other way and not announce a recall, because in such cases it is a viable option, and it allows managers to avoid the competition-inducing harsher penalties associated with a recall.
As such, we hypothesize:
<!-- para 22 -->
> [!hypothesis]
> Hypothesis 2b.
Managerial discretion will moderate the positive association between product competition and manufacturing-related recalls such that the relationship will be weaker for high discretion, low severity recalls.

## methods
<!-- para 3 -->
We selected the pharmaceutical industry as our setting for three reasons.
First, the Hatch-Waxman Act in general, and the ANDA process in particular, was specifically designed to increase product competition in the pharmaceutical industry, thus creating a unique and ideal setting to answer our research questions.
Second, pharmaceutical firms are held to similar quality standards set by the FDA, helping to reduce spurious sources of variation across firms.
Third, product recalls are critical in this sector as the repercussions of low-quality products can cause customer harm and even death.
<!-- para 5 -->
To test our hypotheses, we use the count of manufacturing-related pharmaceutical recalls as the dependent variable.
There are two primary categories of product recalls in the pharmaceutical industry: manufacturing-related and design-related.
Design-related recalls are due to a faulty drug design.
For example, in 2001, Bayer recalled their Baycol cholesterol medication as it led to a severe muscle disorder for thousands of patients that took the drug.
This problem was not related to how the drug was manufactured, but instead stemmed from a design flaw (Pozniak, 2001).
While ANDA drugs can be recalled due to design issues, we contend it is unlikely these recalls are influenced by product competition, as firms cannot meaningfully change the drug's design from the original, pioneer drug.
The FDA, however, provides considerable flexibility as it pertains to the drug manufacturing process for both NDA and ANDA products.
While the FDA requires all drug manufacturers to adhere to CGMPs (AAM, 2013), firms have leeway with numerous manufacturing-related decisions such as employee hiring and training, facility and equipment maintenance, levels of automation, choice of suppliers, and number of suppliers, as just a few examples.
Because the design of ANDA products is unalterable, the manufacturing process is an intuitive place for firms to compete, and this is why we use manufacturing-related recalls as our primary dependent variable.
<!-- para 6 -->
We obtained a list of all recalls from the FDA through Freedom of Information Act (FOIA) requests, where each recall was identified as either manufacturing-related or design-related.
Manufacturing-related recalls were initiated due to anything outside the design of the drug.
This includes all aspects of the supply chain such as supplier, distribution, labeling, or production line-related defects, all of which could be affected by the amount of product competition a firm faces.
For completeness, and to thoroughly explore our hypothesized relationships, we use design-related recalls as a dependent variable in robustness checks.
The FDA also classifies recalls based on their severity.
Class 1 and Class 2 recalls are the most severe as they have a significant risk of leading to customer death or medically reversible harm; thus managers have less discretion in making the recall decision.
Class 3 recalls are not as severe, as by definition they cannot harm consumers, and have high managerial discretion (FDA, 2009).

Our data contains 939 recalls, which include 56 Class 1, 516 Class 2, and 367 Class 3 recalls.
Table 2 displays the count of recalls by severity and by source.
The unit of analysis in our study is the firm-year because the FDA reports both new product details and recalls at the firm level.
Our main recall measure is the total number of manufacturing-related recalls by a firm for the year following the year of the independent and control variable measures.
In other words, the dependent variable is a count measure that leads all other variables by one year.
The FDA could only provide recalls grouped into manufacturing-related and non-manufacturing-related recalls from 2002 to 2014.
We therefore end the panel in 2013, and use recall counts in 2014 as the lead dependent variable for the 2013 panel year.
In our analysis, we include only voluntary recalls for two reasons.
First, there were an insignificant number of involuntary recalls to analyze separately; our panel only has 62 involuntary recalls, compared to 939 voluntary recalls.
Second, by their very nature, involuntary recalls contain no discretion, as they are mandated by the FDA; hence our exploration of the influence of discretion on recalls led us to exclude involuntary recalls. [^2] As described below, the final sample includes firms that were publicly traded with data available in Compustat and had FDA Orange Book submission data available between 2002 and 2013.
<!-- para 11 -->
The independent variable in our study is product competition, which was computed with data obtained via numerous FOIAs to the FDA.
The measures for the control variables were obtained from Compustat via Wharton Data Research Services database.
We explain how each variable is measured below.
<!-- para 12 -->
Product Competition: We measure the ratio of ANDA products actively marketed by a firm in each year, divided by the total number of NDA and ANDA products marketed by the firm, as our product competition measure.
If this ratio is zero, the firm markets only NDA products that year.
Alternately, if it is one, the firm only markets ANDA products and faces intense competition across its portfolio of products.
<!-- para 13 -->
To create this novel product competition measure, we used data from the FDA's Orange Book.
The Orange Book has been published annually since the Hatch-Waxman Act was passed, with the first version published in 1984.
The annual FDA Orange Book contains all approved pharmaceutical products for sale in the U.S. categorized by whether the drug gained approval using the NDA or ANDA approval pathway.
Each drug is also categorized as to whether or not it is actively marketed by the firm, or if it has been discontinued.
However, if a drug is discontinued, the FDA does not stipulate the year in which the drug moved to the discontinued status in the most recent Orange Book, which is the only one publicly available online. [^3]  In order to determine the exact year a drug was discontinued, which is necessary to obtain a precise measure of product competition for each firm-year, we needed to acquire every Orange Book published since 1984.
We were able to obtain 30 years of Orange Books (1984–2013) through multiple FOIAs to the FDA.
We used the following Orange Book data from each year in this study: (1) the year in which each drug was launched; (2) the firm gaining drug approval; (3) the type of approval (NDA or ANDA); (4) the category of the drug (prescription or over-the-counter); (5) if the drug was discontinued; and (6) the year a drug was discontinued.
This Orange Book data allowed us to precisely measure the total number of NDA and ANDA products actively marketed by each firm, in each year.
<!-- para 14 -->
Our product competition measure is the centered and standardized ratio of the total number of ANDA products marketed by a firm each year divided by the sum of all NDA and ANDA products marketed by that firm in that year.
We chose ANDA as the numerator of the ratio because the higher the number of ANDA products marketed each year, the greater overall product competition a firm faces across its entire product portfolio.
The average product age for NDA products in our study is 15.8 years, the median is 16.0 years, and the $90^{\text{th}}$ percentile is 18.8 years.
This summary data indicates that the vast majority of NDA drugs in our data fall within the patent protection window of 20 years.
This patent protection window prohibits competitors from gaining approval on similar products under the ANDA designation until the drug has been on the market for 20 years (FDA, 2017a).
Hence, within our dataset, NDA products face very limited competition while ANDA products face considerable competition from either the original NDA drug, or the numerous, similar ANDA products in the market.
<!-- para 15 -->
There were 64 publicly traded firms with data available in Compustat and with product submission data in the Orange Book during our panel.
Of those 64, 19 firms had only NDA approvals in the entire time of our panel, 6 had only ANDA approvals, and 39 had both NDA and ANDA approvals (Table A1 in the appendix).
Firms in the study had an average of 40.4 ANDA and 53.6 NDA products on the market in any given year.
<!-- para 17 -->
To measure the varying levels of discretion that exists in pharmaceutical recall decisions for Hypotheses 2a and 2b, we differentiate our dependent variable according to recall classes provided by the FDA.
According to the FDA, Class 1 and 2 recalls have the potential to create serious or medically reversible customer harm and thus have little managerial discretion, while Class 3 recalls have no such potential (FDA, 2009) and thus have significant managerial discretion.
Consistent with this perspective, we use all manufacturing-related recalls to examine Hypotheses 1, Class 1 and 2 recalls to examine Hypothesis 2a, and Class 3 recalls to examine Hypothesis 2b.
<!-- para 19 -->
Recall Experience.
Future recalls may be associated with past recalls and thus similar to prior recall research (Haunschild and Rhee, 2004), we control for the natural log of the number of recalls in the prior year by each firm.
To increase the precision of our analysis, we align the Recall Experience measure with the appropriate dependent variable being explored in each model.
More specifically, when our dependent variable is all manufacturing-related recalls, the Recall Experience measure corresponds to all manufacturing-related recalls.
When we test for managerial discretion and restrict the dependent variable to high or low severity recalls, the Recall Experience measure is similarly restricted to high or low severity recalls as well.

<!-- para 24 -->
Market Share.
It is possible that the market share of the firm may influence their manufacturing-related recall counts (Rhee and Haunschild, 2006).
We measure the relative size of the firm's sales in comparison to other firms by creating a market share measure, which is the natural log of the total sales of each firm, in a given year, divided by the sales for all the other firms in their four digit SIC code.
<!-- para 25 -->
R&D Intensity.
We control for the level of R&D intensity at the firm, which has been shown to effect the number of recalls (Thirumalai and Sinha, 2011).
We measure R&D intensity as the ratio of R&D expenses to firm sales.
<!-- para 26 -->
Return on Assets.
It is possible that cost cutting in manufacturing could manifest itself through a more efficient return on assets (ROA).
To control for this possibility, we use the natural log of the firm's ROA.
This measures the efficiency by which the firm captures sales from each dollar of asset investment, which is consistent with past recall research (Wowak et al., 2015).
<!-- para 27 -->
Firm Age.
We control for the experience of the firm with the natural log of the number of years that the firm has been in business (Wowak et al., 2015).
We gathered the founding date of each firm using public sources, such as the firm's website, and subtracted it from each year of the panel to create a firm age measure.
<!-- para 28 -->
Number of Products.
A firm that has more products on the market has a greater chance for product recalls (Thirumalai and Sinha, 2011).
To control for the total number of products that a firm marketed in each year of the panel, we used data from the FDA's Orange Book.
Specifically, we include the natural log of the total number of NDA and ANDA drugs actively marketed by the firm.
On average for each firm-year, a firm marketed 94 products (54 NDA and 40 ANDA).
<!-- para 29 -->
Percent Rx.
Product complexity may be associated with the propensity for a recall (Thirumalai and Sinha, 2011).
For example, it is possible that firms that market more complex prescription drugs (Rx) will be more likely to experience a recall than firms that market less complex over-the-counter drugs (OTC).
To control for this possibility, we measured the percent of products that are Rx, divided by the sum of Rx and OTC products, for each firm-year observation, as obtained from the FDA's Orange Book.
<!-- para 30 -->
Product Age.
Recall research has found that new products are more susceptible to recalls than older products (Shah et al., 2017); the same may be true in our case.
We control for this possibility by taking the natural log of the average age of each product marketed by the firm in a given year.
The starting year for each product was the year of approval from the FDA's Orange Book, and the ending year was the year of the observation.
The average age over all products in the study is 16.8 years (15.8 years for NDA and 17.7 years for ANDA).
<!-- para 31 -->
Year.
FDA policies, which may impact the likelihood of a recall, can change over time (Ball et al., 2017).
To control for this possibility, we use an indicator variable for each year of the panel and treat 2013 as the reference category.
Year fixed effects are included, but not shown in all regression tables. [^5]

## results
<!-- para 2 -->
Our dependent variable is an over-dispersed count variable.
Consistent with other studies performing product recall panel data analyses (Thirumalai and Sinha, 2011; Haunschild and Rhee, 2004), we use a negative binomial panel model to test our hypotheses.
Negative binomial panel models can incorporate both fixed effects (FE) and random effects (RE) in the regression equation.
The benefit of the FE approach is that the regression equation de-means the data to correct for endogeneity driven by omitted variable bias attributable to time-invariant unobservables.
However, a downside of this specification is that any firm that does not vary in the dependent variable, or in the product competition measure across the 12-year period, is mathematically excluded from the analysis.
For example, if a firm only markets NDA (or ANDA) products from 2002 to 2013, it will be dropped from the FE model.
Hence, beta coefficients of an FE model reflect within-firm effects and demonstrate how changes within an individual firm influence changes in future recall counts for that firm.
While this is interesting and informative, we also include an RE model that pools within- and across-firm effects that does not de-mean the data, nor drop any observations (Greene, 2012).
We therefore provide both FE and RE negative binomial panel model results to test our hypotheses.
<!-- para 3 -->
Descriptive statistics and correlations are provided in Table 3.
Recall Experience, Market Share, ROA, Firm Age, and Number of Products have a positive correlation with recalls, while others such as R&D Intensity and Percent Rx, have a negative correlation.
The results of the FE and RE regression analyses are provided in Table 4 (columns 1–4: FE models, columns 5–7: RE models).
We first incorporate control variables (column 1) and find that Recall Experience and Sales per Employee are positively associated with recalls while Product Age is negatively associated with recalls.
We then enter Product Competition (column 2) and find that it is positively associated with all manufacturing-related recalls ( $\beta = 1.57$ , p < 0.05), providing support for Hypothesis 1.
<!-- para 4 -->
In columns 3 and 4 of Table 4, we test Hypotheses 2a and 2b by dividing our dependent variable into the sum of Class 1 and Class 2 recalls (column 3) and Class 3 recalls (column 4).
For Hypothesis 2a, we predicted that the positive relationship between product competition and manufacturing-related recalls would be stronger for low discretion, high severity Class 1 and 2 recalls (column 3 of Table 4).
This is evident as $\beta = 3.23$ (p < 0.01) from column 3 of Table 4 is statistically different and larger than $\beta = 1.57$ (p < 0.05) from column 2 of Table 4,[^6] providing support for Hypothesis 2a.
For Hypothesis 2b, we predicted that the positive relationship between product competition and manufacturing-related recalls would be weaker for high discretion, low severity Class 3 recalls.
In fact, what we observe is that the positive relationship is weakened to the point of becoming a negative relationship, but still retaining statistical significance.
In other words, product competition is negatively associated with high discretion, low severity Class 3 recalls ($\beta = -2.82$, p < 0.05; Table 4, column 4), providing support for Hypothesis 2b.

We then retested these relationships using an RE model, which includes all observations and tests both within- and across-firm effects in the same regression (Greene, 2012).
The results (Table 4, columns 5 through 7) are essentially the same as the FE model presented above.
The beta coefficient for Product Competition using all manufacturing-related recalls as the dependent variable, remains positive and significant ($\beta = 0.17$, p < 0.05; Table 4, column 5).
The coefficient is significantly larger when only Class 1 and 2 manufacturing-related recalls are used as the dependent variable ($\beta = 0.23$, p < 0.05; Table 4, column 6)[^7] and it once again becomes negative and significant when the dependent variable is only high discretion, low severity recalls ($\beta = -0.32$, p < 0.05; Table 4, column 7).
<!-- para 9 -->
To provide further insight into our results, we interpret the beta coefficients from the RE model, as that model is inclusive of all firms in the study and does not drop any observations.
Beta coefficients for negative binomial models are interpreted multiplicatively after exponentiation (Wooldridge, 2015).
Thus, the positive beta coefficient for product competition in column 6 can be interpreted as the following: a one standard deviation increase in Product Competition is associated with a $26\%$ increase in Class 1 and 2 manufacturing-related recalls in a firm-year, which corresponds to approximately three additional Class 1 and 2 manufacturing-related recalls per firm across the 12-year panel. [^8] Similarly, we can interpret the negative coefficient in column 7 as a one standard deviation increase in Product Competition is associated with a $27\%$ reduction, or approximately two fewer Class 3 manufacturing-related recalls across the panel. [^9] Collectively, using both FE and RE models, these results indicate robust support for Hypotheses 1, 2a, and 2b.
More precisely, the relationships persist when analyzing only within-firm effects in the FE model, or when pooling within- and across- firm effects in the RE model.
<!-- para 11 -->
We conduct four additional analyses to more comprehensively explore our hypothesized relationships.
These include a mediation test, a sample selection test, a regression using design-related recalls as the dependent variable, and a reverse causality test.
First, we delve deeper into the mechanisms that may underlie the product competition-recalls relationship.
In Hypothesis 1, we conjectured that firms experience more manufacturing-related recalls at high product competition because they may either consciously choose to cut corners in manufacturing, or may inadvertently allow their quality system to decay as product competition increases (Gray et al., 2015a, 2015b; Anand et al., 2012).
We use mediation tests to check which of these two alternative mechanisms provide a more plausible explanation.
Three things are generally required to demonstrate mediation: (1) the explanatory variable predicts the mediator; (2) the mediator predicts the dependent variable; and (3) the direct effect of the explanatory variable is significantly reduced when the mediator is included into the model with the explanatory variable (Baron and Kenny, 1986).
<!-- para 12 -->
We first examine the conscious cost-cutting argument.
If the firm is proactively cutting corners in manufacturing as a result of product competition, ROA is likely to mediate this relationship, as advertent cost-cutting should translate into greater return on assets (Burton et al., 2002).
However, this is not the case as ROA is not a significant predictor of manufacturing-related recalls (Table 4, column 1).
Additionally, in a separate analysis not included but available upon request, we repeated the analysis in Table 4, but replaced ROA with Net Income.
We did so because Net Income is a comparable measure to ROA, and is likely to increase as a firm shaves costs out of the manufacturing process (Modi and Mishra, 2011).
We again found no evidence of mediation as Net Income is also not a significant predictor of recalls.
Neither of these results meet the second criteria for mediation as explained above.
<!-- para 13 -->
Next, we explore the alternative mechanism and test whether decaying quality systems mediate the relationship between product competition and recalls.
Following previous researchers, we consider FDA inspection outcomes as valid and reliable proxies for firms' compliance with CGMP standards (Gray et al., 2015b; Anand et al., 2012).
More specifically, the FDA inspects pharmaceutical plants approximately every two years, and scores each plant on their level of quality compliance with generally accepted CGMPs (FDA, 2017b).
A high quality plant receives a "No Action Indicated" (NAI) score, while a poor quality plant receives an "Official Action Indicated" (OAI) score.
A "Voluntary Action Indicated" (VAI) score is given when less significant, voluntary improvement opportunities are identified during the inspection.
Past research indicates that OAI outcomes are the most severe and are most commonly associated with poor product quality (Ball et al., 2017; Anand et al., 2012).
If OAI inspection outcomes are found to mediate the relationship between product competition and manufacturing-related recalls, it would provide support for the notion that high product competition may lead firms to relax their attention to quality, which can have negative consequences in the form of manufacturing-related recalls.
<!-- para 14 -->
To test this alternative, we obtained all FDA plant inspection outcomes for each firm-year in our study through an additional FOIA to the FDA.
We normalized the OAI counts by dividing the number of OAI inspection outcomes at the firm by all inspections at the firm that year.
On average, a firm in our sample received 0.32 OAI's, 1.76 VAI's, and 2.29 NAI's per year.
The mean OAI ratio is 0.073 across the firm years in our sample, implying that $7.3\%$ of the inspections resulted in an OAI outcome. [^10] The minimum of this measure is 0.00, and the maximum is 1.00.
The mediation results using OAI ratio as the mediator are provided in Table 5.
First, we use the OAI ratio as the dependent variable, and find that Product Competition has a positive and significant relationship with it (column 1), suggesting that as product competition increases, a firm experiences more poor quality inspection outcomes.
In column 2, we provide the result using Product Competition to predict manufacturing-related recalls (repeated from Table 4, column 2).
Lastly, in column 3 we incorporate the OAI ratio into the full FE model and find that while the OAI ratio positively predicts manufacturing-related recalls, Product Competition is no longer significant at the p < 0.05 level.
Taken together, this provides evidence that poor FDA inspection outcomes mediate the relationship between product competition and manufacturing-related recalls and supports the notion that firms may inadvertently reduce their attentiveness to product quality as product competition increases, leading to an increase in manufacturing-related recalls.

Our next robustness check addresses possible sample selection bias that may confound our analysis.
To explore this possibility, we provide results of a propensity score matching model (Table 6).
These models assess treatment effects in the presence of possible confounding factors by matching firms on their propensity to receive a certain treatment, and then compares the effect of being treated on an outcome measure (Brown et al., 2013).
For a treatment, we use whether a firm ever marketed an ANDA product in a given firm-year or not.
In other words, if the product competition measure was greater than zero for a firm-year, this treatment measure was assigned a value of one, and a zero otherwise.
Of the 422 firm-year observations, there were 93 instances in which a firm's product competition measure was zero and 329 instances in which its product competition was greater than zero.
The results of propensity score matching (Table 6) show that the average treatment effect of ANDA products in a firm-year is positive and significant on all manufacturing-related recalls (column 1), and on Class 1 and 2 manufacturing-related recalls (column 2).
We also observe a negative average treatment effect when just Class 3 recalls are used as the outcome measure of the propensity score model (column 3), though the treatment effect is not significant at the p < 0.05 level.
These results help alleviate sample selection bias concerns related to the conclusions of our study.
<!-- para 25 -->
To ensure that our results are not contingent on our dependent variable, we test our hypotheses using design-related recalls as the dependent variable.
In describing our setting, we stated that firms are unable to modify the design of an ANDA product; hence, there should be no reason to expect a significant relationship between product competition and design-related recalls.
Because this is the first study to examine how ANDA products influence future recalls, this assumption is vital to test.
We replicate our main analysis and present results related to design-related recalls in Table 7, column 1.
As expected, Product Competition does not have a significant relationship with design-related recalls.
This provides support for our contention that competitive actions related to ANDA products in the pharmaceutical industry are focused on manufacturing-related choices, as firms are unable to modify the product design.
<!-- para 26 -->
In column 2 of Table 7, we report results of a regression analysis conducted to test reverse causality as it is possible that firms adjust their level of product competition after experiencing recalls.
We use the subsequent year's Product Competition as the dependent variable, and regress it upon all other measures, including all manufacturing-related recalls as the measure for Recall Experience.
We find no support for reverse causality as Recall Experience is not associated with future Product Competition.
<!-- para 27 -->
We also examined if resource slack may directly or indirectly influence manufacturing-related product recalls using three measures of slack: inventory, production, and administrative slack, which is consistent with Modi and Mishra (2011).
To test direct effects, we incorporated each of these slack measures into our models as main effects, and to examine indirect effects, we used their interaction terms with product competition.
We did not find any significant direct or indirect association between any of the slack measures and manufacturing-related recalls. [^11]  This non-significant result is consistent with the non-significant result related to ROA and Net Income and the mechanism identified in Table 5.
More precisely, previous research has shown slack to be negatively correlated with both ROA and Net Income (Modi and Mishra, 2011).
Thus, it is likely that the insignificant result for slack, and for ROA and Net Income, indicate an inattention to quality as demonstrated in Table 5, as opposed to the conscious cost-cutting mechanism.
