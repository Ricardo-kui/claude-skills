# ball_et_al_2022_product_recall_research_dimens 句子库存

## introduction
<!-- para 2 -->
A product recall is a repair, replacement, or removal of manufactured products already on the market.
Recalls occur across a variety of industries including automotive, medical device, pharmaceutical, food, cosmetics, and consumer products.
They are initiated when a manufacturer—or, in rare cases, a regulator—determines that a systemic, as opposed to a random, quality failure has occurred in products already sold and that these failures need to be remedied.
Whereas broader product quality management research has long-standing roots in operations management and has been explored for decades (Deming [12]), product recall research has more recently emerged as a separate and distinct domain of academic inquiry that crosses multiple academic disciplines.
In this tutorial, we examine recall research across its dimensions and methods, identify research that has had a significant impact on the field, and offer guidance on where we believe the field should go next.
<!-- para 3 -->
One particular aspect of recall research that we explore in this tutorial is the ethical dilemma that surrounds a recall decision and the temptations presented for profit-seeking firms and their managers.
On one hand, faulty products that need to be recalled may adversely impact customer health and safety.
On the other hand, most product recalls are voluntarily initiated; thus, there is a varying degree of discretion on behalf of firm leadership about if or when to initiate recalls, contingent on defect severity (Wowak et al. [31]).
Given that recalls are predominantly viewed as negative events for firms, firm leaders may be tempted to conceal product quality problems rather than initiate a recall to avoid the negative publicity, unwanted federal regulator attention, and financial penalties, even when there are public safety implications.
This juxtaposition of high-stakes incentives for firms, even well-meaning firms, creates an ideal context for a robust stream of behavioral research exploring recall decision-making biases.
<!-- para 4 -->
Regulating product recalls is serious business, yet the three recall regulators in the United States—the U.S. Food and Drug Administration (FDA), the National Highway Traffic Safety Administration (NHTSA), and the U.S. Consumer Product Safety Commission (CPSC)—are surprisingly disconnected from each other.
We conclude this tutorial with an overview of each recall regulator, their strengths and weaknesses, and how they may be able to enhance product safety and federal regulation by leveraging recall research implications.
Finally, as product recalls motivate a practical field of research, active engagement with recall regulators can create unique research partnership opportunities that may enrich studies and deepen insights.
Such engagement, however, is challenging, is time consuming, and entails significant risks.
Members of this author team have had the opportunity to closely collaborate twice with arguably one of the most important recall regulators in the United States, the FDA, using two different regulator collaboration mechanisms.
We juxtapose the benefits and risks of working closely with the FDA on these two approaches: federal grants and federal contracts.
<!-- para 6 -->
We discuss recall research dimensions and methods of published recall studies that are foundational in nature, highly cited, or more recent, rather than a comprehensive literature review covering all recall studies.
This approach allows us to provide more depth in our discussion.
We group studies based on three recall dimensions examined—namely, causes, decision making, and effects—and three methodological approaches used—namely, empirical data, analytical modeling, and behavioral experiments.
Partitioning studies in this way and presenting them in chronological order reveals interesting insights about how recall research has evolved over time.
When discussing next steps for research on each of these dimensions, we provide a brief illustrative anecdote of an actual product recall that we have had the opportunity to capture through our industry knowledge and collaboration.
The objective of these anecdotes is to provide practical context to recall research, reveal possible future research opportunities, and provide insights about how some of our own studies originated.
<!-- para 8 -->
One of the first empirical studies on recall causes, and one of the more highly cited recall studies, is Haunschild and Rhee [16].
This study laid the groundwork for empirical recall research investigating how firm volition related to past recalls may influence future recalls.
The authors examined 34 years of automotive recalls, distinguishing between those recalls that were initiated voluntarily (i.e., high on volition) and those mandated by NHTSA (i.e., low on volition).
Employing panel data with fixed effects negative binomial count models to predict recalls, the authors found that when a recall is initiated voluntarily, a firm has fewer future recalls, possibly as a result of the benefit of learning from volition in the recall decision.
In the medical device industry, Thirumalai and Sinha [29] studied a cross section of medical device manufacturers and found that proliferation of a firm's product portfolio is associated with a higher likelihood of recalls; however, past recall experience reduces future recall likelihood.
In the same vein, Steven et al. [27] extended Haunschild and Rhee [16] and Thirumalai and Sinha [29] by considering recall causes within a different sector: the consumer product industry.
Steven et al. [27] explored how sourcing strategies by publicly traded consumer product firms may influence future recalls.
The authors found that, among other things, simultaneously outsourcing and offshoring is more likely to result in product defects that need to be recalled compared with outsourcing or offshoring independently or sequentially.
<!-- para 9 -->
One of the first studies to move from operational to corporate governance causes of recalls was by Wowak et al. [30].
Wowak and colleagues investigated whether and how chief executive officer (CEO) compensation—base pay versus stock options—influences how likely a firm is to experience a product recall.
Using a generalized estimated equation regression model, they found that option-heavy pay packages incentivize CEOs to take greater product safety risks when designing and manufacturing products in FDA-regulated food, pharmaceutical, and medical device industries.
This study set the stage for subsequent studies that explore the influence of firm leadership on recall decision making, such as Wowak et al. [31] and Mayo et al. [21], both of which are discussed in Section 3.2.
<!-- para 10 -->
Shah et al. [26] took an important step in being the first study to bridge production line decisions and future recalls in the automotive industry by developing a model-year-level data set that directly associates production plant, line-level data for a specific model-year with manufacturing-related recalls on the same model-year.
Among other things, the authors found that overworking factory employees with excessive overtime and having an overabundance of factory-installed vehicle options are both associated with more manufacturing-related recalls.
This study was also one of the first to distinguish between manufacturing- and design-related recalls, developing an important distinction in how to understand recall causes. [^1] Ball et al. [3] extended the research on manufacturing-related recall causes to another important industry sector: the pharmaceutical industry.
The authors explored how excessive cost pressures amid intense generic drug competition may create a setting in which manufacturing plant quality practices are shirked and manufacturing-related recalls increase.
Building on Ball et al. [4], Ball et al. [3] uses FDA plant inspection scores as a mediator to proxy for quality complacency in the production plant.
They found that greater generic drug cost competition leads to an increase in manufacturing-related recalls, as mediated by lower FDA plant inspection scores.
<!-- para 11 -->
A nuanced subset of studies in this area has examined factors that predict, although do not cause, recalls.
Ball et al. [4], for example, examined how well FDA plant inspections can serve as a leading indicator predicting future medical device recalls. [^2] Using a conditional-gap-time recurrent event Cox proportional hazard model, the authors found that when the FDA assigns the lowest quality rating to a plant, an “official action indicated,” the hazard of a recall occurring in the upcoming months or years is much higher than when the FDA assigns the highest quality rating, a “no action indicated” score.
However, this difference in recall likelihood, which on the surface indicates accurate FDA plant inspections, is dependent on the number of times the FDA inspector has visited that particular plant.
FDA inspectors who are new to a plant are most objective and assign the most predictive scores, whereas inspectors who have been to the plant even just one time in the past have significantly less predictive plant inspection scores.
This outcome helped inform the FDA in inspection assignment strategies.
<!-- para 12 -->
More recently, Mukherjee and Sinha [23] mined a particularly large and unstructured database of FDA medical device adverse events to generate a predictive model that associates adverse events to medical device recalls.
The authors found an underreaction bias in initiating medical device recalls, which may be explained by noisy signals contained in the medical device complaint language in the adverse events database.
They also found that highly severe product defects may bias managers to overreact and initiate a recall when one may not be needed.
<!-- para 14 -->
The vast majority of recall causal studies are empirical in nature, with only a few studies using analytical modeling to understand the causes of product recalls.
Chao et al. [8] is one of the earliest analytical recall studies; the authors examined causes of recalls as well as mitigation tactics by modeling supply chain contracts, particularly cost-sharing contracts for recalls.
Similar to other supply chain contracting studies, the authors found that the optimal efforts exerted by supply chain entities are a function of the rewards that each entity receives from the contact.
Both the extent and mechanism of cost sharing determine how much recall mitigation and quality improvement efforts the manufacturer and supplier will exert in identifying the root cause of a recall and in improving product quality to minimize the future risk of similar recalls.
The authors demonstrated using a supermodular game that both cost-sharing contracts based on selective root cause analysis of a recall and partial cost-sharing contracts based on complete root cause analysis of a recall can motivate the manufacturer and the supplier to exert the optimal effort that minimizes the risk of future recalls.
These authors analyzed a complicated aspect of product recalls, as supply chain cost-sharing data and the effort exerted by the manufacturer and the supplier are not observable in empirical studies.
In other words, other than episodic anecdotal evidence of cost and effort sharing in supply chains, sufficient secondary data are unavailable to conduct this sort of study.
<!-- para 15 -->
More recently, Gao et al. [15] used an analytical model to analyze the trade-off between design efforts associated with the reliability of a product and the time-to-market.
They also modeled the role of the regulator in imposing minimum product reliability standards.
Surprisingly, the authors found that minimum product reliability standards set by a government regulator may harm product reliability, resulting in less reliable products that are associated with higher recall rates.
The authors modeled the disutility to customers from a recall event and a customer's purchase likelihood as a function of customer utility, as well as the cost associated with the design and marketing of a product, as the joint cost of product innovation.
The paper demonstrates that if highly innovative products are not given sufficient time to establish reliability through testing and surveillance, the likelihood of recall is considerably higher than for products with a low level of innovation but the same level of reliability.
Consequently, the authors concluded that for highly innovative products, it may be optimal to delay product launch and improve the reliability of the product.
A novel aspect of this study is that the authors also imposed a constraint on the lower bound of product reliability that may be imposed by regulators, such as the FDA, in approving a product.
The model surprisingly shows that regulator-imposed minimum product reliability levels may fail to reduce recall likelihood because the optimal strategy of the firm shifts from delaying the product to early product launch.
In such cases, the firm exerts less effort on reliability than the firm would have exerted under no minimum threshold.
<!-- para 17 -->
Although experiments are an ideal methodology to examine recall causes, as they allow researchers to examine causal relationships with little to no risk of endogeneity, we know of no published studies in this area.
Members of this author team, however, are working on a few papers in this area that may help take the first step in filling this void in the literature.
One such paper uses a simulated manufacturing line in a laboratory with subjects making Lego cars and randomly assigns various treatments to production line assemblers and inspectors, to seek out the optimal set of incentives that can be leveraged to both reduce the rate of quality problems created by assemblers and reduce the rate of quality escapes by inspectors, contingent on the subject's gender.
Research of this sort may be particularly insightful because it could shed light on how or why design engineers, manufacturing operators, and quality inspectors may be incentivized to reduce systemic product quality defects or escapes that cause a recall.
<!-- para 19 -->
We preface each future research section with a brief illustrative anecdote of an actual recall.
The recall discussed here is for a first-of-its-kind implantable device that was under development by a large, well-established medical device company.
The implantable device screwed onto a cable, which was used by surgeons to implant the device into an organ via a catheter.
The device was symmetric: it had the same connection end screws on the top, which was used for implantation, and the bottom, which was used for possible retraction.
The only distinguishing factor indicating whether the device was right side up was the orientation of metal barbs that were used to set the device as part of implantation.
When the device was implanted right side up, the barbs would help to anchor the device in place.
However, if the device was accidentally inserted upside down, the barbs would puncture the organ, which could potentially kill the patient.
The device was screwed onto the cable and pulled into a catheter by production line operators during the final packaging process; thus, implanting physicians would have no reason to inspect the device before implanting it, as they would assume the device was attached to the cable correctly by the manufacturer.
Even if implanting physicians inspected the device, they were unlikely to know whether it was right side up or upside down because it was a novel device, and the barbs were small and difficult to detect.
<!-- para 20 -->
During a design review meeting a few weeks before product launch, a production manager asked a design manager if the production line operators would be able to accidentally screw the device to the cable upside down, observing that both the top and bottom of the device looked almost identical and had identically bored end screws.
The design manager stated it was unlikely, assuming that the production line operators were properly trained on which side of the device to attach the cable, but it could technically happen.
Given the high stakes of an error in the production line, the production manager requested a design change before product launch that would alter the boring of the end screw on one end of the device so it could not be packaged upside down—an error-proofing solution.
The senior executive at the meeting, who was heavily compensated with stock options, discounted the concern, as changing its design now would push back product launch.
Given that this product was the first of its kind and expected to make the firm a considerable amount of money, the production manager was directed to fix the issue with a manufacturing engineering change after product launch.
<!-- para 21 -->
Several months after the product launch, a physician was attempting to implant the device into a patient, and a sales representative from the company happened to be present at this implant.
The sales representative noticed, as the device was pushed through the catheter, that it was upside down.
The sales representative stopped the physician from implanting the device and then called the company, notifying them of the defect.
The company investigated the issue and realized that operators had been repeatedly, but not consistently, packaging devices upside down.
The firm chose to recall all devices that had shipped since launch.
The fix for the recall, unsurprisingly, was to alter the end screw on one end of the device so that production line operators could not attach the device to the cable upside down: the exact solution the production manager requested prior to product launch.
This story is representative of similar recalls in which a series of unfortunate, but at times predictable, events occur.
<!-- para 22 -->
This brief anecdote highlights several research questions that scholars may explore.
First, what is the benefit and prevalence of error-proofing solutions in production lines as effective recall deterrents?
There is a rich literature stream on such solutions, but it has not been bridged with product recall research; doing so could reveal rich insights.
Second, given that most recall research published to date has focused on operational production line causes to recalls, we believe there are numerous research opportunities to explore the relationship between product design decisions and recall causes.
From our experience, almost half of all recalls are rooted in poor design decisions; thus, research examining the relationship between product design decisions and product recalls could have considerable implications for firms.
For example, are product design decisions, which may cause recalls, made differently when there is greater gender, ethnic, or background diversity among employees?
Third, could recall causes be reduced if executive compensation changes?
Executives are often financially rewarded for the number of new products introduced or being the first to market with new products, which may result in products being rushed to market.
Would changing executive compensation to be partially based on having fewer product quality problems reduce recall causes? [^3]
<!-- para 23 -->
From a methodological perspective, empirical research exploring recall causes can also be enhanced by employing methods to better tease out true causal relationships.
These may include exogenous shocks, difference-in-difference models, or instrumental variables when appropriate, which have been infrequently used in recall causal research.
Similarly, natural or laboratory-based experiments examining how incentives, or exposure and sensitivity to customer harm from defective products, may help identify mechanisms that can be used to reduce recalls.
<!-- para 25 -->
The recall decision is a minefield of conflicting objectives—recalling protects customers, for example, but doing so can erode shareholder value and cost executives financial bonuses or even jobs in some extreme cases—making it a fruitful and exciting area to study.
The illustrative examples of Toyota's brake-pedal recall (Mukherjee et al. [24]) and Medtronic's Sprint Fidelis defibrillator recall (Morrison et al. [22]) provide anecdotal evidence toward the existence of these trade-offs and, arguably, opportunistic behavior by firm managers.
As the majority of recalls in most industries are voluntary, these conflicting objectives can create managerial biases that warrant rigorous research attention.
Firm leaders who are tasked with making recall decisions have to simultaneously balance protecting customer well-being, maximizing profits for shareholders, pleasing regulators, and managing concerned employees, all while also trying to protect their own career and financial security.
Research indicates these factors influence the recall decisions to varying degrees.
<!-- para 26 -->
Understanding who makes these decisions and how they are made has been an important aspect of the few empirical recall decision-making studies.
These studies have shown that quality managers, vice presidents, boards of directors (Ball et al. [2], Wowak et al. [31]), and regulators (Mukherjee et al. [24]) all have a hand in if, when, and how recall decisions are made.
Additionally, teasing out the difference between high-severity recalls that have little discretion in if but significant discretion in when they are issued versus low-severity recalls that have discretion both in if and when they are issued has helped shed light on this decision (Ball et al. [3], Wowak et al. [31]).
Perhaps most important, however, is understanding how a novel recall variable—time-to-recall—has grown in recent popularity as a dependent variable in recall decision-making studies (Astvansh et al. [1]).
We begin this section by first explaining the recall decision-making process to properly contextualize what is studied, and then we discuss the limited research in this area across empirical, analytical, and behavioral methods.
<!-- para 28 -->
From an industry agnostic and highly generalized viewpoint, recall decisions are made in the following manner.
Product quality concerns are raised, which could be due to complaints from customers (e.g., automotive crash caused by faulty brakes), problems identified by suppliers (e.g., using an unvalidated setting on equipment), production personnel (e.g., employee improperly trained on a process), research and development (R&D) (e.g., a material validation that exposes risk in a marketed product), or a regulator (e.g., NHTSA runs a testing laboratory to confirm that products comply with safety standards).
Regardless of the source, the potential problem is routed to what is usually a reliability or quality engineer, who then determines whether (1) the potential problem is a false alarm, (2) the problem is an isolated incident, in which case a recall is not needed, or (3) there is a systematic quality issue with a subset of similarly made products on the market that may warrant a recall.
Although the quality or reliability engineer is typically in charge of the investigation process, such engineers often collaborate with the research engineers who designed the product and the manufacturing engineers who oversee production.
Once sufficient evidence is accumulated to make a decision, the quality or reliability engineer passes the information to his or her manager.
That manager initiates a cross-functional recall review committee that consists of managers from various functions such as R&D, manufacturing, regulatory, legal, reliability, and quality.
The committee then makes a recommendation to the executive responsible for recall decisions.
Once a recall decision is made, things move very quickly.
The appropriate recall regulator is immediately informed, a notification is put on the firm's website, a press release may be distributed depending on the scope and severity of the issue, and customers are notified, highlighting the exact impacted lot and/or serial numbers sold and what to do with the faulty product.
The recall has thus begun.
<!-- para 30 -->
The earliest study that examined recall decisions—in particular, the time to recall—was Hora et al. [17], leveraging toy industry data.
Hora and colleagues found, among other things, that when the defect underlying the recall is design related as opposed to manufacturing related, the time to recall is longer.
This was a groundbreaking study in examining predictors of time to recall and set the stage for numerous studies.
The authors also explored the influence of the supply chain entity responsible for the recall and observed that the farther upstream the responsible entity was (i.e., the further away it was from the end customer), the longer the time to recall.
Because of data limitations at the CPSC, the government agency that oversees toy recalls, the authors were unable to determine when a recalling firm first became aware of a defect needing recall; thus, they measured time to recall as the time elapsed between the product launch date and the recall date.
<!-- para 31 -->
Eilert et al. [13] took a significant step toward capturing true defect awareness in their study on the timing of automotive recalls.
The authors used the date when NHTSA, the automobile recall regulator, began an official investigation into a defect as the proxy for the defect awareness date.
They compared this date to the recall initiation date to create the time-to-recall measure.
This time-to-recall measure is more accurate than comparing the launch date to recall date, as in Hora et al. [17], but it still has two significant limitations: (1) many recalls are not associated with a regulator investigation, and (2) firms are almost always aware of a quality issue well before a regulator is.
Eilert et al. [13] found that more severe defects are associated with longer recall times but less so when the brand has a strong quality reputation or has a history of severe recalls.
The authors also explored market reactions to recalls, finding that the market penalizes recall delays, an important point that is examined in other settings and with different time-to-recall measures in future studies.
<!-- para 32 -->
Hora et al. [17] used recall data from the CPSC, and Eilert et al. [13] used recall data from NHTSA; neither agency requires recalling firms to submit a defect awareness date—that is, the date when a firm became aware of a defect—in their official recall reports to the regulators.
Without this defect awareness date, it is difficult to accurately study how long firm leaders take to initiate a recall and to examine any biases or negligence that may be at play in potentially delaying recalls.
The FDA, however, does require recalling firms to provide a defect awareness date in their official recall reports, making it the ideal context to study recall decision making.
This is the date that a firm first became aware of a product quality problem and normally originates from consumer complaints, internal testing, or supplier notifications.
These dates are validated by the FDA through their monitoring of product complaints and when conducting plant inspections.
Thus, the defect awareness date provided by the FDA is, from our experience working closely with the FDA, accurate.
Interestingly, this defect awareness date has been collected by the FDA for years but was not used by scholars in research until Wowak et al. [31].
The defect awareness date was not something that those coauthors were even aware of until an FDA director raised the possibility of using it when one of that paper's coauthors was giving a presentation at FDA headquarters.
It is now something that anyone can retrieve in a straightforward manner via a Freedom of Information Act (FOIA) request. [^4] Wowak and colleagues used this defect awareness date to examine how the gender diversity of firm leadership—namely, board members—influences how often and how quickly firms issue pharmaceutical and medical device recalls.
They argue that leadership gender diversity is essential for setting a tone within the firm for making timely recall decisions that adhere to FDA recall guidelines.
As one woman is added to an all-male board, firms are significantly less likely to hide low-severity quality problems that are high in recall decision-making discretion and simultaneously make much faster recall decisions for the high-severity quality problems that are low in recall decision-making discretion.
These results strengthen as each additional woman is added to the board.
From a theoretical perspective, the authors conclude that all-male boards may emphasize financial returns at the expense of customer safety, which helps explain the benefit of having gender-diverse boards.
<!-- para 33 -->
Mukherjee et al. [24] extended Wowak et al. [31] by considering how external factors—namely, competition—influence recall decisions in the automotive industry.
As NHTSA does not collect defect awareness dates from firms, and as this study is focused on competition and recalls, the authors use the time between a competitor recall and a firm recall as the time to recall, an imperfect proxy for recall timing.
Mukherjee et al. [24] used both empirical data and analytical modeling to examine how competition in the automobile industry influences the time to recall, as well as how this timing impacts the stock market effects of a recall.
In doing so, the authors identified the phenomenon of recall clustering, which is when a recall by one firm (i.e., a leading recall) stimulates recalls by competitors (i.e., following recalls) in close temporal proximity for both related and unrelated defects.
The authors argued that this phenomenon is driven by following firms wanting to escape blame for a recall, so they decide to issue a recall immediately after a competitor announces a recall.
This rationale is substantiated by their finding that the leading recall in a cluster results in the largest stock market penalty, whereas following recalls generate significantly lower stock market penalties, weakening the further away in time a following recall is from the leading recall.
In other words, firms benefit financially by waiting to announce a recall until immediately after a competitor announces a recall.
<!-- para 34 -->
Mayo et al. [21] is the most recent empirical recall decision-making study we identified.
Building on the studies of Wowak et al. [30, 31], both of which examined how firm leadership influences recall occurrences, Mayo et al. [21] examined the relationship between the CEO tenure of all publicly traded consumer product industry firms and the hazard of a future recall.
As the CPSC does not collect defect awareness dates, the authors examined the time from the CEO hire date to recall initiation to proxy for time to recall.
The authors found that shorter-tenured CEOs appear much more open to recalling defective products than are longer-tenured CEOs.
After the first three years of a CEO's tenure, however, a recall is very unlikely, indicating some concern that consumer product firms may be hiding product quality problems to protect long-tenured CEOs who have closely aligned their reputation with the firm's.
<!-- para 36 -->
We only identified two studies that analytically model the recall decision.
The earliest analytical recall decision-making study is Mao et al. [20].
The authors used a modified hazard function to model the risk of product failure and the subsequent likelihood a customer purchases the product.
They used a static multiperiod cumulative profit maximization objective function to model recall decisions and found that when product failures occur early in the product's life cycle, firms tend to (1) delay recalls more and (2) engage less in recall investigation and mitigation efforts.
<!-- para 37 -->
Mukherjee et al. [24], described in the previous subsection, extended the static model in Mao et al. [20] by using a Nash stopping game, which is a dynamic analytical modeling approach, to model recall decisions of competing firms.
Recalls involve multiple costs, including the cost of managing the recall, brand equity loss, financial losses as a result of adverse reactions by investors, and possibly legal fees or damages if customers are harmed.
Given the competing demands on managers to protect customers yet maximize profits for shareholders, it is only natural they behave in an economically rational manner when making recall decisions.
Leveraging herding theory and attribution theory, Mukherjee et al. [24] argued that when only one firm issues a recall during a certain period, outsiders attribute the recall to something unique about that firm; thus, the firm is assigned more blame in the form of a larger stock market penalty.
However, if multiple firms issue a recall during a certain time period, outsiders are more likely to attribute the recalls to factors outside the firm's control; thus, the firms are assigned less blame in the form of a lower stock market penalty.
<!-- para 39 -->
Ball et al. [2] is the lone study we identified using a behavioral experiment to examine recall decision making.
Through partnering with a large medical device firm, the authors identified three primary factors that were randomized in a vignette-type experiment using real recall decision makers at the firm as subjects.
These factors were (1) physician concern (the amount of negative feedback received by the firm from physician customers of the failing medical device), (2) defect detectability (how detectable the failing medical device defect was to physician customers before using it on a patient), and (3) root cause understanding (the depth of firm understanding of what was actually causing the potentially faulty medical device to fail).
The authors also employed the cognitive reflection test (CRT) (Frederick [14]) to examine how a recall decision maker's level of reflection influenced his or her recall decision.
The findings indicate that CRT was the most influential factor, followed by defect detectability and root cause understanding.
Recall decision makers who were highly reflective were significantly less likely to recall, ostensibly because they were prone to wait for more data before recalling (e.g., analysis-paralysis).
Defects that were highly detectable by the physician customer, such as a cracked screen on an electronic cardiac device, were less likely to be recalled because the decision makers at the firm could rely on the physician customers to screen out defects for the firm, eliminating the need to recall—something that is not allowed by the FDA.
Finally, the more that was understood about the root cause of the failure, the more likely the device would be recalled.
The CRT finding was particularly surprising to the partner firm, who incorporated the insights into a revised recall manager training program designed to highlight behavioral characteristics that may inadvertently slow recall decisions.
<!-- para 41 -->
A recall from a different medical device firm is the subject of the recall decision-making anecdote.
A medical product failed to provide therapy to a young patient who subsequently died from the defective product.
The patient's doctor, concerned about the product failure, discussed it with the firm.
During these discussions, it was revealed that this was not the first time the product failed; it had failed more than 25 other times, creating potentially life-threatening situations for the patients.
The doctor strongly recommended that the firm recall the product.
The firm leaders, who were predominantly engineering executives, declined to recall, citing a low failure rate that was under the predicted and expected failure rate for such a product, which was a top-selling product at the time.
The doctor, concerned about other patients who may lose their lives because of the defective product, approached a national newspaper about the situation; the newspaper quickly picked up the story.
The journalist at the newspaper reached out to the firm for a comment.
Upon discovering the story was going to run on the front page of the newspaper, the firm decided to recall the product.
The hesitation to act until the firm was under national media scrutiny created a negative ripple effect, which included increased pressure from the FDA via targeted and aggressive plant inspections, questions from members of Congress, and mass departures of middle-level and senior managers.
The firm never fully recovered.
<!-- para 42 -->
Although this is an admittedly extreme example of a recall decision gone awry, recall decisions are rarely straightforward for firms, and we include this story to demonstrate that there are numerous intriguing areas of future exploration in this recall decision-making setting.
We believe that biases that sway recall decisions may be a particularly rich future research area.
Recall decision-making biases can be subtle, and leaders may frequently fall prey to them unconsciously.
Future research examining how various aspects of diversity (e.g., ethic, gender, background) influence recall decisions and in what echelons of the organization does it matter most (e.g., board, top management team, midlevel managers, employees) could be particularly impactful research.
Compensation incentives may also change a firm's recall decision-making calculus and thus bias recall decisions.
Recall decision-making biases could also stem from political leanings of firm leadership, customer feedback, or the amount of media attention a firm receives.
<!-- para 43 -->
From a methodological perspective, it is difficult to capture true underlying biases using purely secondary empirical data, and thus analytical models and behavioral experiments can play an important role in future research investigating recall decisions.
For example, the true effort levels of firms and managers, or individual risk attitudes of managers making recall decisions, make empirical research on decision biases difficult from an identification perspective.
Experimental setups can simulate decision making in a laboratory or a field setting and help reveal the influence of such factors on recall decisions.
Similarly, analytical models can utilize reasonable assumptions related to these factors and analyze the influence of these factors on the recall decisions from a theoretical perspective.
We contend that although these three methodological domains of research have been viewed independently of each other in many situations, in the case of recall decisions, there is a need to integrate and cross-validate these different methodologies.
Although analytical models can propose theory related to recall decisions under behavioral biases, such as risk averseness and game playing among the stakeholders, it is imperative that the presence and the extent of such biases be verified from experimental studies and the final effect of these latent factors on the manifested recall decisions be empirically cross-validated.
<!-- para 46 -->
Jarrell and Peltzman [18] is one of the first empirical recall studies and one of the most highly cited recall papers.
They broke new ground by examining how the stock market reacts to recalls.
Studying automobile and pharmaceutical recalls, the authors found that the market penalizes firms for recalling and that these penalties can exceed costs to the firm for the recall and repair of the products.
This study had a profound impact on recall research because it was the first study, to our knowledge, to use an event study to explore how the market reacts to recall announcements.
<!-- para 47 -->
The results of subsequent studies are mixed and reveal how nuanced aspects of a recall, firm, or industry may influence how the market reacts to a recall announcement.
Bromiley and Marcus [7], for example, observed a temporary drop in share price following automotive recalls but found that the stock generally recovers quickly to its previous price, just days after the recall-related drop—an insight that was later observed in Mukherjee et al. [24].
Insights from this study challenged the findings of Jarrell and Peltzman [18] that the market serves to effectively deter dubious behavior on the part of negligent firms.
Davidson and Worrell [10] sought to extend Jarrell and Peltzman [18] and Bromiley and Marcus [7] by investigating stock market reactions to nonautomotive recall announcements.
Their rationale for doing so was that automobile recalls are so prevalent that the market reaction to them may be muted.
Davidson and Worrell [10] examined all nonautomotive recalls reported in the Wall Street
<!-- para 48 -->
Journal over a 20-year period (1968–1987) and found, among other things, that there is a negative and significant market reaction to recalls.
They also compared government-ordered recalls against voluntary recalls and found little statistical difference in the market reaction between the two recall types.
<!-- para 49 -->
Chen et al. [9] examined whether a firm's recall strategy influences the recall-market relationship in the consumer products industry.
Chen and colleagues found that proactive recalls (i.e., recalls announced before major injuries or customer harm occurs) lead to a more negative market reaction than do passive recalls (i.e., when a recall is delayed and/or a firm tries to shift responsibility to another firm), especially when the recalling firm is less reputable.
The authors concluded that this is due to the market perceiving the early, proactive action as a signal for a more serious product quality issue.
Thirumalai and Sinha [29] extended research in this area by being the first study, to our knowledge, to conduct a recall-related event study in the medical device industry.
The authors found no significant negative market reaction following serious life-threatening medical device recalls, concluding that investors have possibly already calculated the riskiness of such boundary-pushing technological devices in their share price.
Mukherjee et al. [24], the most recent study in this area, is the first study to consider how recall clusters (i.e., recalls announced by competitors in close temporal proximity) influence the market's reaction to recalls.
As noted previously, they found that firms that issue a leading recall experience a more severe market penalty than firms that issue following recalls, a finding that is implicitly hinted in the findings of Chen et al. [9].
In aggregate, these recall-stock market studies indicate that the market's reaction to recalls is contingent on several factors such as industry risk, competitor actions, recall strategy, and firm reputation, just to name a few examples.
<!-- para 50 -->
Although the majority of studies in this area have focused on the financial effects of product recalls, there is limited yet growing research that considers the reputational effects of recalls.
Rhee and Haunschild [25] was the first study to explore how firm reputation influences market reactions to product defects, using market share to measure the market reaction.
The authors found that, as the title of their study hints, there is a liability to a good reputation.
Firms that have a higher-quality reputation heading into a recall suffer a greater customer loyalty reaction, losing market share more quickly than firms that recall with a weaker quality reputation, adding important insight into the domain of recall effects.
Zavyalova et al. [32] extended research in this area by investigating how the media respond to recalls in the toy industry.
The authors found that the media react negatively to recalls, but the effect is contingent on the number of recalls in the industry.
Liu and Shankar [19] also considered media attention while investigating how recalls impact market share and sales but in the automotive industry.
Using a structural model, they find that recalls that have greater media attention, are more severe, and are issued by firms that have a superior quality reputation are associated with a larger decrease in market share and sales.
<!-- para 51 -->
As media have evolved, so too has recall research that examines media effects of recalls.
Borah and Tellis [6, p. 143] is one of the first recall studies to consider the influence of social media.
The authors identify a “perverse halo” effect as it pertains to recalls.
Specifically, they found that negative online chatter about a brand as a consequence of a recall spills over to other brands in a significant way.
Furthermore, online chatter amplifies the negative effects of a recall on a firm’s sales and stock market performance.
More recently, Beattie et al. [5] examined the relationship between advertising spending by manufacturers and media coverage following a recall.
The authors found that media outlets provide less coverage of recalls issued by firms that spend more on advertising at their outlets, a concerning finding that may indicate media bias on recall coverage driven by media firm financial interests.
<!-- para 53 -->
To our knowledge, the only paper that develops an analytical model that examines the effect of recall timing on shareholder value from an analytical model perspective is Mukherjee et al.
<!-- para 54 -->
[24], which has been previously discussed.
Using a game-theoretic Nash stopping time model, the authors proposed that unique recalls attract a higher stock market penalty compared with nonunique recalls in terms of the timing of the recalls.
Therefore, we contend that there is significant opportunity to analytically model consequences of a recall decision.
<!-- para 56 -->
The key study we identified on recall effects in a laboratory setting is Dawar and Pillutla [11].
The authors manipulated prior customer expectations using newspaper articles that described firms as either highly reputable or not highly reputable leading up to a recall.
Firm responses were manipulated using fictitious firm recall reactions including a full-page newspaper apology; an ambiguous firm response consisting primarily of a recall notice; and a less ambiguous, defensive response denying responsibility for the problem.
A 2 by 3 factorial between-subjects design was employed to examine how these factors may influence the subjects' perception of the recalling brand.
The authors found, among other things, that customer expectations moderate the firm response-brand perception relationship following a recall.
Lower expectations accentuate the negative relationship between ambiguous and defensive firm responses and brand perception.
<!-- para 58 -->
Our final anecdote highlights the cascading effects of product recalls.
A design engineer at a firm attempted a validation of a new plastic pouch-sealing process for a sterilized medical product.
The validation failed, indicating the possibility of a leak in the sterile seal, which would cause the products to become unsterilized and hazardous to patients.
In an effort to determine whether the issue was due to the new pouch-sealing equipment, the design engineer reattempted the validation using an older sealer that was already released for production.
The validation, once again, failed.
To assess whether this was a new problem, the design engineer reviewed the design history files for the products already on the market that used the older sealing equipment and found that the previous validation process had been done incorrectly.
This meant all products sealed with the old process were potentially unsterile, although not a single complaint was recorded related to this problem. [^5]
<!-- para 59 -->
Nonetheless, out of an abundance of caution, the executives at the firm decided to recall all products ever made using the old sealer—almost half a million products and all products within a given market area sold by the firm.
By executing such an extensive recall, the company went into immediate backorder on hundreds of product lines.
Unfortunately, the company did not have the manufacturing capacity needed to catch up on all of the orders.
In an effort to quickly increase capacity, the company implemented mandatory overtime for all employees, from vice presidents to operators, including working on Saturdays and Sundays for many months.
The firm's sales reps faced irate customers who were not getting orders on time or in the desired quantity, and the firm's market share quickly eroded, as competitors had comparable products in stock and ready to ship.
All of these factors resulted in the firm's once-positive culture turning negative, eventually causing several senior managers to leave.
Customers faced backorders, competitors gained ground, managers left the firm, and employees were overworked and frustrated—all for a recall that did not have a single complaint tied to the problem but could endanger customers.
Much can be learned from this real example.
<!-- para 60 -->
Researchers may need to closely examine the cascading, and sometimes unexpected, effects of product recalls in hopes of providing guidance to firms about how to mitigate the consequences in advance.
For example, what can companies do to prevent their culture from turning negative during major recalls?
What strategies can companies implement to maintain market share while enduring a recall?
One specific area of future research opportunity is in understanding the factors that moderate the market penalties of a recall decision.
Several studies have identified negative market penalties of recalls; however, only a few studies have tried to understand factors that moderate such penalties.
Relevant potential moderators tied to this anecdote could include the number of firm competitors or the intensity of competition, the number of actual complaints associated with the recall, or the source of the defect.
<!-- para 62 -->
The role of the regulator is a key attribute that sets recalls apart from more generalized product quality problems.
In this final section, we compare and contrast the three primary recall regulators, identifying their strengths and weaknesses and recall research implications, and provide a narrative on our research partnerships with one of those regulators.
<!-- para 64 -->
The United States has three primary regulatory bodies that oversee product recalls: NHTSA regulates automotive recalls; the FDA regulates food, medical device, pharmaceutical, and cosmetic recalls; and the CPSC regulates nearly everything else, such as toys, baby strollers, blinds, and space heaters.
Interestingly, our extensive study of these regulators indicates that little coordination occurs between them.
They appear to operate in silos, failing to learn best practices from each other.
Although we acknowledge the fact that regulators adopt policies that align with the riskiness of the industry they regulate, and that an implantable medical device regulated by the FDA is not as risky as a space heater regulated by the CPSC, each regulator oversees products that have the potential to seriously harm customers if and when they malfunction.
Consequently, we highlight a few key differences herein to ideally stimulate some best-practice sharing.
We describe each regulator in the following in what we perceive to be the order of most rigorous to least rigorous product quality and recall oversight.
<!-- para 65 -->
The FDA is, in our view, the regulator with the most rigorous product quality and recall oversight strategy.
The FDA maintains a full product life-cycle responsibility on many of their products.
For example, for pharmaceuticals and medical devices, products require market clearance by the FDA before sales can commence, although this is not the case for food and cosmetics.
For all FDA-managed products, including food and cosmetics, once the product is on the market, quality and compliance surveillance activities include frequent and often surprise plant inspections.
These inspections examine compliance with FDA-developed criteria to help ensure the safety of products and high-quality manufacturing.
Market surveillance activities also include complaint monitoring and analyses roles that collect, dissect, and disseminate product failure data. [^6] Although the FDA mandates almost no recalls in practice, they are adroit at soft mandates.
In other words, through close industry relationships, FDA managers frequently reach out directly to the appropriate industry colleagues and inquire about the need for a recall when product failure data indicate that it may be warranted.
Although this does not always lead to a recall, the FDA has chosen to allow firms to voluntarily recall nearly all the time, even when there are behind-the-scenes discussions occurring between FDA and industry.
When recalls do occur, the FDA requires firms to provide a defect awareness date, indicating how long the firm has been aware of the problem.
These dates range from one day to as much as one year or more in some cases, with an average of approximately three months.
To officially close a recall, the FDA needs to agree with the firm that all the required steps have been taken; that the defective products have been repaired, removed, or replaced; and that the firm has addressed the root cause underlying the recall.
Significant amounts of recall data are made available online, and even more are available through a FOIA request. [^7]
<!-- para 66 -->
NHTSA has less of a full life-cycle approach to product quality and recall management and has less of a collaborative relationship with firms, at least from our perspective.
NHTSA does not technically approve new vehicles before they come to market, although firms are required to adhere to vehicle safety standards.
Unlike the collaborative approach espoused by FDA, NHTSA mandates nearly 20% of automotive recalls, on average.
Consistent with this more confrontational approach, NHTSA also runs a product testing laboratory in which vehicles are tested for compliance, and failures in such a laboratory can lead to NHTSA-mandated recalls.
NHTSA does not have a premarket approval role, nor does it conduct regular plant inspections as does the FDA, but NHTSA does monitor product quality complaints similar to the FDA.
NHTSA maintains a publicly available complaint database and a large recall database that contains PDF files of all firm-provided recall documentation. [^8] Although the PDF nature of these documents is not amenable to data download and requires extensive cleaning, it is quite transparent.
Although NHTSA does not collect defect awareness dates from firms, they do require manufacturers to provide a full recall chronology section in their recall notice to NHTSA.
However, these chronologies are highly unstructured, sometimes contain no dates, and at other times have dates such as “in early summer” or “in 2006.” This lack of structure in regard to specific dates inhibits rigorous time-to-recall research in the automobile industry.
<!-- para 67 -->
From our perspective, CPSC is the distant third in recall regulatory rigor.
There are not only no premarket approval activities but also little market surveillance activities.
In addition, CPSC almost never mandates a recall.
CPSC does, however, maintain a complaint and recall database, similar to NHTSA and FDA, [^9] but there are few data fields provided, no defect awareness dates, and no chronologies such as what NHTSA requires.
This lack of recall data and product quality oversight is a cause for concern that has been highlighted in the popular press (Terlep and Prang [28]) and academic studies (Mayo et al. [21]).
On the basis of these significant differences, we offer three recommendations.
<!-- para 68 -->
First, FDA, NHTSA, and CPSC would benefit from establishing a joint federal recall oversight committee.
From our experience interacting with each regulator, employees in these three branches of government have highly varied backgrounds, experiences, and familiarity with data in general and data analytics in particular.
Establishing tighter connections between these three important regulators may help to ensure that cost-effective recall regulations are enacted across all three bodies that encourage industry innovation while at the same time protecting customers from harmful products.
Second, as one example of a shared best practice that would emanate from such a joint recall committee, NHTSA and CPSC would significantly improve recall decision-making transparency if they began requiring firms to report a defect awareness date when initiating a recall.
This simple, cost-neutral step can enable richer research by regulators and academics that can help uncover the many potential biases within the recall decision, as well as help to make these decisions more objective and, ideally, more timely.
Third, another likely outcome of closer collaboration between these organizations is improved recall data transparency and comprehensiveness.
The FDA defect awareness date is an apt example.
The data were always collected by FDA but not made public on their recall websites.
In other words, there is a significant amount of recall data that can be obtained by a FOIA request to any of these agencies, but researchers cannot request FOIA data that they do not know exist in the first place.
A more transparent approach that all three agencies could take is to make a significantly greater amount of their recall data fully available online without the need for a FOIA request and to ensure that what each agency provides is consistent with the others so that cross-industry comparisons can be made.
<!-- para 70 -->
Certain members of this author team, along with several other academic researchers, have had the opportunity to partner with the FDA in two separate collaborations.
The first was a multiuniversity grant; the second was a multiuniversity federal contract.
These two distinct means of working with a federal regulator provide benefits and limitations that we share to hopefully empower future academic researchers to make appropriate collaboration decisions.
We preface this discussion with the caveat that this is a result of our idiosyncratic experiences that may not necessarily represent all such collaborations.
The three primary benefits, from our experiences, of a federal grant with a regulator such as the FDA are close access to federal regulators, research funding, and publication flexibility.
Through monthly meetings with senior members of the FDA and their well-trained PhD researchers, we gained deep insight into how the FDA views various aspects of research including plant inspections, recall decision making, motivations that drive firm executives in their product quality decision making, and pharmaceutical product quality for drugs made within and outside the United States, as well as for original versus generic drugs.
This level of access and insight has informed our future research in this domain in a manner that is difficult to quantify but very valuable.
In addition, a grant provides significant funding to purchase data, hire postdoctoral students to conduct grant research, travel to the FDA to meet in person with collaborators, and even buy out teaching semesters to enable a singular focus on research for short bursts of time.
<!-- para 71 -->
From our view, a key negative aspect of a grant is the lack of data availability.
We learned in this experience that regulators are not allowed to provide grant-funded researchers with any proprietary data that would not be reasonably made available to the general public through the FOIA.
Thus, our aspiration to access specialized FDA data in the grant was never realized.
However, this key negative has a research implication that ties directly into the third benefit of a grant, which is publication flexibility.
Because data are not proprietarily provided by the FDA, the FDA, at least from our understanding, has limited to no control over what is published emanating from a grant, as long as the FDA is acknowledged as the funder of the project.
<!-- para 72 -->
Federal contracts are similar in some ways and different in others.
First, it is worth highlighting that the terminology used by the government for such a federal contract, from our experience, is a “broad agency announcement,” or BAA.
The broad agency announcement is a way to describe a call for proposals for contract funding. [^10] The BAA contract work provides similar funding to a grant and further shrinks the space between the researcher and the regulator, as the researcher becomes an actual contract employee of the regulator.
This translates into significantly greater data and regulator access in comparison with a grant.
For example, monthly meetings on a grant turned into weekly meetings on a contract.
Furthermore, we were excited to obtain federal security clearances and be issued government laptops, both of which allowed access to large amounts of proprietary regulatory information.
Unsurprisingly, this comes with strings attached.
From our experience, research studies that use proprietary data obtained via a contract require a rigorous internal federal government paper review process prior to submission to an academic journal that has, arguably, opaque objectives.
Papers can be denied the right to be submitted for academic publication at the discretion of the regulator if they use proprietary data obtained within the confines of the contract.
What is apparent to us is that the ability to publish, which is the ultimate objective for academic researchers, may be significantly constrained in a contract, whereas it is essentially unconstrained in a grant.
These are important points for future researchers to consider as they explore these opportunities.

## discussion
<!-- para 2 -->
Product recall research is a burgeoning academic research domain.
Our objective in this tutorial is to explain recall research, what it is, what has been discovered, and where from our perspective the field can go to enrich our knowledge on this phenomenon.
Recall decisions, predominantly voluntary, are a particularly ripe area for rigorous behavioral research exploration, and we hope this paper stimulates more work in this area.
Finally, regulating product recalls is serious business, and the federal regulators who oversee product quality and recalls in the United States have taught us much and, we believe, have opportunities themselves to strengthen recall oversight.
