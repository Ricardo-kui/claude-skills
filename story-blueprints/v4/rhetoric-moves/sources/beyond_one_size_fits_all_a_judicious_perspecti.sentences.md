# beyond_one_size_fits_all_a_judicious_perspecti 句子库存

## introduction
<!-- para 1 -->
Despite the increasing use of artificial intelligence, humans remain the primary drivers of firm performance and economic growth (Campbell, Ganco & Franco, 2012).
This poses challenges for firms, as, unlike physical assets and intellectual property (IP), employees can easily move to competitors of their own free will, transferring valuable firm-sponsored human capital (Becker, 1962; Coff, 1997; Campbell, Coff & Kryscynski, 2012).
These challenges impact society as the risks may lead firms to limit growth-enhancing investments.
Noncompete agreements (NCAs) legally restrict employees from moving to competitors, thus offering firms a level of security akin to tangible and intellectual assets.
Proponents assert that such security bolsters investments in human capital, spurring innovation and growth (e.g., Barnett & Sichelman, 2020; Wickelgren, 2018).
Opponents argue that NCAs impose unnecessary restrictions on employee freedom and decrease innovation and economic growth (e.g., Anand, Hasan, Sharma & Wang, 2018; Marx & Fleming, 2012; Marx, Singh & Fleming, 2015).
<!-- para 2 -->
As the NCA debate intensifies, NCA opponents are making significant strides in shaping policy.
Several jurisdictions are proposing or enacting restrictions.
For example, in 2023, the United Kingdom proposed capping NCAs to three months post-employment, and New York banned NCAs for middle-class and low-wage workers.
In 2017, Oregon banned NCAs for low-wage workers.
In 2015, Hawaii banned NCAs for tech workers.
In 2015, both Norway and Denmark introduced significant changes to NCA policies, and on April 23, 2024, the U.S. Federal Trade Commission (FTC) issued a rule effectively banning all NCAs across the U.S. [^2] This influence would typically be heralded as a testament to the relevance and rigor of academic research.
However, lost in the translation from academic research to active policy change are the nuances of NCA research findings.
<!-- para 3 -->
The oversimplified narrative presented in popular media is a conclusive case against NCAs: NCAs suppress wages and hinder innovation. [^3] However, the body of NCA research is actually inconclusive, imbalanced, and incomplete (Barnett & Sichelman, 2020, 2016; Lam, Lenard & Wallsten, 2023; Meese, 2022; Yaggie, 2023).
Although some evidence supports the claims of NCAs' negative effects on wages and innovation (e.g., Balasubramanian, Chang, Sakakibara, Sivadasan & Starr, 2022; Lipsitz & Starr, 2022; Samila & Sorenson, 2011; Starr, Balasubramanian & Sakakibara, 2018; Starr, Prescott & Bishara, 2020), a growing body of research challenges them (e.g., Kini, Williams & Yin, 2021; Lavetti, Simon & White, 2020; Rothstein & Starr, 2022; Seo & Somaya, 2022; Xiao, 2022).
<!-- para 4 -->
Simplifying the narrative to cast NCAs in a universally negative light could lead to policy decisions that exacerbate rather than mitigate the issues they aim to address.
The necessity for firms to safeguard against the repercussions of employee mobility might drive them to adopt less transparent, and potentially illegal, strategies to restrict workforce mobility.
Historical precedents include California's tech sector engaging in widely criticized non-poaching agreements (In re High-Tech Employee Antitrust Litigation, 2011–2015) [^4] and, more recently, Norway's experience with high levels of illegal non-poaching agreements following efforts to restrict NCAs (Skogli, Karttinen, Ljøgodt von Hanno, Borch & Jacobsen Øyen, 2023).
Furthermore, firms might resort to creating internal barriers to training and knowledge-sharing (Seo & Somaya, 2022).
Some also contend that NCA bans and restrictions will result in more cases of costly trade secret litigation, although this position is challenged in a recent working paper by Greenwood, Kobayashi, & Starr (2024).
<!-- para 5 -->
This article evaluates the NCA literature, offering insights to practitioners, policymakers, and researchers.
For practicing managers, the decision to implement or forego an NCA requires a balance between safeguarding firm assets and fostering an innovative, mobile workforce.
By exploring the complex nature and diverse NCA impacts across different contexts, our analysis directs managers to areas where NCAs may be advantageous for meeting their specific organizational needs.
For instance, in niche product markets with long research and development (R&D) requirements, especially where patents do not offer sufficient protection, NCAs might be determinative for survival.
In well-functioning labor markets, using NCAs to protect investments in general skills will likely do more harm than good.
Nonetheless, our analyses reveal that the literature lacks clear, evidence-backed advice to firms due to two main deficiencies: a lack of firm-level data, and a research focus on negative societal impacts at the expense of potential positive firm-level effects.
<!-- para 6 -->
For policymakers currently debating NCA policy, our evaluation offers timely insights (Aguinis, Jensen & Kraus, 2022; Markman & Wood, 2022).
We highlight that despite the polarization in the current debate, there is research to support the intended NCA effect of promoting innovation enhancing investments (e.g., Seo & Somaya, 2022; Xiao, 2022).
We posit that many of the ill effects attributed to NCAs arise from NCA misuse rather than targeted, strategic use.
Examples like the widely publicized overly broad NCAs applied to Jimmy John's low-wage sandwich-makers, or restrictive agreements in harassment situations, highlight the critical need for penalties for oppressive and unenforceable NCAs (Aydinliyim, 2022).
Rather than outright bans, which might lead firms to adopt counterproductive measures against collaboration and innovation, it is essential to develop a tailored policy framework that recognizes the need to incentivize uninhibited internal knowledge flows and firm-sponsored investments in employees (Seo & Somaya, 2022; Xiao, 2022).
<!-- para 7 -->
We further elucidate how NCAs are intertwined within a dynamic interplay of cultural norms, management practices, IP, and labor laws.
Our evaluation reveals that this complexity is only superficially understood due to data limitations, a concerning issue given that U.S.-based research is often used to justify restrictions and bans in Europe.
The dynamic nature of NCAs presents numerous untapped research opportunities to explore NCAs' integration into the legal and cultural landscape and NCAs' heterogeneous effects.
These insights can be instrumental insights for both managers and policymakers.
<!-- para 8 -->
Overall, our analysis illuminates the vast uncertainties that pervade the literature.
The diverse and complex conditions surrounding NCA use and enforceability, along with the notable absence of conclusive evidence, should compel scholars, managers, and policymakers to adopt a judicious approach and pause the rush to judgment until more comprehensive research can inform our understanding.

## theory
<!-- para 3 -->
NCAs, also known as restrictive covenants, covenants not to compete, or simply noncompetes, are legal instruments used to prevent employees from joining or becoming competitors (Posner, Triantis & Triantis, 2004). [^5] Almost all jurisdictions that allow NCAs require that the NCA's length, geographical scope, and professional scope is reasonable and necessary to protect firms' legitimate interests.
For example, a chemist at a pharmaceutical firm may be barred from joining any global competitor for a year—a restriction that is considered reasonable given global competition in the industry.
In contrast, the same broad restriction is unreasonable for a chef in Zurich, as their move would be unlikely to harm a local restaurant's competitive position.
However, determining what is “reasonable” varies by jurisdiction and is inherently context-specific, with no predictable legal standard, even in civil law systems with codified laws.
<!-- para 4 -->
In cases of NCA dispute, courts often grant injunctive relief, for which employees risk imprisonment if they start working for the disputed competitor.
Alternatively, damages are assessed if the employee has already breached the NCA by working for a competitor.
<!-- para 6 -->
**Incentivization of efficient investments in human capital.** The theoretical rationale for NCAs predominantly stems from scholarship in law and economics.
This rationale is anchored in the premise that NCAs promote efficient investments in human capital (i.e., knowledge, skills, abilities, and health), which are pivotal to fueling innovation and propelling economic growth (see, e.g., Rubin & Shedd, 1981).
Since human capital investments are costly, society relies on firms to a great extent to supplement individually financed investments in human capital (Becker, 2002).
These investments consist of both general (i.e., investments that any individual or firm can apply) and specific (i.e., investments that can only be applied by specific firms or individuals) human capital investments (Becker, 1962).
<!-- para 7 -->
Since the property rights of human capital belong to the individual (Becker, 1962), the prospect of employee mobility decreases incentives for firms to invest in employee human capital since when employees leave, the potential returns on investment are largely lost to firms providing the investments and, in the case of general human capital, are instead transferred to the individual or other firms (Lester, 2001; Meccheri, 2009; Posner et al., 2004; Rubin & Shedd, 1981; Trebilcock, 1986).
This makes human capital investments tenuous.
<!-- para 8 -->
NCAs, proponents argue, address this concern by offering firms a measure of protection for their investments.
By limiting employees from utilizing firm-sponsored human capital investments elsewhere, NCAs deter the misappropriation of a firm's investment (Meese, 2022).
While NCA enforceability is typically bounded by reasonableness concerning professional, temporal, and geographical factors, proponents argue that even this limited restriction sufficiently incentivizes firms to invest efficiently in their employees' human capital (Posner et al., 2004).
As recipients of optimal human capital investments, employees are argued to also benefit from NCAs, despite their constraints on future mobility, by receiving skill enhancements and development opportunities that they might not be able to afford or pursue on their own (Azevedo, Pereira & Rodrigues, 2018; Lester, 2001; Meccheri, 2009; Meese, 2022; Posner et al., 2004; Rubin & Shedd, 1981; Trebilcock, 1986).
<!-- para 9 -->
Significantly, proponents of human capital investment stimulation through NCAs see benefits beyond formal or on-the-job training.
This investment includes enhancing employee knowledge acquired from active and passive interactions with a firm's culture, routines, trade secrets, and other intellectual assets.
NCAs protect firm knowledge by preventing its transfer to competitors through labor mobility.
Such protection is believed to encourage internal knowledge-sharing, thus spurring innovation (Posner et al., 2004; Seo & Somaya, 2022).
<!-- para 10 -->
Similarly, NCAs may encourage external investment in firms (i.e., venture capital funding and acquisitions) where human capital assets significantly contribute to productivity, innovation, and profitability (Kaplan & Strömberg, 2003; Younge, Tong & Fleming, 2015).
This could thereby amplify NCAs' investment-encouraging effect and, in turn, innovation and economic growth in the aggregate, especially in acquisitions, which have been shown to increase productivity through sorting and upskilling of employees (Siegel & Simons, 2010).
<!-- para 11 -->
**Theoretical opposition: Limitations on employee freedom and knowledge flows.** However, in addition to preventing employees from reaping the benefits of the firm's investments in human capital in other employment, NCAs prevent employees from reaping the benefits of their own investments in general human capital acquired outside of the relationship with the departed firm.
This imposes a cost on employees, as employees with NCAs who leave their firms, provided the departed firms choose to enforce the NCA, must enter a different field of employment or a different geographical location for employment, or wait for the NCA to expire (Aydinliyim, 2022; Marx et al., 2015; Marx, Strumsky & Fleming, 2009).
The limited outside options introduced by the NCA, opponents argue, diminish both employees' bargaining power and their capacity to negotiate higher wages through job mobility (Lipsitz & Starr, 2022) and incentives to invest in their own human capital (Amir & Lobel, 2010; Garmaise, 2011).
<!-- para 12 -->
The theoretical arguments for NCAs' negative impact on innovation and entrepreneurship revolve around NCAs' impact on labor mobility and knowledge spillovers.
Research confirms that knowledge spillovers are instrumental in fostering innovation and entrepreneurship, as they facilitate the dissemination of ideas, technologies, and practices that can spur the development of new products, services, and business models (Acs, Braunerhjelm, Audretsch & Carlsson, 2009; Audretsch & Feldman, 1996).
Thus, by restricting mobility, opponents argue that NCAs restrict knowledge spillovers that are essential for innovation and entrepreneurship (e.g., Baslandze, 2022; Belezon & Schankerman, 2013; Kang & Lee, 2022; Singh & Marx, 2013; Starr, Balasubramanian & Sakakibara, 2018).
<!-- para 14 -->
The prevailing literature largely concentrates on the societal NCA costs and benefits, even though the choice of whether to use NCAs is an individual firm-level management decision.
While the proposed societal NCA benefits do align with firm-performance benefits (i.e., net positive individual firm value creation leads to net positive aggregate value creation), the more aggregate, dynamic, and long-term societal effects may have the opposite effect on firm performance (i.e., the NCA mechanisms that enhance individual firm performance might lead to net negative societal outcomes).
Below, we explore the sources and mechanisms underlying these theoretical relationships, some of which are backed by empirical evidence.
<!-- para 15 -->
**Source of investment.** The most discussed NCA benefit is the inducement of efficient firm-sponsored human capital investments.
However, as mentioned above, NCAs may facilitate external investments in the firm.
Thus, firms might use NCAs purely to acquire funding or increase their acquisition appeal.
NCA use in this context may not lead to increases in firm-sponsored employee human capital, especially if the management team assesses the alternative appropriability mechanisms as sufficient.
That is not to say that the NCA is not value-creating at the firm or societal level, but the mechanism by which NCAs create value may differ.
The firm might use the additional investments to increase complementary physical capital investments or reduce debt (Jeffers, 2024).
In these cases, individual employees, despite their NCA status being the source of the increased investment, are not necessarily the target of that investment.
In these cases, the employees are burdened with the future opportunity costs without receiving any direct benefit.
Moreover, the outcome is affected by whether the investment is skills-based or knowledge-based.
<!-- para 16 -->
**Skills-based investments.** A firm's decision to use NCAs, and NCAs' contribution to value creation and capture, depend on labor and product-market characteristics and overall strategy.
If employees are both willing and able to finance the necessary skills-based human capital investments, NCAs are unnecessary (Meccheri, 2009).
Prospective employees may acquire necessary training through self- or government-sponsored education or under employee training pay-back agreements (TRAPs) (Long, 2004).
For example, accounting firms often finance accounting certification programs subject to TRAPs.
Most jurisdictions allowing TRAPs require that the training is voluntary, discrete, quantifiable, and portable (Lester, 2001; Long, 2004).
Moreover, firm investments in general training—the target of TRAPs—are generally not considered sufficient for NCA enforcement (Long, 2004).
<!-- para 17 -->
The issue is that most required human capital investments are continuous, comprise a mix of general and specific investments, and are difficult to quantify, while many employees are unable or unwilling to finance the human capital desired by firms, even if this could be quantified (Lester, 2001; Posner et al., 2004).
It is also important to remember that the prophesized benefit of NCAs is protection against loss of returns to human capital investments, which include, but are not limited to, the costs.
For example, many large consulting firms, especially in management, IT, and finance, invest heavily in training new hires in specific tools and methodologies, but they also invest heavily in continuous sector-specific training and team buildup.
The diversity of the training and the integration with the employees' productive work make it difficult to quantify and thus justify that the employee pay directly for such investments.
For firms building up skills within teams, the potential losses associated with mobility can greatly outstrip the costs (Mawdsley & Somaya, 2016).
<!-- para 18 -->
Thus, for firms in labor markets that do not supply individuals with the required skills, the theoretical NCA benefits of increasing investments in employee training and productivity, as envisioned by NCA proponents within the social welfare perspective, are aligned with the firm perspective.
The actual benefits, however, depend on whether NCAs are complementary, contradictory, or superfluous to the wide array of value-appropriation mechanisms available to the firm.
These include other legal mechanisms, such as non-solicitation clauses (Balasubramanian, Starr & Yamaguchi, 2024), compensation mechanisms (Callahan, 2024; Kräkel & Sliwka, 2009), and strategic mechanisms, such as strategic complementarity and firm-specificity, which can limit both the real and the perceived value of the employee outside the focal firm and thus limit mobility or the costs of mobility (Campbell, Ganco & Franco, 2012; Coff & Raffiee, 2015).
In some cases, NCAs may be entirely redundant, offering no additional benefit because other mechanisms sufficiently protect firm interests.
<!-- para 19 -->
**Knowledge-based investments.** In contrast to firm-sponsored investments in employee skill-based human capital, knowledge-based investments are often made through passive exposure to firm know-how.
In this sense, by protecting against knowledge outflows, NCAs facilitate and encourage the removal of barriers to internal knowledge flows (Seo & Somaya, 2022).
This can reduce costs for the firm (i.e., value capture), and increase collaboration and innovation (i.e., value creation) (Seo & Somaya, 2022; Xiao, 2022).
<!-- para 20 -->
Whether the firm extracts additional value from NCAs is again related to the extent to which NCAs complement, contradict, or are superfluous to other value-appropriation mechanisms (Balasubramanian et al., 2024).
The legal and strategic value-appropriation mechanisms discussed above are relevant here too, but more important to knowledge-based investments are perhaps nondisclosure clauses, patents, inventor assignment agreements, and trade secrets protections.
When a firm's knowledge-based investments are tied to discrete and codifiable innovation and the firm has the resources to monitor and enforce patents, NCAs are unlikely to add the prophesized value to either the firm or society.
<!-- para 21 -->
For other types of knowledge-based investments, NCAs, proponents argue, hold many advantages (Barnett & Sichelman, 2020).
As compared to trade secret protection or nondisclosure agreements, NCA breach is easier to detect.
An employee's move to a competitor is generally visible, while disclosure of a trade secret can be difficult to determine (Lester, 2001).
The burden of proof in trade secret cases is onerous, requiring the firm to prove transmission of the trade secret, the secret's distinct value, and the firm's reasonable efforts to preserve secrecy (Posner et al., 2004; Rubin & Shedd, 1981).
Challenging a trade secret law breach may require public disclosure of confidential information (Posner et al., 2004).
<!-- para 22 -->
Furthermore, NCAs provide a unique form of injunctive relief that helps avert irreversible damage, which is essential in cases where quantifying damages is challenging and compensation is inadequate.
For instance, consider a startup creating hard-to-patent software requiring extensive R&D for a niche market.
If a larger company recruits the startup's main developers, monopolizes the market, and utilizes the acquired knowledge, the startup will likely fail, unable to contest the transfer or recover losses.
Therefore, beyond mere deterrence, NCAs can be instrumental for firms aiming to safeguard their competitive position.
<!-- para 23 -->
Moreover, it is also important to emphasize again that most human capital investments contain elements of both general and specific skills, often leaning toward industry-specific knowledge (Posner et al., 2004).
This blend makes them valuable to competitors, thereby intensifying potential losses when they do occur (Meese, 2022).
Recalling that NCAs are limited in scope, geography, and duration, the provision of knowledge-based investments might ultimately impose a net loss to firms if the value to competitors remains beyond the NCA's durational and geographical coverage.
<!-- para 24 -->
Related to this is the consideration of whether the NCA will negatively affect employee morale and demotivate staff.
Here, the role of the employee, the perceived benefit of the NCA, and the general characteristics of the employee (e.g., family status, gender, and preference for an NCA) are all factors to consider.
Therefore, NCAs' contribution to value creation largely depends on the necessity to safeguard human capital investments, the nature of these investments, and the spectrum of available protective measures.

## methods
<!-- para 3 -->
A critical aspect of assessing the real-world implications of theoretical NCA costs and benefits is to gauge their potential magnitude.
One might ask: Are NCAs merely a niche phenomenon, or do NCAs fundamentally impact numerous employee–employer relationships?
Accurately determining NCA prevalence presents a challenge, as neither employees nor employers typically have an obligation to publicly disclose NCA usage.
However, a review of published cases and studies suggests that NCAs are nearly ubiquitous in employment contracts across all skill levels, industries, and developed countries.

<!-- para 9 -->
The extant literature generally takes two approaches to measure NCA effects: (1) through enforcement policy differences and (2) through NCA use differences.
NCA enforcement policy studies exploit the jurisdiction-level variation in NCA enforcement and changes to NCA enforcement policy to identify relationships between NCAs and employee, firm, and societal outcomes.
Most U.S. states enforce NCAs (prior to the federal ban); however, states vary in the extent of their NCA enforcement.
For instance, states that mandate employers to provide employees with consideration (i.e., additional compensation) for signing NCAs and states that restrict the types of employees that employers can bind with NCAs are deemed weak-enforcing (Prescott et al., 2016; Starr, 2019; Starr et al., 2021).
States that impose no restrictions beyond the general requirement of reasonableness are deemed strong-enforcing (Prescott et al., 2016; Starr et al., 2021).
The few states that impose bans or significant restrictions on NCAs, such as California, Minnesota, and North Dakota, are labeled non-enforcing (Prescott et al., 2016).
NCA enforcement policy studies posit that by controlling for confounding factors, differences in employee, firm, or societal outcomes can be attributed to NCA enforcement by courts.
Most early NCA studies, particularly those reporting negative effects, rely on enforcement policy data.
<!-- para 10 -->
Studies on NCA use rely on survey data or data extracted from reviewing employee contracts either manually or using text recognition software to determine whether employees have agreed to NCAs (e.g., Johnson & Lipsitz, 2022; Kini et al., 2021; Lavetti et al., 2020; Starr et al., 2020).
The idea behind NCA use studies is the same as that of enforcement policy studies; that is, by controlling for confounding factors, we can explore the differences in NCA use to determine NCAs' effects.
Earlier NCA use studies examine CEO contracts at publicly traded firms (e.g., Garmaise, 2011), while more recent studies use ad hoc survey data (e.g., Johnson & Lipsitz, 2022; Lavetti et al., 2020; Starr et al., 2021).
<!-- para 11 -->
The following sections present influential NCA studies utilizing both methodologies.
We begin with NCAs' effects on mobility and firm investments, as these foundational effects influence virtually all subsequent outcomes.
Next, we present NCAs' impact on firm performance and social welfare outcomes.
We then synthesize the current research and assess whether the current state of the literature supports the NCA restriction trend.
<!-- para 13 -->
**Mobility.** For NCAs to have any effect, they must limit labor mobility, or at least be perceived to do so.
A seminal study by Fallick, Fleischman, and Rebitzer (2006) reports lower levels of interfirm mobility among college-educated male computer industry employees in states enforcing NCAs compared to California, where NCAs are not enforced.
In another seminal study, Marx, Strumsky, and Fleming (2009) compare inventor mobility in Michigan before and after the 1985 NCA enforcement repeal, finding a 8.1% mobility drop post-1985, with larger drops for inventors with more firm-specific knowledge or skills.
Similarly, Garmaise's (2011) time-series and cross-sectional U.S. NCA enforcement study finds that increases in enforcement reduce rates of within-industry executive mobility.
Outside the United States, Young (2024) finds an increase in low-wage worker mobility following an Austrian NCA ban on this demographic.
<!-- para 14 -->
Following these initial NCA enforcement mobility studies, Evan Starr published several papers with various coauthors based on an NCA employee survey (hereafter referred to as the 2014 NSP [Noncompete Survey Project]).
Although exact estimates vary due to different model specifications, the 2014 NSP studies agree that NCAs, in terms of both use and enforceability, are linked with higher employee tenure and lower employee mobility (e.g., Starr, 2019; Starr, Ganco & Campbell, 2018; Starr et al., 2019; Starr et al., 2020).
<!-- para 15 -->
Somewhat in contrast, Ertimur, Rawson, Rogers, and Zechman (2018) fail to find evidence that strong NCA enforcement increases the likelihood or lengths of externally hired CEO career gaps.
Similarly, Jeffers (2024), relying on LinkedIn data, finds no overall NCA enforceability effect on mobility, except for occupations requiring a bachelor's degree or higher, where she finds a 7%–11% decrease in mobility associated with increased NCA enforceability.
<!-- para 16 -->
**Firm investments.** A key debate centers on whether NCAs lead to the anticipated increase in firm-sponsored human capital, which is the foundation of NCAs' proposed social utility.
While empirical data are sparse, one study by Starr (2019) finds a positive association between NCA use and training.
Nonetheless, it is difficult to identify whether the increase in training is due to NCAs or the higher education levels and other attributes of employees typically subject to NCAs.
Moreover, as referred to above, Jeffers (2024) suggests that increased NCA enforcement incentivizes greater physical capital investments due to the complementarity between physical and human capital assets, particularly in knowledge-intensive firms.
<!-- para 18 -->
**Firm productivity.** Given that NCAs are argued to provide incentives for efficient firm-sponsored human capital, and the large number of studies that consistently show a strong positive association between efficient firm-sponsored human capital investments and firm productivity (e.g., Almeida & Carneiro, 2009; Bassanini, Booth, Brunello, De & Leuven, 2005; Conti, 2005; Frazis & Loewenstein, 2005; Siegel & Simons, 2010), we should see a positive association between NCAs and firm productivity.
<!-- para 19 -->
Of the two studies that examine NCA enforcement and productivity, Garmaise (2011) finds no association, and Anand et al. (2018) find a negative association.
Garmaise (2011) theorizes that the lack of association results from increased firm incentives to invest in employee human capital being offset by decreased employee incentives to invest in their own human capital.
Conversely, Anand et al. (2018) theorize that strict enforceability raises firm costs through increased search costs and lowered employee morale, leading to reduced productivity.
Notably, both studies lack data on potential mechanisms and cannot isolate specific value-creation and productivity effects associated with NCA use.
<!-- para 20 -->
In addition, two experimental studies examine NCAs and employee effort, theorizing effects on productivity.
Amir and Lobel (2010) find that NCAs lead to less determination and engagement, and higher failure rates, in tasks requiring pure effort.
In tasks involving talent and creativity, engagement is still lower, but performance quality remains the same (Amir & Lobel, 2010).
Amir and Lobel (2010) posit that firms using NCAs may suffer from lower employee performance, and thus productivity.
Buenstorf, Engel, Fischer, and Guth (2016), using similar experimental methods but allowing for wage adjustments, find no reduction in effort with NCAs.
Buenstorf et al. (2016) contend that employers offset potential effort reductions by offering higher wages—a finding somewhat supported by other studies on NCA use and wages (i.e., wages are higher for employees with NCAs) (Kini et al., 2021; Lavetti et al., 2020; Rothstein & Starr, 2022; Starr, 2019).
<!-- para 21 -->
**CEO behavior and firm-level outcomes.** A niche body of literature investigates NCAs, CEO behavior, and firm-level outcomes, revealing mixed evidence.
Arslan-Ayaydin, Bishara, Thewissen, and Torsin (2020) report an increase in inflating press release tones, which is considered unethical behavior, associated with increased NCA enforcement.
Chen, Zhang, and Zhou (2018) find that increases in NCA enforcement lead CEOs to focus on short-term benchmarks and inflate performance, ultimately harming long-term performance.
<!-- para 22 -->
Conversely, other studies report positive NCA effects on CEO behavior and firm outcomes.
Tang, Wang, and Zhou (2020) find a positive association between increased NCA enforcement, expectation management, and CEO performance, while Gao, Guo, Lisic, and Omer (2023) report that increases in NCA enforcement reduce exploitation of insider information and thus reduce unethical CEO behavior and future harm to the firm.

**Entrepreneurship.** Several studies suggest that NCAs influence firm entry and entrepreneurship decisions.
Silicon Valley's high entrepreneurship rate is often cited as evidence that NCAs are unnecessary obstacles to entrepreneurial growth (Gilson, 1999; Hyde, 2010).
Many firms operating in Silicon Valley are "spinouts," which are firms created by the ex-employees of incumbent firms in the same industry, as opposed to spinoffs, which are formed on the firms' initiative (Franco & Mitchell, 2008).
The contention, therefore, is that NCAs, by tethering the employee to the firm, establish barriers for spinouts.
Indeed, Franco and Mitchell's (2008) model simulation finds that fewer spinouts resulted from Massachusetts's strong NCA enforcement (i.e., Route 128) compared with California's non-enforcement (i.e., Silicon Valley).
Other studies by Starr, Balasubramanian, and Sakakibara (2018) and Baslandze (2022) also report negative associations between strong NCA enforcement and spinouts.
Similarly, using U.S. panel data, Stuart and Sorenson (2003) find that strong NCA enforcement diminishes the entrepreneurial boost typically spurred by initial public offerings and acquisitions, and Samila and Sorenson (2011) find that the effect of entrepreneurial stimulation via venture capital influxes also diminishes with stronger NCA enforcement.
<!-- para 32 -->
Other studies focus on various qualitative aspects of new firm entry.
Kang and Fleming (2020) report a relative reduction in small firm establishment in Florida following increased NCA enforceability.
Can and Fossen (2022) find that decreased NCA enforcement in Utah increased both incorporated and unincorporated entrepreneurship; however, in Massachusetts, only unincorporated entrepreneurship increased.
Marx (2022) reports that female entrepreneurship is negatively affected by increased NCA enforceability.
<!-- para 33 -->
In contrast to the general finding of a negative relationship between NCA enforcement and entrepreneurship, Carlino (2021) reports that Michigan's NCA policy change to increase enforcement had no effect on startup entry rates and a positive effect on startup job creation.
Jeffers (2024) also finds no statistically significant effect of increased NCA enforcement on overall startup rates (i.e., rate of creation of new company pages on LinkedIn), although she reports a negative effect for startup rates in the knowledge sector.
<!-- para 34 -->
Moreover, although the above study by Starr, Balasubramanian, and Sakakibara (2018) is most cited for the negative NCA enforcement effects on within-industry spinouts, Starr et al. also find that NCA enforcement improved the quality and the average per-firm employment rate at new firms.
Similarly, Kang and Fleming (2020) report an increase in large firm establishment and an overall increase in job creation by new firms following an increase in NCA enforceability in Florida, despite the reduction of small firm entry.

<!-- para 44 -->
The empirical literature on NCAs has grown significantly over the past decade.
While certain relationships have become relatively more well-established along with the growth in the literature, such as the relationship between NCAs and increased employee tenure, other relationships have become less established, such as those between NCAs and innovation, entrepreneurship, and wages.
The following section evaluates the current state of the empirical literature on NCAs as it relates to our overall framework of NCAs' contribution to value creation and capture from both the firm and the societal perspective.
<!-- para 47 -->
Most NCA literature, especially the early influential studies, rely on NCA enforcement policy rather than use differences as the key independent variable for identifying NCA effects.
As the NCA literature evolves, the deficiencies of relying solely on enforcement policy methodology become increasingly evident and difficult to justify.
<!-- para 48 -->
First, a major deficiency results from the use of variations in enforcement policy as a proxy for variations in NCA use.
Although some studies find a correlation between variations in NCA enforceability and NCA use rates (e.g., Bishara, 2011), most studies find high NCA use rates in California and North Dakota, which are classified as non-enforcing (e.g., Colvin & Shierholz, 2019; Garmaise, 2011; Ghosh & Shankar, 2017; Gillan, Hartzell & Parrino, 2009; Kaplan & Strömberg, 2003; Starr et al., 2021).
This would be unproblematic if enforceability was the only factor that affects behavior, but Starr et al. (2020) present convincing evidence that unenforceable NCAs also decrease labor mobility, which is the main underlying effect of almost all of the enforcement-level studies (i.e., less-mobile employees mean less knowledge spillovers, spinouts, etc.).
Some scholars argue that the combination of findings does not discredit the findings of NCA enforcement studies, but instead provides evidence that extant NCA enforcement studies likely underestimate NCAs' effects (Starr et al., 2020).
However, we lack solid empirical evidence to confirm this assertion.
<!-- para 49 -->
Moreover, legal scholars Barnett and Sichelman (2016) present convincing arguments that most NCA enforcement studies misclassify states in their assignments to weak, strong, or non-enforcing jurisdictions, and misrepresent NCA policy changes as natural experiments (i.e., the changes were not significant or were not exogenous).
The extant NCA literature has yet to provide a satisfactory response to this criticism.
While we acknowledge the breadth and depth of the critique, we do not explore it in detail due to its expansive nature and the comprehensive analysis it warrants.
<!-- para 50 -->
It should, however, be noted that most enforcement indices used in the NCA literature are derived, at least in part, from various editions of Malsberger's (2020) state-by-state NCA survey, which was first published in 1991.
The book is a survey in the broadest sense of the word and serves as a guide for legal practitioners navigating the law on NCAs in the various states.
Yet, as most legal practitioners know, context plays a significant role in the interpretation of statute and legal precedent, and it is difficult to reduce NCA enforceability to a numerical scale with any level of precision.
For instance, the Texas Supreme Court in Light vs. Centel Cellular Co. of Texas [^6] rejected the enforceability of an NCA for a radio technician, finding it excessively restrictive and not narrowly tailored enough to protect legitimate business interests.
Conversely, in Mann Frankfort Stein & Lipp Advisors, Inc. vs. Fielding [^7], the same court upheld an NCA for a partner in an accounting firm, emphasizing the employer's legitimate business interest in protecting its investment in specialized training and customer relationships.
Indeed, the sentiment among those who deal with NCA litigation is that “no category of litigation is less predictable” (Hyde, 2010).
Instead, legal practitioners use such guides to draft legal memorandums to clients indicating how their case might be judged based on the applicable statutes and legal precedents, or as starting points to build their case in the event of NCA litigation.
<!-- para 51 -->
Although some studies acknowledge the weaknesses in enforcement studies in terms of using NCA enforcement as a proxy for use, and point out the need to ensure that enforcement is not used alone in assessing the NCA effects (e.g., Rothstein & Starr, 2022), most studies of NCA enforcement do not acknowledge the weaknesses of this approach, and thus do not accommodate for the weaknesses in modeling or interpreting the effects of NCAs.
<!-- para 52 -->
The major issue is that the current methodology prevents us from gaining an in-depth understanding of the effects of NCAs.
In other words, we have some evidence that states with or that switch to stronger NCA enforcement experience fewer spinouts (see Table 3), but even if we trust the enforceability scales, we do not know whether this finding is due to NCA use.
This becomes even more problematic in the face of contradictory evidence (e.g., Carlino, 2021; Jeffers, 2024 in Table 3 above).
We also have evidence that strong NCA enforcement is linked to decreased firm productivity for groups of firms in the aggregate (e.g., Anand et al., 2018; Garmaise, 2011), but we do not know whether this is driven by the firms using NCAs or the firms that are not using NCAs, or whether NCA use matters at all.
<!-- para 53 -->
Moreover, strong NCA enforceability is associated with lower wages for employees, and NCA use is associated with higher wages (see Table 1).
Here, we do not know whether the wage puzzle results from the methodology, timeframe, or populations studied.
We also lack information on whether contended wage increases following bans on certain employee classes are due to the unfair usurpation of one firm's investments in employee human capital by another, which is not welfare-increasing (Aydinliyim, 2022; Meese, 2022).
A major concern here is avoiding biases of selection effects in our determination of the impact of NCAs, which is difficult to do using aggregate enforcement-level data or employee survey data alone.
<!-- para 54 -->
Notably, the current narrative in the media, and the one embraced by policymakers, is that a ban on NCAs is justified as NCAs hold down wages—which, as should be made clear from the arguments above, there is not enough evidence to justify.
Hence, many theoretical gaps in the literature are intricately linked to the methodological shortcomings in the NCA literature.
<!-- para 56 -->
The dearth of studies on NCA use and overreliance on enforcement-level studies and so-called natural experiments present serious challenges for the formulation and implementation of NCA policy.
Many NCA bans and proposed restrictions on enforcement do not come with the means to detect unenforceable NCAs, nor significant enough penalties to discourage the use of unenforceable NCAs. [^8] This is significant, since, when we look closely, we see that the negative effects attributed to NCAs often originate from NCA misuse and abuse, rather than legitimate application and enforcement, which raises critical questions about the core drivers of the issues that NCA opponents associate with NCAs.
<!-- para 57 -->
Notably, most jurisdictions that enforce NCAs, both in the United States and globally, already extensively limit NCA enforceability under the doctrine of reasonableness.
As referred to above, this rule ensures that courts will only enforce an NCA if it is reasonable and necessary to protect the legitimate business interests of the firm seeking enforcement.
Such a framework guards against the enforcement of NCAs that are excessively long, geographically wide, or professionally broad, rendering NCAs effectively unenforceable for most low-skilled and otherwise vulnerable workers.
Nonetheless, in nearly all studies measuring NCA prevalence, in the United States and abroad, NCAs are used in contracts with low-skilled, vulnerable workers whose mobility is inconsequential to the employer's business interests.
<!-- para 58 -->
A blanket ban that lacks the means to detect and impose additional and meaningful penalties for the use of unenforceable NCAs is unlikely to improve the situation of NCA misuse.
Presently, most proposed NCA bans do not incorporate significant enough penalties to deter the use of unenforceable NCAs.
This oversight presents a significant policy loophole that allows employers to continue using unenforceable NCAs without consequence.
Therefore, introducing policies for routine reviews of NCAs and penalties for NCA misuse could prove to be a more effective policy tool for addressing NCA-related issues.
<!-- para 59 -->
Moreover, in many instances, issues attributed to NCAs extend beyond NCAs to more encompassing forms of employee maltreatment.
A simple ban on NCAs fails to address these issues.
In particular, the core difficulties often lie within broader labor conditions, rather than the NCAs themselves.
For example, an infamous story circulating in the media features a woman unable to escape sexual harassment at her workplace due to her NCA (see e.g., Willingham, 2019).
While this situation is distressing, the problem lies not with the NCA, but with the sexual harassment itself and the aspects of the U.S. legal system that undermine employee empowerment.
<!-- para 60 -->
Considering the paucity of studies on NCA use, especially at the firm level, coupled with doubts about the reliability of enforcement scales and debates over the scope and impact of enforcement changes, the research supporting NCA bans is significantly challenged.
<!-- para 62 -->
Echoing concerns about relying on enforcement data, many NCA studies examining innovation or entrepreneurship utilize the more easily accessible data, such as enforceability scales, patent counts, new firm startups, and publicly traded companies' information.
However, while convenient, these data often fall short of accurately capturing NCAs' nuanced impact.
Notably, while patents often serve as a decent proxy for innovation in other contexts, patents are ill-suited for capturing nuanced NCA effects (Barnett & Sichelman, 2016).
<!-- para 63 -->
NCAs' real power lies in their ability to stimulate efficient human capital investments and spur internal innovations—factors that are challenging to measure empirically.
While some investments manifest through structured training programs, the more impactful investments tend to be intangible and tacit and emerge from deeper immersion in firm strategy, culture, routines, and invaluable on-the-job experiences.
The lack of straightforward accounting measures of these activities makes them elusive for aggregate-level determination.
<!-- para 64 -->
Furthermore, the innovation that results from these activities is also not well-documented, leading to an incomplete portrayal of NCAs' impact.
Firms that develop new products, processes, or innovations internally often do not document this in a way that researchers can use.
Likewise, firms that secure human capital investments through NCAs have less incentive to patent, and firms with strong patent protections have less incentive to use NCAs (Barnett & Sichelman, 2016, 2020).
Essentially, firms that can patent will patent; firms that cannot patent, due to the nature of their IP, rely on measures such as NCAs, trade secrets, and non-solicitation- and non-poaching-type clauses (Barnett & Sichelman, 2016).
Indeed, Kang and Lee (2022) find that firms rely more on patenting relative to trade secrets following decreased NCA enforcement.
Patents, as opposed to risky internal human capital investments and innovations, can be measured numerically, and patent data are easily obtained—so, too, are data on spinouts.
As these substitutes (i.e., patents instead of NCAs, and spinouts instead of internal firm innovation) are easily documented, studies relying on patent and spinout data are somewhat predisposed to detect adverse outcomes.
<!-- para 65 -->
Despite the overreliance on patent data as proxies for innovation, and the related preponderance against finding positive effects, the most recent literature actually supports NCAs as facilitators of overall innovation (Seo & Somaya, 2022; Xiao, 2022).
Notably, Xiao (2022) avoids the issue of patents altogether by using data from FDA applications for medical devices, while Seo and Somaya's (2022) research design allows them to go beyond raw patent counts to uncover NCAs' collaboration-inducing effects.
<!-- para 67 -->
The prevailing methodological bias in empirical research has serious policy implications resulting in a distorted depiction of NCAs, wherein potential advantages are either entirely overlooked or unjustifiably downplayed.
This distorted view may lead policymakers to conclude that jurisdictions can thrive without NCAs, fostering a dismissive attitude toward their potential benefits.
Such conclusions are often justified by reference to the success of Silicon Valley, which is in a region known for non-enforcement of NCAs.
This position, however, fails to consider that NCA use rates in California are higher than the average enforcing jurisdiction (Colvin & Shierholz, 2019; Prescott et al., 2016) and the absence of a genuine counterfactual scenario.
Stated differently, it leaves unanswered the question of whether a distinct cultural and economic context like California would demonstrate better performance if NCAs were enforceable.
<!-- para 68 -->
This underrepresentation of positive effects may precipitate unintended policy consequences as it is used to justify bans and, even without bans, discourage firms from using NCAs for fear of public backlash.
Firms might shift strategic focus toward investments that can be safeguarded via more tangible legal instruments, such as patents and trademarks.
Such a shift could drive a transition from human capital-intensive to physical capital-intensive investments and innovations (see Jeffers, 2024).
In scenarios where employee mobility incurs substantial costs, it could accelerate the pace of job automation; additionally, it might divert investments away from industry-specific and niche inventions.
<!-- para 69 -->
While this shift might be inconsequential for some firms, for others the shift might be either prohibitively expensive or incompatible with their operations.
For small entrepreneurs working on industry-specific inventions for niche markets, NCAs are often the optimal protective mechanism when patenting is not an option (Carlino, 2021; Kang & Fleming, 2020).
Establishing trade secret violations or breaches of nondisclosure agreements is a complex and costly process (Pfeifer, 2024).
Moreover, when damages occur, they are often irreversible and can precipitate entrepreneurial failure, especially for smaller firms with limited resources (Pfeifer, 2024).
<!-- para 70 -->
Furthermore, in markets characterized by financially constrained individuals, the combination of minimum-wage laws and NCA prohibitions might impede these individuals' ability to acquire the general and industry-specific skills, such as trade skills, needed to make them attractive to the labor market.
Concurrently, firms might be disinclined to invest in the development of less productive workers (Pfeifer, 2024; Starr, Ganco & Campbell, 2018), given the inability to secure returns on their investments due to NCA restrictions.
<!-- para 71 -->
These potential consequences underscore the need for comprehensive research that can inform policy decisions about NCAs.
While these potential scenarios are not definitively proven due to the dearth of studies, their existence and possible impacts warrant scholarly attention to establish a balanced understanding NCAs.
<!-- para 73 -->
The majority of empirical research on NCAs is based on U.S. data.
Although U.S. state variation offers a fertile ground for NCA research, it also introduces limitations to the interpretation and global applicability of findings.
As mentioned above, a significant limitation is the lack of clarity regarding whether enforcement policy studies are correctly specified and thus whether findings from aggregate-level NCA studies actually result from NCAs or something else.
Notably, judicial interpretation and legislative changes, being inherently political processes, are seldom isolated events (Bonica & Sen, 2021).
This politically contingent nature of enforcement in the United States, along NCA usage variation across states, often makes it challenging to draw reliable conclusions on NCA policy effects (Barnett & Sichelman, 2020; Starr, Ganco & Campbell, 2018).
<!-- para 74 -->
Further, the differences in enforcement in the United States impose limitations in terms of interpreting national-level NCA use surveys.
Surveys conducted nationally or across states often result in relatively few observations per state (Barnett & Sichelman, 2020).
When we combine the variation in enforcement with the variation in use in the different states, it becomes more difficult to draw meaningful inferences, even with the help of statistical techniques (Barnett & Sichelman, 2020).
Ideally, we should have either sufficiently large sample sizes or studies from outside the United States that avoid the issue of enforcement variability.
<!-- para 76 -->
As the United States is distinct in terms of its configuration of robust IP protection laws and relatively limited employee protection measures, [^9] NCA studies based on U.S. data may not readily apply to other jurisdictions.
U.S. labor law starkly contrasts with other Western jurisdictions, which necessitate written employment contracts, recognize a right to work, and deliberately favor employee rights over employer rights.
For example, the United States is often characterized by at-will employment, which allows for employment termination by either party without notice or cause—a practice scarcely used in other countries.
Further, employee-friendly tendencies extend directly to judicial NCA interpretation, leaving enforcement of oppressive NCAs even less likely outside the United States (see Murphy, 2015).
<!-- para 77 -->
Moreover, Europe’s relatively weaker IP protection laws make the patenting of certain types of inventions more challenging, thereby limiting the alternative mechanisms for protecting internal firm investments that could be compromised by employee mobility.
Thus NCAs’ potential benefits in facilitating investments in risky, human-capital-intensive and non-patentable innovations might be greater in Europe than in the United States.
<!-- para 78 -->
Despite these discrepancies, U.S.-based research has substantially influenced changes to international NCA policy.
The effects of these discrepancies might reflect variations in degree rather than fundamental differences; however, they could shift the overall assessment of NCAs' net impact from negative to positive.
Understanding the potential outcomes of removing NCAs from firms' strategic options necessitates a broader global research scope on NCAs' prevalence and impacts.
This expanded perspective is crucial for making informed decisions that reflect the diverse labor market landscapes worldwide.
<!-- para 80 -->
Just 15 years ago, the NCA literature was scant and split distinctly into two factions: theoretical proponents of NCAs from law and economics, and anecdotal detractors from these fields and sociology.
The theoretical arguments were compelling, yet the narratives offered by the detractors presented a persuasive counterpoint.
This emphasized the need for empirical evidence to reconcile these divergent views, especially in light of the growing dependence of the economy on human capital and the related risks (Coff, 1997).
<!-- para 81 -->
Since then, NCA empirical literature has expanded from a handful of published studies pre-2010 to over 30 studies post-2010, many of which have been published in top sociology, strategy and management, law and economics, finance, and accounting journals.
Despite this growth, two camps remain.
Although there are empirical studies to support both, NCA opponents are the current front-runners.
What are we to make of this?
Which camp is right?
These are the questions that have guided this study.
The overarching answer is that we still do not have a definitive answer.
Instead, our analyses demonstrate that NCAs' value, whether assessed from the perspective of firm performance or that of social welfare, depends on numerous factors.
The presence or absence of these factors dictates how we should research NCAs, interpret the findings of NCA studies, and ultimately evaluate the net benefit of NCAs.
<!-- para 83 -->
More research is still needed to move us toward a position where we can give informed advice to firms as to NCAs' relative costs and benefits with respect to other human capital protection mechanisms, and where we can give informed advice to policymakers regarding NCAs' socioeconomic value.

Importantly, to address many of the outstanding questions presented in Table 5, we need more research that identifies incidences of use combined with quality data on the outcomes studied.
Specifically, we need more firm-level studies on NCA use.
Firms in all industries use NCAs, and each firm's reason for using NCAs likely varies along with the industry, investment type the firm seeks to protect, and other market characteristics.
Likewise, the outcomes for individuals, firms, and society differ depending on these context-specific factors, which we still do not know much about.
<!-- para 91 -->
For firms in fast-paced innovative manufacturing industries, where patents provide significant protection, removing NCAs may not have a major impact on the firms' productivity and would only serve to prevent negative mobility externalities.
This may not be the case for firms in other industries that might resort to other measures to retain human capital investments and deter mobility (Pfeifer, 2024).
The other mechanisms might be more like “carrots” with potential positive externalities, or they may be more like “sticks” with potentially greater negative externalities.
Indeed, the ban on NCAs in California did not stop interested firms from using NCAs, nor from finding other ways to deter mobility, and the alternative was secret, collusive, and illegal non-poaching pacts among the major firms in the software industry (Krueger & Posner, 2018).
The same behavior was observed in Norway, with an increase in illegal non-poaching agreements following increased restrictions on NCAs and other restrictive covenants (Skogli et al., 2023).
Crafting optimal human capital protection policy demands a better understanding of firm-level NCA use.
<!-- para 92 -->
Furthermore, if we are to fully understand NCAs' impact, it is essential to go beyond aggregate data and explore alternative research methods.
For instance, qualitative data collection is better suited to capturing NCAs' interaction with firm strategy and organizational structure, and also for identifying where and when NCAs add value.
Qualitative methods might also be better suited for capturing the less salient costs and benefits NCAs and measuring the differential effects on various stakeholders.
<!-- para 93 -->
Given the influence of economic freedom on entrepreneurship and other business outcomes (Bradley & Klein, 2016), more research is needed to explore the extent to which NCAs restrict freedom.
This includes the freedom of employees to choose NCAs, or to select into firms that use NCAs in return for firm-sponsored investments.
Scholars can also build on the burgeoning research on an ethical case for NCAs (Aydinliyim, 2022).
<!-- para 94 -->
Finally, we need more research from outside the United States.
Austria, Denmark, Norway, and the United Kingdom are examples of the many countries that implemented NCA policy changes in reaction to U.S. research without having data on NCA use or effects in their markets.
As stated above, it might be that NCAs function in the same way in other countries as they do in the United States, but we cannot make such an assumption, and future studies are needed.
<!-- para 96 -->
NCAs, by limiting the ability of employees to move to competitors, hold promise of providing incentives for firms to make socially efficient investments in employee human capital.
However, as suggested in the extant NCA literature, NCAs may also create negative externalities that are ultimately harmful to society.
This article provides a much-needed overview and assessment of NCAs' overall impact.
This article does not, however, seek to champion or condemn NCAs, but to call for deeper inquiry.
Our assessment makes clear that the current state of research does not support unequivocal positions.
The deficiencies and divergences within the literature necessitate a judicious stance, pressing the need for further investigation to bridge these critical knowledge gaps.
