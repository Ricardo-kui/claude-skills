# castellaneta_2017_smj_how_does_trade_secret_legal_protection 句子库存

## introduction
<!-- para 2 -->
The strategy literature has long argued that valuable resources are key for firm sustainable competitive advantage (e.g., Barney, 1991; Mahoney and Pandian, 1992; Rumelt, 1984).
In particular, as world economies become increasingly knowledge-intensive, outperforming rivals depends more and more on firms developing proprietary knowledge (Grant, 1996; Kogut and Zander, 1992).
Yet, the resulting competitive advantage is hardly sustainable if firms fail to protect their know-how by minimizing the threat of imitation by rivals (e.g., Barney, 1991; Mahoney and Pandian, 1992; Rumelt, 1984).
In this respect, the legal appropriability regime—the extent to which the legal system protects and enforces property rights on intangible resources—plays a crucial role.
A large body of work on intellectual property rights (IPRs) has established that a strong legal IPR protection mitigates the risk of imitation of valuable know-how, which ensures that firms can appropriate a greater share of returns from their intangible resources and so increases their market value (Teece, 1986).
However, the extant accounts have mainly focused on the role of patents (e.g., Cockburn and Griliches, 1988; Hall, Jaffe, and Trajtenberg, 2005), which require complete disclosure of the underlying knowledge resource and therefore facilitate the assessment process.
Insights gleaned from this literature might not extend to other forms of IPR protection, such as trade secrets, whose owners are “rewarded for keeping information … away from the public for an unlimited duration” (Risch, 2007: 11).
<!-- para 3 -->
Hence, we ask: How does the legal protection of trade secrets affect firm market value? [^1] Trade secrets—encompassing information on marketing data, manufacturing know-how, chemical formulae, and technical data—are “arguably the most important and most heavily litigated intellectual property rights” (Risch, 2007: 1) and are considered the “crown jewels” of a firm’s intellectual capital (Jorda, 2007).
Yet, researchers have not linked trade secret protection to firm value, even though its effect has been documented with respect to employee mobility (Png and Samila, 2015) and to a firm’s incentives to innovate (Png, 2015) and to cluster (Fosfuri and Rønde, 2004).
To fill this gap, we focus on the market for corporate control, which represents strategic factor markets where bundles of resources are traded and the competitive advantage of firms is monetized (e.g., Barney, 1991; Capron and Shen, 2007; Shen and Reuer, 2005).
<!-- para 4 -->
Against this background, we propose that trade secret protection may affect firm market value in two opposite ways, generating a paradox worth exploring.
On one hand, a stronger protection of a firm's trade secrets might limit the outward flow of information about trade secrets to competitors and thus lessen the risk of misappropriation and imitation.
Hence, the target's value might increase.
On the other hand, trade secret protection might limit the amount of firm-specific information available to potential buyers and thus increase the information asymmetries between buyers and sellers.
Lack of information increases uncertainty about the value of the target firm, motivating buyers to discount their offers as a compensation for pursuing a more uncertain and riskier deal.
Hence, the target's value might decrease.
In sum, trade secret protection may be a double-edged sword for the target firm, creating advantageous information asymmetries vis-à-vis its competitors as well as disadvantageous information asymmetries vis-à-vis its potential buyers.
<!-- para 5 -->
To explore these seemingly contradictory arguments, we examine the heterogeneous effects of trade secret protection on the target's market value.
We propose that the direction of the trade secret protection effect might depend on the target's industry characteristic.
First, an increase in trade secret protection may have a positive effect in industries where the cross-firm mobility of knowledge workers is higher, because a strong protection of trade secrets constrains the amount of knowledge that former employees may transfer to competitors (Fosfuri and Rønde, 2004; Png and Samila, 2015).
Second, an increase in trade secret protection may have a negative effect when the target's industry exhibits (1) substantial resource-value uncertainty and hence detailed information on the target's intangible assets becomes crucial for a proper target evaluation (Barney, 1991) and (2) high risk of making a poor—or "lemon"—investment, especially if a stronger trade secret protection hampers the assessment of target firms (Akerlof, 1970; Coff, 1999).
<!-- para 6 -->
Assessing the impact of trade secret protection on firm market value is empirically challenging because such protection might be endogenous with respect to firm value.
For example, policymakers might enact a law enhancing trade secret protection to improve the state economic conditions—which would affect local firms' value—or the law might result from lobbying efforts by state companies.
Hence, a research design that facilitates a clean causal estimate is central to understanding the impact of trade secret protection on firm market value.
<!-- para 7 -->
We address this empirical challenge by exploiting a quasi-natural experiment provided by the enactment of statutes based on the Uniform Trade Secret Act (UTSA), which occurred in 46 U.S. states between 1975 and 2008.
In this period, trade secret protection increased dramatically (Png, 2015).
Important for our identification strategy, accounts of the political economy of these reforms suggest that their enactment was exogenous to state economic and political characteristics, and was not the result of any lobbying effort (Png, 2015).
Specifically, we test our theory on a sample of 1,890 U.S. firms managed by 132 private equity (PE) firms.
The PE market is an advantageous empirical setting for testing our theory.
PE firms are investors that make profits through buyouts—buying and reselling the control of private companies—over relatively short periods.
In this setting, companies are usually sold at least twice, once when the PE firms buy them and the second time when they resell them.
Unlike in other settings, it is thus possible to assess the change in market value of companies acquired by PE firms through a quasi-experimental methodology—with the “treatment” group composed of target firms located in states that adopted the UTSA during the PE company holding period, and the “control” group composed of target firms located in states that did not.
Hence, we are able to assess the causal impact of trade secret laws on firm value in the context of the market for corporate control.
<!-- para 8 -->
We find support for the notion that the effect of trade secrets protection is contingent in its nature.
On one hand, a stronger protection of trade secrets leads to an increase in firm market value when the risk of mobility of knowledge workers is higher, making the protection of trade secrets beneficial to the target firm.
On the other hand, the effect on firm market value is negative when the target's industry displays more resource-value uncertainty and a higher risk of poor investments.
Overall, the findings in our study suggest that the protection of trade secrets is a double-edged sword and that its effects on firm value are more complex than may have been thought.

## theory
<!-- para 2 -->
In today's knowledge-based economy, competitive advantage depends more and more on firms having and protecting valuable and rare proprietary information, unknown to rivals (e.g., Barney, 1991; Grant, 1996; Kogut and Zander, 1992).
Such information is typically referred to as "trade secrets," a term that indicates a "vast bulk of intangible assets" (Halligan, 2008: 656), which are often considered the "crown jewels" of a firm's intellectual capital, developed over many years through myriad interactions and projects (Jorda, 2007).
In the United States, which constitutes the empirical setting of our paper, the most common definition of trade secret is contained in the UTSA, according to which "a trade secret means information, including a formula, pattern, compilation, program, device, method, technique, or process, that (1) derives independent economic value, actual or potential, from not being generally known to, or readily ascertainable by proper means by other persons who might obtain economic value from its disclosure or use; and (2) is the subject of efforts that are reasonable under the circumstances to maintain its secrecy." That is, information of nearly any subject matter can qualify and be protected as a trade secret, including drawings and designs, prototypes, manufacturing processes, not-patentable or not-patented inventions, know-how, formulae or recipes, genetic materials and fragrances, commercial secrets, customer and supplier lists, business methods and strategies, and cost and price information.
The only requisites for a trade secret receiving legal protection are that (1) the underlying information confer an economic advantage when kept secret and (2) that it be protected from disclosure by reasonable secrecy safeguards.
Given this broad definition, trade secrets make up an average of two-thirds of the value of firms' intangible assets, and as much as 80 percent for companies in knowledge-intensive sectors; publicly traded U.S. companies have been estimated to own $5 trillion worth of trade secrets (Schwarts and Weil, 2010; U.S.

Precisely because of their value, however, trade secrets are prone to misappropriation.
For instance, in a Virginia federal court in September 2011, "Kolon Industries Inc. was held liable to paying DuPont Co. the amount of US$919.9 million for the theft of 149 trade secrets related to the production of Kevlar, a special fiber"—“an average value of$6.3 million per trade secret” (Hall et al., 2012: 8).
More generally, U.S. litigation statistics show a sharp increase in cases of trade secret theft: the number of such cases doubled between 1988–1995 and again between 1995–2004; it is expected to double once more by 2017 (U.S.
Misappropriation implies the acquisition of a trade secret by improper means—for instance, through industrial espionage, fraud, bribery, or the breaching of an obligation of confidence and limited use (Aplin et al., 2012; Lemley, 2008).
In this regard, trade secret law defines the extent to which trade secrets are protected from misappropriation by establishing (1) the scope of trade secret protection, which might extend to any valuable information rather than only to information continuously used in the operation of the business; (2) the remedies available to the injured party, which might include monetary relief—that is, compensation for any loss deriving from the misappropriation—or an injunctive relief—that is, an obligation for the misappropriator either to do something (e.g., to return, withdraw, or destroy any infringing goods or materials embedding trade secrets) or to avoid a certain behavior (e.g., to use trade-secret-related information for a limited amount of time or even permanently); and (3) the harshness of punishment for misappropriation, which might range from monetary penalties even to imprisonment (cf.
Malsberger, Pedowitz, and Brock, 2008; Png, 2015).
<!-- para 4 -->
An increase along any of the aforementioned dimensions can be paramount for a firm's ability to protect its knowledge base and eventually for its competitive advantage (Jorda, 2007; Malsberger et al., 2008).
Notwithstanding the importance of the protection of trade secrets for company value, previous research on the topic is scarce.
Studies have explored the impact of the strength of local trade-secrecy protection on employee mobility (Flammer and Kacperczyk, 2016; Png and Samila, 2015), employee beliefs about obligations to protect trade secrets (Hannah, 2005), as well as firm incentives to innovate and patent (Png, 2015) and firm decisions to cluster (Fosfuri and Rønde, 2004).
A related literature has focused on patents and established a positive impact of patent protection on stock market valuation (i.e., stock price) (Cockburn and Griliches, 1988; Hall, Thoma, and Torrisi, 2009; Hall et al., 2005).
Although insightful overall, these studies shed less light on how the protection of trade secrets might affect firm value specifically.
Trade secrets differ significantly from patents and any other IPRs in that they do not require any disclosure of the underlying knowledge asset that is protected—in fact, owners of trade secrets are often required by law to undertake reasonable steps to conceal them.
Thus, “the trade secret owner is rewarded for keeping information … away from the public for an unlimited duration” (Risch, 2007: 11).
Given the substantial differences between patents and trade secret protection, the impact of the latter on firm market value remains less well understood.
<!-- para 6 -->
One context in which trade secret protection may have a clear effect on firm market value is the market for corporate control.
Scholars in strategy have long established that this is a strategic-factor market where firm assets are traded and priced as a whole (e.g., Barney, 1991; Capron and Shen, 2007; Seth, 1990).
We propose that a stronger protection of trade secrets may trigger countervailing forces, exposing a fundamental tension inherent in the relationship between trade secret protection and firm market value.
On one hand, a stronger trade secret protection may decrease valuable firm-specific information available to competitors—and thus enhance the target's value.
On the other hand, a stronger protection of trade secrets may decrease information about the target and its rivals available to potential buyers—and thus decrease the target's value.
When less able to source private information about knowledge assets held by the target and by its competitors, buyers will tend to discount their offers because of greater uncertainty about the target company's overall value.
Similarly, when uncertainty about the target's value is high, potential buyers may even withdraw from the bidding process, further reducing the target's market value.
<!-- para 7 -->
To investigate the proposed mechanisms, we assess the heterogeneous effects of the impact of trade secret protection on a target's market value.
In what follows, we identify a number of industry contingencies to understand when trade secret protection enhances and when it hinders a target's value.
<!-- para 9 -->
A stronger protection of trade secrets likely increases a target's value by reducing the outward flow of information about such secrets to competitors.
Stronger trade secret protection laws, by ensuring severe punishments for trade secret misappropriation, act to limit the amount of trade-secret-related information available to rivals (Risch, 2007).
Hence, the level of "ex-post competition"—the extent of rivals' imitation—shrinks (Peteraf, 1993).
As a result, companies whose intangible assets are better protected will be more valuable than companies with weaker protections, and the prices that buyers will pay for them will be higher.
We expect this positive effect to be stronger for target companies in industries where mobility of knowledge workers is higher.
First, mobility is a crucial driver of knowledge leakages (Agarwal, Ganco, and Ziedonis, 2009; Almeida and Kogut, 1999), and employee departures, associated with the loss of relevant knowledge to established competitors (Hansen, Nohria, and Tierney, 1999) or start-ups (Kacperczyk, 2012), are an important challenge for any acquirer (Jemison and Sitkin, 1986).
In this respect, trade secret protection may play a crucial role: the stronger such protection, the more that former employees will be constrained in the amount of knowledge they can transfer to a new employer for fear of being sued for and convicted of trade secret misappropriation; for example, for breaching a written confidentiality agreement signed with the former employer.
In some cases, a court may even block employees' departure if this inevitably implies a trade secret disclosure to a rival.
The fact that in over 75 percent of trade secret cases in U.S. state courts and over half the cases in U.S. federal courts, the alleged misappropriator was a former employee (Almeling et al., 2010) testifies to the importance of strong trade secret protection for avoiding mobility-induced knowledge leaks.
<!-- para 10 -->
Second, stronger trade secret protection laws are likely to reduce the availability and attractiveness of external labor-market opportunities for employees in firms subject to such protection.
When substantial penalties for trade secret misappropriation are imposed, employees may even lose their bargaining power vis-à-vis their employers (Fosfuri and Rønde, 2004); this may lead to lower wages and higher firm profitability.
Profitability increases due to loss of employee bargaining power are particularly acute in industries where the risk of inter-firm mobility of knowledge workers is high.
For example, in knowledge-intensive industries, firms give their key workers rewards in order to reduce the risk of mobility and thus avoid trade secret leaks to competitors (e.g., Flammer and Kacperczyk, 2016; Hom and Griffith, 1995).
Yet, when the protection of trade secrets is stronger, pecuniary incentives become less central to a firm's ability to retain knowledge workers—as the employees' bargaining power vis-à-vis their employers will typically decrease.
Even if the risk of employees joining a rival is high, any trade secret misappropriation will be subject to court sanctions.
<!-- para 11 -->
**Hypothesis 1 (H1): The impact of trade secret protection on firm market value will be more positive when the target company operates in an industry characterized by a higher mobility rate of knowledge workers.**
<!-- para 13 -->
Although in some industries trade secret protection might have a positive impact on firm market value, in other industries the impact might be negative, since trade secret protection likely constrains the information about both the target and its competitors available to potential buyers.
The consequent uncertainty about the target value possibly induces bidders to decrease their offers.
<!-- para 14 -->
First, a stronger protection of trade secrets limits the quantity of information about the target firm's knowledge available to potential buyers; a greater share of that information is likely to be concealed and protected via trade secrets, rather than being publicly available.
As the scope of trade secret protection increases, some information formerly without any legal safeguard could become eligible for protection under trade secret laws and therefore more difficult to source.
For instance, U.S. states enacting the UTSA extended the status of trade secrets even to so-called negative know-how—that is, knowledge about attempted but flawed techniques or approaches—which otherwise could not be safeguarded by IPRs (Malsberger et al., 2008).
A stronger punishment for trade secret misappropriation also encourages firms to protect any new, valuable information via trade secrets, which rely on secrecy, instead of via other IPRs (like patents), for which knowledge disclosure is a prerequisite for protection (Png, 2015; Risch, 2007).
<!-- para 15 -->
Some information concealed via trade secrets could still be sourced by the buyers during the due diligence phase, either with the collaboration of the target or privately.
However, it is unlikely that the voluntary knowledge disclosed by the target would increase when trade secrets are better protected.
Irrespective of the strength in trade secret protection, vendors usually disclose to potential buyers only ancillary information about trade secrets (e.g., how many employees know them, or, more generally, the target's policy on trade secrets)—and rarely the core information constituting a trade secret itself—at least before the transaction is concluded. [^2] There is evidence that, given such challenges, bidders tend to solicit a target company's employees or business partners privately in order to obtain data about the company's trade secrets; indeed, bidders might even rely on industrial espionage to source trade-secret-related information (Lamb, 2002). [^3] When the protection of trade secrets is strong, such behaviors are more likely to be considered fraudulent and to be punished by the judicial system (Risch, 2007), such that the quantity of valuable information about the target possibly sourced by potential buyers decreases.
<!-- para 16 -->
Moreover, even in the unlikely case where the buyer, as a result of a stronger trade secret protection, is not worse off in terms of the quantity of information about the target's trade secrets, the quality of such information—its usefulness for assessing the target value—might still decrease, because information about competitors' trade secrets is less available.
Indeed, information about the competitors' assets is core to evaluating the target accurately, because it helps describe the extent to which the target's trade secrets are unique and thus able to confer a sustainable competitive advantage (Peteraf, 1993).
Quaker Oats' 1994 $1.7 billion purchase of Snapple (a beverage company with unique trade secrets) illustrates the consequences of a poor assessment of rivals' trade secrets.
Two years after the purchase, Quaker Oats was forced to resell Snapple for just$300 million, after its brewing technique had been reverse-engineered (and so copied) by several competitors (Lamb, 2002).
This significant loss might have been avoided had Quaker Oats collected better information about the intangible assets of Snapple's competitors.
This example implies that assessing a target firm's trade secrets requires having some knowledge of the trade secrets of the target's competitors.
Thus (although unlikely), even if all trade secrets of the target firm were fully disclosed during due diligence, potential buyers would still be less able to assess their value when the trade secrets of the target's rival are better protected.
<!-- para 17 -->
Whenever information relevant to evaluating the target properly becomes scarce, buyers will likely discount their offers—not only as compensation for pursuing a more uncertain and riskier deal, but also because the risk of adverse selection is higher: to avoid overpaying a target with uncertain value and winding up with a “lemon,” buyers will reduce their bids (Akerlof, 1970; Capron and Shen, 2007; Coff, 1999; Shen and Reuer, 2005).
The lack of information on targets may even discourage potential acquirers from making any offer (Capron and Shen, 2007): any withdrawal from the bidding process will again be reflected in a lower market value.
<!-- para 18 -->
A direct corollary of our argument is the prediction that trade secret protection will have particularly deleterious effects on firm market value in industries where resources' value is already quite uncertain and therefore difficult to estimate without proper information.
Indeed, studies on M&As find that the probability of acquiring private firms—for which limited information is usually available, compared with public firms—decreases when those firms operate in industries where the target resources' value is difficult to assess and forecast, such as those unfamiliar to the acquirer or those that are R&D-intensive (Capron and Shen, 2007; Shen and Reuer, 2005).
<!-- para 19 -->
From the buyers' perspective, having detailed information on the target firm's assets becomes especially useful when the target's industry features high uncertainty about the resource value.
In such an industry, past performance is an unreliable indicator of the target's future performance: not only could competitive position easily be subject to unforeseeable challenges, but it is also unclear whether past performance reflects a target's sustainable competitive advantage or sheer luck (Barney, 1991; Kor, Mahoney, and Watson, 2008).
However, if buyers are able to glean any information about the target's assets—including those protected by trade secrets—industry resource-value uncertainty will pose a lesser challenge to the proper evaluation of the target.
Indeed, firsthand information about knowledge assets will enhance buyers' ability to assess whether past performance reflects the firm's unique and valuable assets—rather than sheer luck—and, most important, whether firm performance can be enhanced and sustained in the long run (Barney, 1991; Kor et al., 2008).
Hence, we expect a stronger trade secret protection—by limiting information on targets' intangible assets—to erode bidders' ability to assess the target, especially when the target operates in an industry exhibiting high resource-value uncertainty.
<!-- para 20 -->
**Hypothesis 2 (H2): The impact of trade secret protection on firm market value will be more negative when the target company operates in an industry characterized by greater resource-value uncertainty.**
<!-- para 21 -->
Relatedly, we expect the effect of trade secret protection to be negative for target firms in industries that exhibit the classic “lemons problem”—that is, a substantial risk for a buyer of incurring an extremely poor deal.
In his seminal model, Akerlof (1970) demonstrated how, when information asymmetries between buyers and sellers are present, buyers will only buy at a discounted price that reflects the average quality of goods traded, and sellers of high-quality goods will have little incentive to trade, such that both prices and average quality continue to spiral down.
<!-- para 22 -->
Strategic management researchers and economists have long linked the challenges of acquisition to the possibility of adverse selection (Capron and Shen, 2007; Mishkin, 1992; Shen and Reuer, 2005).
On one side, potential acquirers struggle to assess a target firm's resource properly; on the other side, sellers have natural incentives to inflate their representation of a firm's quality in order to command a higher sale price.
In the presence of these information asymmetries and the threat of adverse selection, the classic response of potential buyers is to reduce the offer price (Akerlof, 1970; Coff, 1999), and this discount will be proportionate to the presence of lemons in the market (Akerlof, 1970).
As the offers by potential buyers decrease in value, sellers of good-quality firms might even decide to leave the market, which determines a further price discount for companies that stay in the market.
<!-- para 23 -->
Stronger protection of trade secrets might exacerbate the typical information asymmetries between buyers and sellers in the markets for corporate control.
When trade secrets are better protected, potential buyers are less able to evaluate the target's knowledge resources or prospects (Kogut and Zander, 1992).
Hence, discriminating between valuable and poor investments becomes more difficult.
This challenge is less acute in an industry where the risk of a poor investment is low.
The price discount determined by a stronger trade secret protection is minimal when winding up with a low-quality deal is unlikely.
However, a stronger trade secret protection will lead buyers to discount their offers in order to avoid overpaying for a poor company when the risk of poor investments in the industry increases.

<!-- para 26 -->
The ideal setting to test our theory would allow us to observe changes in company market value after an increase in the legal protection of trade secrets: that is, the same company would need to be sold twice—before and after the strengthening of trade secrecy protection.
While identifying a suitable context might be challenging, we leverage the PE industry.
PE firms make profits through buyouts; that is, they buy and resell companies.
This setting holds an important advantage for our study because the great majority of firms are sold at least twice: once when the PE firms buy them, and again when they resell them.
Moreover, in this empirical setting we can easily identify companies acquired by PE firms before the strengthening of trade secret protection and sold after, as well as companies acquired and sold by PE firms without any change in trade secret protection.
<!-- para 27 -->
The specific source of variation in trade secret protection that we exploit in this paper is the U.S. states' enactment of the UTSA, the objective of which is to protect trade secrets held by a company in the state where such protection has been enacted (Friedman, Landes, and Posner, 1991).
Prior to UTSA enactment, trade secrets in the United States were generally governed by state common law, which varied significantly from state to state.
The key motivation for the UTSA enactment was to alleviate the uneven development and uncertainty concerning the parameters of trade secret protection by recommending a uniform trade secrets law and allowing states the flexibility to meet local circumstances.
By 2012, 47 states and the District of Columbia had enacted the UTSA or similar statutes, the exceptions being Massachusetts, New York, and Texas (Malsberger et al., 2008; Milgrim and Bensen, 2015; Quinto and Singer, 2009).
The states and years of enactment are reported in Table 1.
<!-- para 28 -->
Following the enactment of the UTSA, trade secret protection generally became more extensive and clearer about the appropriate remedies and penalties for misappropriation, thereby strengthening the overall protection of trade secrets at the state level (Lydon, 1987; Malsberger et al., 2008; Milgrim and Bensen, 2015; Samuels and Johnson, 1990).
First, the UTSA expanded the protection of trade secrets by specifying that (1) the mere improper acquisition of a trade secret, without its actual use or disclosure, is a misappropriation and (2) information does not need to be used continuously in order to qualify as a trade secret.
Second, the UTSA enhanced the remedies available to the injured party, giving the owner of a trade secret three years after misappropriation to begin legal action and establishing that an injunction can be long enough to eliminate any advantage from misappropriation.
Third, it increased the severity of punishment, allowing punitive damages of up to twice the actual damage.
More generally, much empirical research has confirmed an increase in the strength of state trade secret protection following UTSA enactment (Malsberger et al., 2008; Png, 2015).
<!-- para 29 -->
The UTSA statutes were enacted in 46 U.S. states between 1975–2008, the period considered in this study.
Important for our identification strategy, recent empirical literature on the topic suggests that the timing of UTSA enactment across U.S. states was unrelated to the economic or political conditions of the state or to any lobbying effort by state companies (Png, 2015).
Because the introduction of the UTSA does not reflect any firm's strategic decision and seems uncorrelated with state characteristics possibly affecting firm value, it offers plausibly exogenous variation in the protection of trade secrets.
<!-- para 30 -->
To identify the effect of the law, we considered the target firm's state of incorporation as the relevant state for our analyses, because empirical evidence suggests that M&As are often governed by the law of the target firm's incorporation state (Coates and John, 2012).
Moreover, there is evidence that the state of incorporation and the main state of business tend to overlap for private firms—such as those owned by PE firms. [^4]

## methods
<!-- para 2 -->
The data were assembled through the collection of the PE fundraising prospectuses—usually referred as private placement memoranda (PPM)—which contain the performance details and some characteristics of all the PE firms' previous buyouts.
Unlike commercially available data on PE investments, where performance is measured at the fund level only, our dataset allows us to measure the performance of each single buyout independent of the performance of the other investments in such companies' portfolios (Landau and Bock, 2013).
We excluded from the analysis those private firms for which key pieces of information (e.g., industry, investment year, performance) were missing, as well as those whose locations we could not confirm.
Our analysis focuses on investments located in the United States (thus we excluded all observations pertaining to target firms located outside the United States), because we account for longitudinal changes in the U.S. regulation of trade secrets.
<!-- para 3 -->
In order to retrieve all the information needed to test our hypotheses, we complemented our proprietary database with data from other sources (see Table S1).
In particular, we used the COMPUSTAT dataset to compute the measure of resource-value uncertainty, based on the unpredictable variation in industry profitability, as well as the NBER patent database (Hall, Jaffe, and Trajtenberg, 2001), coupled with the U.S. patent inventor database (Li et al., 2014), and the Bessen (2009) concordance file between patent assignee and COMPUSTAT GVKEY identification numbers, to compute our measure of the mobility of knowledge workers to rivals.
After we merged all the data, our final sample was comprised of 1,890 companies bought and resold by 132 PE firms between 1975 and 2008 in 61 industries, as defined by SIC at the two-digit level.

Our analysis is conducted at the single-target-firm level, as we assess the impact of an increase in trade-secrecy protection on the change in the market value of a focal target company.
The main dependent variable is the internal rate of return (IRR) deriving from acquiring a target, a standard measure of performance in the context of buyouts (e.g., Kaplan and Schoar, 2005; Phalippou and Gottschalg, 2009).
There is a natural connection between the PE investor's return—as measured by the IRR—and the target firm's market value (see Appendix S1).
In general, any investor gains a positive return on investment if the value of the asset in which she/he invested has increased during the investment period.
In the particular case of the PE investor, the asset is a company: hence, PE returns will be positive only if, during the holding period, the value of the company is greater than the price PE firm initially paid for it.
The IRR measures the target firm's percentage change in market value in any period, discounting any future cash flow by the IRR itself (Bodie, Kane, and Marcus, 2011).
Consistent with prior literature (Castellaneta and Gottschalg, 2016; Castellaneta and Zollo, 2015; Lopez de Silanes, Phalippou, and Gottschalg, 2015), we censor the distribution to account for the outliers (defined as observations three standard deviations above and below the mean).

To examine whether an increase in a firm's trade secret protection impacts the firm's value, we use a quasi-experimental methodology—with a "treatment" group composed of target firms located in states that adopted the UTSA during the PE company's holding period, and a "control" group composed of target firms located in states that did not.
Specifically, we estimate the following ordinary least squares (OLS) regression to assess the impact of the introduction of stricter trade-secrecy laws on market value change of a certain target company:

Our identification strategy can be illustrated with an example.
Assume we want to measure the effect of a strengthening in trade secret protection on firm market value, considering Georgia's 1990 UTSA enactment.
Our dependent variable is the percentage change in firm market value for Georgia-located firms that were acquired by PE firms before 1990 and resold after 1990 (treated firms).
To estimate the effect of the treatment, we include a "control group" of similar firms acquired before 1990 and resold after 1990 but unaffected by the change in trade secret protection because they were located in states where no UTSA was passed.  $\beta$ in Equation 1 represents the difference in the percentage change in firm market value between the treated and the control groups.
An important difference between this example and the regression specification is that the latter accounts for the fact that the introduction of the UTSA is staggered over time: the composition of both the treatment and the control groups changes over time as progressively more states are treated.

Our approach is cross-sectional in that each target firm represents a single observation.
An alternative empirical approach would involve estimating a difference-in-differences (DiD) regression.
Such an approach would require observing the acquisition price of companies at least twice, before and after the treatment.
Since our data—based on how PE investors compute returns (Gompers, Kaplan, and Mukharlyamov, 2015)—only allow us to observe the percentage change in market value, as measured by the investment IRR, the DiD framework is difficult to implement fully.
However, our empirical strategy is equivalent to the DiD framework to the extent that the IRR measures change in the target firm's market value, and so the dependent variable incorporates the first difference before and after the treatment.

To test H1, we estimated the joint effect of UTSA enactment and industry mobility.
Industry Mobility $_{tsell}$ is defined as the rate of inter-firm mobility of knowledge workers at year  $t_{sell}$ in the industry in which the target company operates.
Inventor mobility is computed using the patent data, which allows us not only to measure mobility over an extensive period of time but also to identify mobility-to-competitor events (Almeida and Kogut, 1999; Bessen, 2009).
Inventors represent an important category of knowledge workers, and their departure has been shown to impose significant losses on the focal employer (Pakes and Nitzan, 1983).
To measure inventor mobility, we followed a well-established methodology and assigned each patent to a (public) parent company, based on Bessen's (2009) concordance file.
We identified inventor mobility within a single industry when two consecutive patents were assigned to the same inventor but to a different firm in the same industry (defined by SIC at the two-digit level).
Our final measure is computed as the proportion of inventors moving to rivals (i.e., the number of inventors moving to rivals over the overall number of inventors in that year and that industry).
<!-- para 13 -->
To test H2, we estimated the joint effect of the UTSA enactment and industry-level uncertainty in resource value.
Resource Value Uncertainty $_{tsell}$ is computed according to a well-established approach in both the economic and management literatures to measure industry uncertainty about the resource future value (Carruth, Dickerson, and Henley, 2000; Ghosal and Loungani, 1995, 2000; Lensink, Bo, and Sterken, 2001): we used an industry profit forecasting equation, computing the residuals of that equation (which represents the unpredictable profit component) and then computing the standard deviation of such residuals in the past five years of  $t_{sell}$.
We followed Ghosal and Loungani (2000) for the form of the profit forecasting equation, by regressing current industry profitability (as measured by industry return on sales by SIC two-digit level) on the profitability of the previous two years, plus a time trend.

To test H3, we estimated the joint effect of UTSA enactment and the risk of poor investments.
Poor Investments Risk is an industry time-invariant measure that captures the proportion of investments by industry (SIC two-digit code) in our sample.

Although a shift in trade secret protection provides a quasi-experimental setting, some basic controls were included in vector Z in Equation 1 to alleviate any remaining concerns about the reliability of our estimates.
First, one might expect that large investments in valuable companies lead PE firms to monitor local political situations more thoroughly (or even to try to influence them through lobbying activities).
To account for this possibility, we include Investment Size as a control, measured as the equity paid by the PE firm for the investment expressed in US$ millions (at 2006 values).
Furthermore, investments that take longer to be resold are more likely to be affected by changes in the institutional environments, and—by construction—the duration of an investment is correlated with our measure of change in firm market value.
To address this concern, we include entry-year fixed effects, a dummy for the year $t_{buy}$ of company purchase by the PE company, and exit-year fixed effects, a dummy for the year $t_{sell}$ of company sale.
These fixed effects control not only for any events that occurred in those years that could influence the company's market value but also for buyout duration (the difference between $t_{sell}$ and $t_{buy}$).
Moreover, our analyses include PE-firm fixed effects to control for a number of unobservable time-invariant characteristics of the PE firm involved (e.g., firm political connections).
We also include industry fixed effects to control for the target firm's industry (at the SIC two-digit level) and a dummy—Public at Buy—equal to 1 if the target firm was public when it was initially bought by the PE company.
Another dummy—IPO—equals 1 if the target firm was sold through an initial public offering.
Similarly, we include in our models target firms' state fixed effects to mitigate the concern that the treatment might be driven by a state's pro-business culture, which is difficult to account for with observables.

In any experimental setting, random assignment establishes ex ante symmetry between treatment and control groups and therefore obviates confounding.
In a quasi-experimental setting, this ideal condition is less likely to hold, which could be a concern if the treated firms are ex ante different from control firms along some characteristics that correlate with both our treatment (i.e., UTSA enactment) and our outcome (i.e., change in firm market value).
Importantly, either the level of ex ante risk or the expected value of a target firm may correlate with the change in the target's market value or with policymakers' effort to enact UTSA—for instance, to protect riskier or more valuable businesses better.
To account for such potential confounders, we reestimate the baseline specifications, while also matching the treatment and control firms on the basis of ex ante investment size, which proxies for the target's ex ante value [^8], and industry resource–value uncertainty, which proxies for the target's business riskiness (Acharya et al., 2013; Castellaneta and Zollo, 2015; Shepherd, 1999).
We used a coarsened exact matching (CEM) approach as developed by Iacus, King, and Porro (2009) to perform a match between treatment and control.
As shown in Table S2, our results remain robust.

Another concern is that changes in state-level economic or political conditions may drive both the introduction of the UTSA laws and the increase in firm market value.
For example, laws enacting the UTSA could be part of a broader political agenda aimed at stimulating business activity.
A related concern is that UTSA enactment reflects a firm's choice, as firms, according to their convenience, may lobby for or against such enactment.
To rule out these concerns, we first search for qualitative evidence that would suggest this possibility.
Specifically, we search the Lexis-Nexis database for press releases indicating that PE firms actively lobbied for the UTSA statutes.
Not surprisingly, we find no such evidence.
Indeed, as we already argue, recent empirical literature on the topic suggests that the timing of UTSA enactment across U.S. states was unrelated to state economic or political conditions (Png, 2015).
<!-- para 19 -->
But although the absence of qualitative evidence helps mitigate concerns about reverse causality, we verified empirically whether states' economic (e.g., gross domestic product [GDP] per capita, number of firms, and overall PE investments) and political conditions (e.g., the governor's party affiliation) might have influenced UTSA enactment.
Table 4 reports the results of linear probability models, where the dependent variable is a dummy indicating the treatment (i.e., the year of UTSA enactment in any given state, from 1978–2011).
The covariates of interest are the value of PE investments and disposals, GDP per capita to account for economic conditions, and a dummy variable to indicate the state's political condition, coded 1 if the state displays a "Red," Republican governor (Pe'Er and Gottschalg, 2011).
These variables do not seem to exert a significant effect on the enactment of the UTSA, mitigating potential concern that our treatment might be systematically correlated with a state's economic and political characteristics (Table 4, columns 1–2).
Finally, we obtained similar results when using discrete-time survival models (in particular, a logistic hazard model and a proportional hazard model).
Together, these results reinforce our confidence in the exogeneity of the trade-secrecy variable.

To rule out the possibility that our treatment generates statistically significant results merely by chance, we next perform a series of robustness checks by creating a “placebo” treatment.
In particular, we pretend that the change in trade secrets occurs five years before (Table S3, columns 1–3) and five years after (Table S3, columns 4–5) the real year of change.
We expect the fake treatment to have a weaker (or even null) effect on the dependent variable when compared with the actual treatment.
Consistent with this idea, we find that neither the direct impact of our “placebo treatment” nor any interaction is significant; results are shown in Table S3.
<!-- para 28 -->
To investigate the possibility that later UTSA adoptions might have a different impact than earlier ones in that PE firms may anticipate them, we split “UTSA,” our treatment variable, into two distinct treatment variables—called, respectively, UTSA (early) and UTSA (late).
UTSA (early) is equal to 1 if the target company was located in a state enacting the UTSA during the holding period and before 1992 (the median year that divides the treated observations into two equal subsamples); UTSA (late) is equal to 1 if the target company was located in a state enacting the UTSA during the holding period and after 1992.
We reestimate our regressions with these two different treatment variables (results available upon request).
The signs of the two variables, both alone and interacted, are the same, and the point estimates are quantitatively similar.
Indeed, a t-test (results available upon request) suggests that neither the magnitude of the main effect nor that of the interaction effect is statistically different between firms located in states that adopted the UTSA before 1992 and those located in states that adopted the UTSA after that year.

To further corroborate our results, we perform a series of robustness checks by extending our baseline analyses.
<!-- para 31 -->
Alternative measure of mobility and control for industry patent-intensity
<!-- para 32 -->
Our measure of mobility might raise endogeneity concerns, for two reasons.
First, a stronger state trade secret protection might reduce the amount of knowledge that a moving employee could transfer, reducing the demand for rivals' employees and eventually state rates of mobility to local competitors.
The measures of mobility and trade secret protection we use pertain to different levels of analyses: industry and state respectively, which effectively mitigates the concern that industry mobility may be driven by state trade secret protection.
Yet, to better address this issue, we reestimate the baseline specification but compute an alternative mobility measure, based on only those states and those years where UTSA has not yet been enacted.
For instance, in the case of target firms sold in 1987, we consider only those mobility events that occurred in the target's industry before 1987 in any state that had not enacted the UTSA by then.
In any year, this measure considers only those states that did not pass the UTSA and so is likely exogenous to the change in trade secret protection in the focal state.
As another test, we reestimate the baseline specification but now consider a measure of average industry mobility across all firms in our sample.
In doing so, we closely follow the methodology used by Chava et al.
(2013) and use a time-invariant industry-level measure of mobility to alleviate endogeneity concerns; by construction, our measure is unrelated to the UTSA enactment in any single state.
When we reestimate the baseline specifications with these two alternative measures of mobility, called "Mean industry mobility" and "Industry mobility pre-shock"; our findings remain robust: we still find that a stricter trade secret protection increases firm value in industries with higher cross-firm mobility of knowledge workers (Table S4, columns 1–2).
<!-- para 33 -->
A second concern pertains to measuring mobility rates with patent data: the interaction with a stronger trade secret protection might partially capture any interaction effect between trade secret protection and industry patent intensity.
To rule out this possibility, we measure industry patent intensity either as the number of patents in a given year and industry for all public firms (“Patents”) or as the ratio of the number of patents to the industry R&D expenditure in a given year and industry (“Patents/R&D”).
Our results on mobility hold stable even when we include as a regressor the interaction between the UTSA and our measures of industry patent intensity (Table S4, columns 3–6).
This suggests that the estimate of the interaction of UTSA and the mobility measure is unlikely to be confounded by the possible interaction effect between UTSA and the industry propensity to patent.

We next reestimate the baseline models with alternative operationalizations of our measures.
First, we adopt a more restrictive definition of outliers— that is, as observations below (above) the mean by four (rather than three) standard deviations—and censor the distribution accordingly.
The findings (presented in Table S5, columns 1–3) are again largely consistent with our theory.

Moreover, we perform sensitivity analyses to examine whether our results are robust to alternative operationalizations of poor investments risk within the PE industry.
Specifically, we consider two alternative operationalizations.
First, we compute risk (10%), which measures the proportion of investments by industry where PE firms obtain a return in the 10th percentile of the IRR distribution computed based on our overall sample.
Second, we compute Poor Investments Risk (15%), which measures the proportion of investments by industry where PE firms obtain a return in the 15th percentile of the IRR distribution computed based on our overall sample.
Models 4 and 5 of Table S5 show that the interaction between UTSA and Poor Investments Risk remains robust.
These sensitivity analyses increase our confidence that our findings are not an artifact of any particular operationalization.

Based on previous research (e.g., Bertrand and Mullainathan, 2003; Chava et al., 2013) we coded any change in trade secret protection determined by the UTSA enactment as 1, regardless of the intensity of the change.
The limitation of this approach is that it fails to capture any potential heterogeneity in the strength of change across states.
Hence, an alternative approach, as evidenced by Png (2015), would be to choose dimensions along which to measure the strength of trade secret protection, make appropriate assumptions about the relative importance of such dimensions, and, finally, measure how the change in regulation has affected those dimensions.
As a robustness check, we used the trade secret protection measure elaborated by Png (2015)—called “UTSA new.” The results remain substantially the same (Table S6).

## discussion
<!-- para 2 -->
Previous research has assessed the implications of trade secret protection for firms' innovative performance (Png, 2015), employee knowledge spillovers (Png and Samila, 2015), and a firm's decision to cluster (Fosfuri and Rønde, 2004).
Yet far less is known about the impact of trade secret protection on firm market value.
This study investigates the effect of increased protection of trade secrets on firm value in the context of the market for corporate control.
We find that an increase in trade secret protection generates two countervailing effects.
On one hand, following an increase in trade secret protection, firm market value increases when targets operate in industries with higher knowledge-worker mobility.
On the other hand, firm market value decreases when targets operate in industries with high resource-value uncertainty and high risk of poor investments.
<!-- para 3 -->
Accordingly, this work makes several key contributions to prior literature.
Our study is the first to clarify how the strength of the local appropriability regime affects company market value, by bridging two literatures: on acquisitions (e.g.
Capron and Shen, 2007; Coff, 1999; Laamanen, 2007) and on appropriability (e.g., Cohen, Nelson, and Walsh, 2000) that have remained surprisingly disconnected thus far.
By connecting these two research streams, we offer important insights to each.
First, scholars have devoted much attention to patents as a key driver of firm value (e.g., Cockburn and Griliches, 1988; Hall et al., 2005); our study extends this line of research by documenting the complex and more nuanced impact of trade secrets.
Second, we extend the M&A literature by exploring an important aspect of the market for corporate control that has been underexplored: the role of trade secrets in shaping the outcomes of acquisitions.
In this respect, our results complement prior literature on the role of information environment in target firm evaluation (Chang, 1998; Fuller et al., 2002; Shen and Reuer, 2005), by suggesting that the acquirer's ability to assess a target depends on the extent to which trade secrets are used and enforced.
<!-- para 4 -->
We also contribute to the literature examining the impact of institutions on business activity (e.g., Conti, 2014; Flammer and Kacperczyk, 2015; Kacperczyk, 2009; Pe'Er and Gottschalg, 2011), aimed at showing how government policies affect companies' economic value.
In this respect, we show that the effectiveness of a change in law depends on other factors—often beyond government control.
More specifically, our study implies that regulations promoting stronger trade secret protection do not automatically translate to greater firm value.
In particular, when targets operate in less predictable and uncertain industries or in industries where the risk of bad deals is particularly high, a policy that aims to protect trade-secret-related knowledge assets, and therefore to increase their value, might paradoxically reduce the value of the firm in the context of the market for corporate control.
<!-- para 5 -->
Finally, our findings are relevant for the resource-based view of the firm that aims to understand how firms can establish a sustainable competitive advantage by developing valuable and rare resources that are also difficult to substitute and imitate (e.g., Barney, 1991; Mahoney and Pandian, 1992; Rumelt, 1984).
Our study contributes to this literature by documenting that the protection of such resources—which reduces the threat of resource imitation by rivals—need not increase the market value of firms owning those resources.
Rather, the impact of legal protection of resources on firm market value might be contingent on the nature of protection.
If trade secrets are the most important of a firm's intangible assets, a stronger protection thereof might paradoxically reduce rather than increase the firm's market value, as determined in the market for corporate control.
By limiting information available to potential buyers, a stronger protection of trade secrets reduces the value that a firm might capture from its intangible assets when selling those assets in the market for corporate control.
<!-- para 6 -->
Some limitations of this study are also noteworthy.
First, the IRR (our measure capturing the change in firm market value) assumes that PE firms could reinvest the money obtained from a focal buyout in activities at least as profitable as that buyout (Beaves, 1988)—which clearly may not always be the case.
However, the IRR is still the key performance indicator for practitioners in the PE context, which lessens (even if it does not eliminate) concerns about the reliability of our measure.
Moreover, using patent data to measure inventor mobility presents major limitations, as suggested by Ge, Huang, and Png (2016).
However, given the long period we take into account (1978–2008) and the fact that we measure intra-industry cross-firm mobility of knowledge workers, patent data likely offer the most suitable resource for our study.
Alternative data sources, such as LinkedIn or the Current Population Survey, may suffer from even greater limitations.
<!-- para 7 -->
Another caveat of our study is that here we focus only on firms owned by PE companies, which that may not be representative of the overall firm population.
However, no difference has been identified between firms acquired by financial or strategic bidders in terms of the stock of intangible assets (cf.
Hence, the impact of trade secret protection is unlikely to differ across such firms.
At the same time, assessing the change in acquisition price after a strengthening of trade secret protection might be difficult in other empirical contexts—where firms are usually not sold multiple times.
<!-- para 8 -->
Despite these limitations, this study provides important insights for managers and policymakers.
Increasing trade secret protection might not unequivocally benefit firms' owners.
Companies that operate in industries characterized by high mobility might make greater use of confidentiality agreements to fully capture the advantages of a stronger trade secret protection.
However, sellers of firms operating in industries with uncertain resource value may want to strategically minimize the negative effect of trade secret protection by providing potential buyers—which, after an increase in trade secret protection, are less able to assess the target firm—with a credible signal about the value of the company—such as selling their company through an IPO.
Based on the contingent effect of trade secret protection, companies may adjust their strategic decisions, including where to locate or relocate, based in part on whether they will derive benefits or suffer losses when trade secrets are better protected.
<!-- para 9 -->
This study has also direct implications for designing trade secret policy.
Although most common accounts consider the benefits of trade secret protection, we show that the effect of such policies is contingent and that, under some circumstances, it may even impose significant costs on the focal firm.
Hence, our findings should help policymakers more fully understand the economic impact of government policies associated with trade secrets—which would naturally have relevant policy implications for business, more generally.
Estimating the social welfare effect of trade secret protection is a relevant issue we leave for future research.
