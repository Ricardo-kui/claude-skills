# ceo_military_experience_and_corporate_restructuring_strategi 句子库存

## introduction
<!-- para 1 -->
Imprinting theory, originally introduced by, explores how early experiences and events shape organizations’ policies (), philosophies (), decisions (), and strategies ().
At its core, imprinting theory draws a parallel between organizations and individuals, asserting that the formative experiences they undergo leave a lasting imprint that significantly impacts their future actions, strategies, and overall trajectory.
Fundamentally, this theory posits that early events, particularly those associated with influential figures like founders or leaders, wield considerable influence in shaping an organization’s long-term behavior and decision-making processes.
These initial events establish templates or imprints that serve as guiding frameworks for the organization.
Hence, the founders or leaders leave their marks on their organizations, which in turn, affects their firms’ performance.
This theoretical framework has been used extensively within organizational studies (,,, ).
<!-- para 2 -->
While imprinting theory has established a general framework for understanding how early experiences shape organizational and entrepreneurial trajectories, an intriguing yet understudied area among established businesses lies in the imprinting effects of ex-military CEOs on corporate restructuring strategies.
Despite the theory’s extensive application in organizational studies (,,, ) and entrepreneurial studies (,,, ), limited attention has been directed toward understanding the imprinting effect wielded specifically by CEOs with military backgrounds.
This study focuses on the strategic implications of CEOs’ military background and how it might impact the choice of corporate restructuring strategies.
To understand the impact of military imprint on firm strategic choices, scholars () have recently pointed to two significant dimensions: imprint formation and imprint persistence.
Understanding these dimensions helps elucidate how military experiences shape decision-making processes concerning firm strategies among corporate executives, specifically ex-military CEOs.
<!-- para 3 -->
First, the military imprint formation begins during the early stages of military training ().
Military training encompasses a rigorous [socialization process](https://www.sciencedirect.com/topics/social-sciences/socialization-process) that creates an environment conducive to the formation of a foundational imprint of core values and principles among participants.
Recruits, typically joining the military in their early twenties, undergo a pivotal transitional period in their lives, highly susceptible to environmental influences.
During training and subsequent service, military recruits take part in intensive socialization to instill military norms and values to foster coherence, discipline, and loyalty.
Expectations for specific behaviors and explicit norms are emphasized in a manner that breaks down recruits’ previously established behaviors and habits.
Certain preferred values of order and discipline, focus, goal-directed behavior, obedience to rules, and [stewardship](https://www.sciencedirect.com/topics/economics-econometrics-and-finance/stewardship) for the collective are often instilled during training and service engagements ().
Moreover, the military implements an incentive system that rewards adherence to expectations and penalizes deviation ().
These intensive socialization processes induce changes in daily behavior and personality, leading to the formation of a military imprint characterized by discipline, efficiency, obedience to rules, and collective stewardship ().
<!-- para 4 -->
Military service cannot only profoundly shape a person’s character and behavior but also their decision-making in a work context (, ).
Research has shown a link between CEOs’ military experience and managerial decisions, financial policies, and corporate outcomes., for instance, found that ex-military CEOs pursue more conservative financial and investment policies.
Additionally, ex-military CEOs have also been shown to engage less in corporate fraud/misconduct () and tax avoidance ().
In addition to their impact on financial policies and ethical decision-making, ex-military CEOs-led firms have been shown to do better during financial distress ().
Ex-military CEOs’ strong performance during [industry](https://www.sciencedirect.com/topics/economics-econometrics-and-finance/industry) stress/firm financial distress can be attributed to a sense of commitment and ethical values imprinted during their military experience.
Another factor that may allow ex-military CEOs to perform better under stress is that the military is often a particularly suitable training ground for CEOs, who must often make difficult decisions in high-pressure situations ().
For instance, military personnel learn how to make decisions in extreme conditions during combat or in a combat deployment zone.
Such skills could be transferable to organizational decision-making under certain high-stakes contexts ().
Consequently, military service indeed seems to provide experience and skills that are pertinent to corporate leadership.
In sum, as the discussion above illustrates, imprinting theory offers an important lens for understanding how the military background of CEOs influences their decision-making, leadership style, and organizational strategies.
We argue that the imprint of a military CEO’s experiences and values significantly shapes the choice and intensity of restructuring strategies (such as divestitures, acquisitions, layoffs, and asset sell-offs).
In the next section, we present our hypothesis development, focusing on the influence of ex-military CEO leadership and corporate restructuring strategies.

## theory
<!-- para 3 -->
In this section, we develop our theoretical arguments based on imprinting perspective to align specific attributes cultivated through [military](https://www.sciencedirect.com/topics/social-sciences/military) experiences—such as order and discipline, strategic focus, efficiency, and goal-directed behavior—and distinct components of corporate restructuring strategies (i.e., portfolio, financial, and organizational restructuring).
<!-- para 4 -->
Why might ex-military CEOs be more likely to pursue corporate restructuring strategies?
Military training and socialization often involve instilling discipline and duty-focused values and philosophies into service members ().
Values such as courage, integrity, loyalty, sacrifice, efficiency, focus, order and discipline, and [goal orientation](https://www.sciencedirect.com/topics/social-sciences/goal-orientation) are taught, rewarded, and consistently communicated as the exemplary code of conduct ().
Individuals who pass through these trainings and subsequently serve in the [military](https://www.sciencedirect.com/topics/economics-econometrics-and-finance/military) report using these embedded values in their personal and professional lives long after they depart the military ().
While their military training and service may have occurred earlier in their lives, CEOs are likely to continue to be guided by their professional lives’ values and principles.
<!-- para 5 -->
This assertion is, in fact, in line with imprinting theory predictions.
According to this theory, executives’ background characteristics, experiences, and events are likely to influence their strategic choices, which in turn influence firm performance (; Burton and Beckman, 2012; ).
Using this theoretical lens, it is reasonable to expect that the values and principles CEOs acquired during their military training and service will come to bear in their professional decision-making and strategic choices.
<!-- para 6 -->
An imprinting theory provides a valuable framework for comprehending the profound impact of CEOs’ military backgrounds and experiences on their decision-making, leadership approaches, and organizational strategies, especially in the context of implementing restructuring endeavors.
We argue that the imprint left by a military CEO’s leadership style and principles significantly molds the conceptualization, planning, and execution of restructuring strategies within an organization, encompassing actions like divestitures, acquisitions, layoffs, and asset sell-offs.
Some of the core values ex-military CEOs possess are focus and goal-directed behavior.
As ITT corporation’s former CEO Steven Loranger put it, “Something you learn in the military that is fundamental in business is a sense of mission.
You got very, very focused on your objectives…” ().
To the extent that ex-military CEOs view the development of the firm’s core business as their primary goal, it is reasonable to expect that they will be laser-focused not only on nurturing the core business but also on eliminating any non-core (peripheral) businesses as distractions.
<!-- para 7 -->
Furthermore, CEOs’ military experience is likely to heighten their attention toward maintaining strategic focus and optimal scope.
Accordingly, ex-military CEOs are more likely to see portfolio restructuring strategies as a means of achieving organizational focus and singularity of purpose.
Scholars have long-established restructuring strategies such as divestitures and acquisitions as important strategic actions that help refocus the firm, especially following its expanded scope of operation due to diversification and acquisitions (,, ).
<!-- para 8 -->
Moreover, a military background often instills a strong sense of order and discipline, which may translate into methodical and structured approaches when executing restructuring strategies like asset sell-offs, divestitures, and acquisitions within corporations.
The emphasis on adherence to protocols and systematic execution learned through military experiences might influence corporate leaders with military backgrounds in managing and implementing these strategic portfolio changes.
Hence, the ingrained order and discipline from military experiences may be associated with the execution of restructuring strategies, particularly in divestitures and acquisitions within corporate settings.
<!-- para 9 -->
Additionally, military training fosters a strategic focus on objectives and outcomes.
This heightened focus on specific goals may translate into a precise and targeted approach when executing financial restructuring strategies, such as asset sell-offs, within corporate environments.
The ability to maintain unwavering focus and execute targeted actions---a hallmark of military training and experience---could influence CEOs with military backgrounds in formulating and executing these financial restructuring initiatives.
<!-- para 10 -->
Finally, military environments emphasize efficiency and goal-directed behavior to achieve predefined objectives.
These attributes developed in military experiences might correlate with an efficient and goal-oriented approach in executing [organizational restructuring](https://www.sciencedirect.com/topics/social-sciences/organizational-restructuring) strategies (such as layoffs).
The propensity to streamline processes and direct efforts towards specific objectives learned through military experiences may influence CEOs with military backgrounds when addressing organizational restructuring challenges.
Accordingly, we argue that ex-military CEOs are more likely to view corporate restructuring strategies as a means of enforcing corporate focus and streamlining operations.
The discussion above leads us to the following hypotheses:
<!-- para 11 -->
*Hypothesis 1 The presence of ex-military CEOs is positively related to the likelihood of corporate restructuring strategies.*
<!-- para 13 -->
While ex-military CEOs may be more aggressive in pursuing corporate restructuring strategies (including portfolio, financial, and organizational restructuring), as we argued above, their engagement may potentially weaken over time as they stay longer on the job.
This line of argument is consistent with the established cognitive [rigidity](https://www.sciencedirect.com/topics/social-sciences/rigidity) (“stale in the saddle”) and CEO life cycle perspectives that have been put forth in the literature (, ).
This perspective suggests that CEOs who have been with the firm for a longer than average time tend to become ‘stale’ and more entrenched in their strategies instead of formulating newer and innovative strategic choices.
According to research, CEOs who stay longer on their job tend to develop a commitment to the status quo primarily due to their principal role in formulating current and past strategic actions ().
Such commitment to the status quo is often associated with cognitive rigidity and could adversely affect CEOs’ ability to proactively detect and effectively adapt to changes in environmental conditions ().
Accordingly, research has shown that higher CEO commitment to the status quo is associated with less aggressive strategies over time (, ).
<!-- para 14 -->
This view is also in line with the broader CEO life cycle research that posits the presence of distinct phases or stages in the CEOs’ job tenure ().
For instance, argued that most CEOs go through five major stages in their tenure, including response to the mandate, experimentation, selection of an enduring theme, convergence, and dysfunction.
An extensive body of work in the strategic leadership literature has demonstrated that early CEO tenure is positively related to aggressive corporate actions such as strategic change (,, ), radical innovation (, ), acquisitions () and diversification (, ).
<!-- para 15 -->
Building on these findings, we propose that while ex-military CEOs are inclined to pursue corporate restructuring strategies to streamline the firm’s operation and achieve strategic focus, their propensity to do so is likely to weaken over the course of their tenure.
Pursuing corporate restructuring strategies such as acquisitions, divestitures, asset sell-offs, and layoffs is a major undertaking that is likely to refocus the firm’s scope of operation (, ).
It also requires significant social capital and support from stakeholders (, ).
Insight from the CEO life cycle literature indicates that CEOs are more likely to begin their terms with higher social capital and a mandate for action (, ).
Additionally, CEOs are subjected to more intense scrutiny of their performance from external stakeholders in the early years of their tenure, given their lack of performance track record (, ).
Consequently, it is reasonable to expect that ex-military CEOs will vigorously pursue these strategies in the earlier part of their tenure.
Conversely, CEOs in the later stages of their tenure not only develop cognitive rigidity but are also more likely to be constrained by weaker stakeholder support ().
<!-- para 16 -->
Finally, research on executive determinants of corporate restructuring has also documented a negative link between CEO tenure and these strategies (,, ).
Ang and colleagues (2014) argued and empirically demonstrated that longer-tenured CEOs tend to engage less in corporate restructuring partly because they grow familiar with the business segments, and such familiarity may make it harder for them to divest these businesses.
Similarly, found a negative relationship between CEO tenure and the divestiture of ‘legacy’ (original) business units.
Given these findings, we argue that although ex-military CEOs are more aggressive in pursuing corporate restructuring strategies (such as divestitures, acquisition, asset sell-offs, and layoffs) compared to their non-military counterparts, their engagement may potentially weaken over time as their tenure on the job increases.
The discussion above leads us to the following hypothesis:
<!-- para 17 -->
*Hypothesis 2 CEO tenure negatively moderates the relationship between the presence of ex-military CEOs and the likelihood of corporate restructuring such that the relationship is weaker among longer-tenured CEOs.*

## methods
<!-- para 2 -->
We focus on all publicly traded firms listed in the S&P 500 index as our empirical context.
The S&P 500 index comprises large, diversified firms across the manufacturing and [service sectors](https://www.sciencedirect.com/topics/social-sciences/service-sector), making it an appealing sampling target given our study’s focus on corporate restructuring.
We constructed a panel dataset for the period 2014–2018, with 2500 firm-year observations (five hundred firms over five years) in the sample.
Several databases were used to obtain data on the study’s variables.
To identify ex-military CEOs (our predictor), we used internet sources including Google advanced search, publicly available executive biographies, magazine, and newspaper stories, as well as lists of military executives in major U.S. corporations.
Using search words such as “military CEO,” “ex-military CEO,” and “CEOs with military experience,” we first identified all CEOs with military service experience before their careers as corporate executives.
Then, we cross-checked their company names against S&P 500 firms before including them in our sample.
To be included in our sample, ex-military CEOs had served in any of the five branches of the U.S. military (Army, Navy, Marine Corps, Airforce, and Coast Guard) and the reserves.
Further, we included additional details including the branch they served in, length of service, and major combat experience (e.g., Korea, Vietnam, Iraq/Afghanistan) as well.
<!-- para 3 -->
Data on corporate restructuring (i.e., *divestiture, asset Retrenchment/sell-off, acquisition, and major layoff*) were obtained from the Mergent Online database.
This database includes a chronological list of major corporate activities for each firm in our sample, including acquisitions, [divestments](https://www.sciencedirect.com/topics/economics-econometrics-and-finance/disinvestment), joint ventures, asset sell-offs, and spin-offs, among others.
Data on executive and board variables were obtained from ExecuComp and ISS/RiskMetrics databases.
Finally, data on financial and organizational variables were obtained from the Compustat database.
<!-- para 5 -->
***Dependent Variable****.
Corporate restructuring* is the dependent variable of this study.
Using classification, we focused on portfolio, financial, and organizational restructuring as the main types of corporate restructuring.
Portfolio restructuring was measured using divestiture and acquisition activities.
Similarly, we utilized asset sell-offs to measure financial restructuring.
Finally, we used major layoffs (i.e., a reduction of five percent or more of the workforce) to measure organizational restructuring.
Divestiture is defined as the sale of an existing business unit via sell-offs, buy-outs, or equity carve-outs ().
Asset sell-off is defined as the sale of major firm assets such as manufacturing plants, warehouses, research and development laboratories, specialized equipment, and other physical assets (, ).
Acquisition is the purchase of a controlling interest in a target firm or its business unit (, ).
Layoff is defined as the reduction in an organization’s workforce involving the termination of employment (, ).
<!-- para 6 -->
We measured the likelihood of each restructuring activity for each year during the sampling window.
Each restructuring strategy was operationalized as a binary variable.
The *likelihood of divestiture* takes a value of one if the firm divested at least one business unit in a given year during the sampling window and zero otherwise.
Similarly, the *likelihood of asset sell-off* was coded as one if a firm engaged in any asset sell-offs (e.g., plant and/or equipment sales), and zero otherwise.
The *likelihood of acquisition* was coded as one if a firm engaged in any acquisitions and zero otherwise.
Finally, the *likelihood of major layoff* was coded as one if a firm laid off at least 5 % of its workforce in a given year and zero otherwise (, ).
Data on the dependent variables were collected from the Mergent Online database.
<!-- para 7 -->
***Independent Variable****.
Ex-military CEO* is the study’s independent variable.
It is operationalized as a binary variable with a value of 1 if a sample firm’s CEO had military experience (i.e., served in any branch of the U.S. military, reserves, or other countries’ military), and zero otherwise ().
We did not distinguish between military experience obtained in the U.S. and other countries, as most military training emphasizes core values such as loyalty, courage, discipline, and service.
<!-- para 8 -->
***Moderating Variable.*** *CEO Tenure* is the moderating variable of this study.
We measured CEO tenure by counting the number of years a CEO has been in his/her position.
As previously noted, CEOs with longer tenures are less engaged in corporate restructuring ().
This tendency is attributed to their greater familiarity with specific business segments, which can create challenges in divesting from these areas ().
Consequently, we expect CEO tenure to act as a negative moderator in the relationship between ex-military CEOs and corporate restructuring strategies.
<!-- para 9 -->
***Control Variables****.* We controlled for several [corporate governance](https://www.sciencedirect.com/topics/social-sciences/corporate-governance) (*Board size, Board Independence, CEO Equity Ownership*) and organizational (*Firm Age, Firm size, Financial Distress, Diversification Level, Firm performance*), managerial (*CEO* *[Education Level](https://www.sciencedirect.com/topics/social-sciences/education-level "Learn more about Education Level from ScienceDirect's AI-generated Topic Pages")*) variables that might affect the likelihood of corporate restructuring (divestiture, acquisitions, layoffs, and asset sale-offs).
Past studies have shown that larger boards, despite their advantage in bringing extensive director human and social capital, can pose a challenge for the firm, especially during large-scale restructuring (). *Board size* is operationalized by counting the number of directors on the board.
Similarly, we controlled for firm size as larger firms are more likely to engage in restructuring strategies, particularly following diversification (). *Firm size* was operationalized as a logarithm of the firm’s total assets. *Firm age* plays a crucial role in influencing a company’s inclination toward risk-taking ().
Since corporate restructuring usually entails significant risks encountered over time (), older firms are likely to engage in corporate restructuring strategies.
Firm age is measured as the number of years since its initial incorporation.
Past research (, ) has observed that firms with outsider-dominated boards (*board independence*) are more likely to engage in restructuring strategies.
We measured *board independence* as the ratio of outside directors to the total number of directors on the board.
Furthermore, we controlled for *CEO equity ownership* (percent of equity owned by the CEO) since CEOs holding a significant amount of equity may exhibit different behaviors from those with minimal equity stakes ().
Specifically, CEOs primarily reliant on a base [salary](https://www.sciencedirect.com/topics/social-sciences/salary) often avoid risky decisions (), while a high level of equity ownership can mitigate the risk-averse tendencies associated with sole reliance on base salary ().
Thus, CEOs with greater equity ownership are more likely to pursue major corporate restructuring strategies than their counterparts with lower equity stakes.
We controlled for *CEO's educational background*, as it has been shown to impact various corporate policies and organizational outcomes, such as mergers and acquisitions (), R&D spending (), and firm performance ().
We operationalized the CEO *education level* based on the attainment of the highest academic degree.
This was quantified using a scale from 0 to 4, where 0 equals no degree, one stands for high school completion, two for an undergraduate degree, three for a graduate degree, and four for a PhD or other terminal degree.
<!-- para 10 -->
Firms experiencing *financial distress* often resort to various organizational restructuring strategies ().
In such cases, restructuring acts as a renegotiation tool among shareholders, creditors, and other key stakeholders ().
We operationalized financial distress using [bankruptcy](https://www.sciencedirect.com/topics/social-sciences/insolvency) Z-score.
Generally, a Z-score above 3 indicates [financial stability](https://www.sciencedirect.com/topics/economics-econometrics-and-finance/financial-stability), whereas scores below 3 signal distress, with lower scores indicating an increased risk of [bankruptcy](https://www.sciencedirect.com/topics/economics-econometrics-and-finance/bankruptcy) ().
We multiplied the z-score values with −1 to align them with the financial distress variable (higher values represent greater financial distress).
The firm’s *Diversification level* is likely to influence the degree of corporate restructuring activity, specifically divestiture intensity (,, ).
This variable was measured using the widely used entropy measure ().
One of the strongest predictors of corporate restructuring is poor firm performance. *Firm performance* was operationalized as the logarithm of return on assets (ROA).
In addition to these controls, we also accounted for the possible [industry](https://www.sciencedirect.com/topics/economics-econometrics-and-finance/industry) and temporal effects on restructuring activity by including industry (at 2-digit SIC code level) and year-fixed effect dummies in our models.
Data on control variables were collected from Compustat, ExecuComp, BoardEx, and ISS/RiskMetrics databases.
<!-- para 12 -->
Due to the panel nature of our data, in which a firm might implement various restructuring strategies over the sampling period, the firm-level observations across multiple years are likely to be non-independent.
This lack of independence among observations within a firm makes the use of ordinary least squares regression unsuitable for our analysis (, ).
To address this issue, we used Generalized Estimating Equations (GEEs) for our analyses.
GEE offers maximum likelihood estimates and accounts for the non-independence of multiple observations during the sampling window (, ).
Furthermore, this approach accommodates the non-normal distribution of our dependent variable (i.e., corporate restructuring strategies) ().
This estimation method is widely used in similar corporate restructuring studies (,, ).
We used robust standard errors in all our models to mitigate potential misspecifications in the correlation structure, ensuring a more conservative test of our hypotheses (,, ).
Following, we employed a binomial distribution with a logit link function and an exchangeable correlation to estimate the likelihood of restructuring strategies across our binary measures.
<!-- para 13 -->
Companies may systematically choose executives based on specific backgrounds, while executives similarly select companies that align with their [human capital](https://www.sciencedirect.com/topics/economics-econometrics-and-finance/human-capital) ().
This dynamic can lead to endogeneity issues in studies that focus on the relationship between CEO characteristics and firm outcomes.
Specifically, the role of a CEO, when assumed by former military personnel, might be inherently endogenous ().
Individuals may choose military careers based on unique personal attributes (), which could predispose them to more active participation in company restructuring.
Furthermore, the selection criteria used by the military might also introduce an omitted variable bias in our GEE estimates ().
As a result, our GEE estimates may be biased due to unobserved personal traits related to both military involvement and CEO decision-making ().
To address these endogeneity concerns, we adopted a two-stage treatment model previously used in studies of ex-military CEOs (,, ).
In this approach, it is essential to identify a theoretically meaningful [instrumental variable](https://www.sciencedirect.com/topics/economics-econometrics-and-finance/instrumental-variables) that is correlated with the independent variable (i.e., the presence of an ex-military CEO) but not with the dependent variable (i.e., restructuring strategies).
<!-- para 14 -->
Since the likelihood of joining the military varies significantly with age, we utilized *CEOs’ birth cohort* as an [instrumental variable](https://www.sciencedirect.com/topics/social-sciences/instrumental-variable) to assess the probability of a CEO being an ex-military ().
The birth period is a crucial factor in determining military enlistment ().
Studies have shown that the proportion of CEOs with military backgrounds has varied over time.
These studies have indicated that older CEOs and those born during a specific period are more likely to serve in the military due to a generational (i.e., birth cohort) effect (,, ).
For example, scholars () have observed that CEOs born in earlier decades (e.g., the 1920 s or 1930 s) were more likely to have military experience compared to their younger counterparts.
Furthermore, the probability of military service varies with the CEO’s age, especially during wartime versus peacetime, affecting the availability of eligible individuals ().
<!-- para 15 -->
In the first step, we ran a [probit model](https://www.sciencedirect.com/topics/economics-econometrics-and-finance/probit-model) using an ex-military CEO as a binary outcome variable and regressed it against covariates that are likely to influence the probability that an ex-military CEO will be appointed (see appendix for result table).
Specifically, we included *CEO birth order, CEO education level, Sales, Performance (ROA), Market capitalization, Tobin’s Q,* and *Industry group*.
These variables have been shown to impact the propensity of ex-military CEO appointments.
CEO's educational background is associated with the likelihood of ex-military CEOs’ appointment ().
Firm performance (ROA, Tobin’s Q) and firm size (Market Capitalization, Sales) have been associated with ex-military CEOs’ appointments ().
Similarly, some industry sectors, such as Utilities, Consumer, and Financials, are more likely to employ ex-military CEOs ().
We included the predicted value from the first stage as an endogeneity control variable in the second stage (main analysis) analyses ().

The results of the GEE analyses are presented in, below.
Models 1–4 of report the main effect tests, while models 1–4 of present the results of the moderating effect hypothesis.
In addition to coefficients, marginal effects at the mean are also reported for each model to help with interpretation.
Hypothesis one proposed that the presence of ex-military CEOs is positively related to the likelihood of corporate restructuring strategies.
As shown in model 1 of below, the presence of ex-military CEOs was a statistically significant negative predictor of the likelihood of divestiture (β = -1.265, *p < 0.05*).
The marginal effect at the mean for this variable was −0.176, suggesting that holding other variables in the model at their means, the presence of ex-military CEOs (from no to yes) decreases the likelihood of divestiture by 17.6 % percent.

We conducted additional analyses to further ensure the validity of our findings.
Specifically, we used the [propensity score matching](https://www.sciencedirect.com/topics/economics-econometrics-and-finance/propensity-score-matching) (PSM) technique to verify that ex-military CEO-led firms have a reasonable comparison group in our analyses ().
The PSM method matches observations from both treatment and counterfactual groups across various dimensions (, ).
In our study, companies led by ex-military CEOs formed the treatment group, while those led by non-military CEOs were treated as counterfactuals.
This method determines matches using a “propensity score,” which estimates, in our case, the likelihood of a CEO being ex-military in the treatment group based on specific covariates ().
We used the following covariates that are likely to predict the presence of an ex-military CEO in a company: *CEO Tenure, Sales, Firm Age, Number of Employees, Fama & French industry membership.*
<!-- para 24 -->
We used a [probit model](https://www.sciencedirect.com/topics/economics-econometrics-and-finance/probit-model) to generate the propensity scores for our sample.
Since the number of military CEOs is relatively small in the S&P 500 universe, a one-to-one matching is not feasible.
Accordingly, we conducted a one-to-three matching to increase the sample size for our analysis.
In this step, we identified three appropriate non-military-CEOs for each ex-military-CEO using the closest propensity scores.
As can be seen in, we also conducted a post-PSM balance test to assess the fair distribution of these covariates between matched and unmatched groups as well as treatment and counterfactual firms (, ).
Specifically, we examined the variance ratio (V(Treatment)/V(Control)) for both unmatched and matched samples.
The ideal variance ratio should be close to one, indicating similar variances between the treated and control groups for a specific covariate ().
A commonly accepted range of variance ratio is between 0.5 and 2.
The variance ratios in range from 0.45 to 1.5 indicating acceptable values.
Furthermore, the *t* -test results across all variables both before and after matching show non-significant differences, suggesting that the matching process has effectively balanced these key variables between the treated and control groups.
For the overall model, the desired variance ratio (R) should range from 0.5 to 2.
Moreover, the maximum bias in covariates, known as Rubin’s B, should be under 25 %.
As can be seen in, our matching demonstrated a satisfactory balance with R at 0.55 and Rubin’s B at 21.2 %.

## discussion
<!-- para 2 -->
This study examines the relationship between the presence of ex-military CEOs and the likelihood of corporate restructuring strategies.
The findings of our empirical analyses indicate that firms led by ex-military CEOs are more likely to engage in asset sell-offs (a financial restructuring strategy) and acquisitions (a portfolio restructuring strategy).
Contrary to our prediction, ex-military CEO leadership was found to be a significant negative predictor of divestitures among sample firms.
Finally, we found some support for our prediction that the relationship between ex-military CEO leadership and corporate restructuring indeed declines with longer CEO tenure.
<!-- para 3 -->
The findings contribute to the ongoing research on corporate restructuring.
Drawing insight from imprinting theory, we argued and empirically demonstrated that CEOs’ life experiences and values do influence their propensity to engage in corporate restructuring in general and financial (asset sell-offs) and portfolio (acquisitions) restructuring in particular.
The extant literature on executive determinants of corporate restructuring has mostly focused on exploring how specific governance arrangements such as CEO compensation and equity ownership (, ), as well as CEO succession (; Chiu, Johnson, Hoskisson & Johnson, 2016) affect the likelihood and intensity of corporate restructuring.
Our findings extend current research by shedding some light on CEOs’ background characteristics in general and military experience in particular.
Although our prediction regarding the likelihood of divestitures did not receive empirical support, we found that the presence of ex-military CEOs does increase the likelihood of asset sell-offs and acquisitions.
Accordingly, the mixed findings contribute to the restructuring literature by emphasizing the importance of CEOs’ backgrounds as a driver of strategic actions and the nuanced nature of the influence executives’ backgrounds may have on these diverse sets of strategic choices.
<!-- para 4 -->
Furthermore, the findings of this study also contribute to the strategic leadership (corporate governance) literature.
There is an extensive body of research that examines the influence of executives’ background characteristics on their strategic choice and firm performance, relying on the upper echelons theory logic (, ).
Recent scholarly works have examined how CEOs’ military experience influences several corporate outcomes.
Specifically, researchers have found that firms led by ex-military CEOs engage in less corporate fraud/unethical behavior (, ), make conservative investment decisions (), embrace environmental causes (), and are likely to perform well underperformance distress ().
While these studies have generated much-needed insights into how CEOs’ military experience shapes strategic choices, how such experience might shape corporate restructuring has not yet received significant scholarly attention.
Accordingly, this study addresses this research gap and contributes to our understanding of the important influence CEOs’ military experience has in shaping corporate restructuring strategies beyond the financial and ethical decision-making outcomes.
<!-- para 5 -->
Similarly, this study also contributes to the growing area of research around ‘CEOs’ life events’.
This stream of research focuses on unpacking how CEOs’ early life experiences influence their strategic choice and the performance of their firms ().
Past works in this area have shown that CEOs’ early life experiences influence their leadership style (), risk-taking tendencies (), as well as their firms’ financial disclosure (), to name a few.
This study contributes additional insights to this growing area of research by highlighting how CEOs’ military background (which is often acquired during their early years) and the values and skills associated with military training influence their use of restructuring strategies.
<!-- para 6 -->
Beyond the study of the main effect of ex-military CEO leadership and the likelihood of corporate restructuring strategies, this study also examined the role of CEO tenure as a potential boundary condition.
According to our findings, firms led by ex-military CEOs are more likely to engage in financial restructuring such as asset sell-offs, but this relationship declines among longer-tenured CEOs.
We also observed that ex-military CEOs are less likely to engage in major layoffs (organizational restructuring) as they stay longer in their position.
Past studies have examined how CEOs’ propensity to engage in restructuring evolves over time, particularly with their power and influence (, )., for instance, found that CEO structural power was negatively related to the likelihood of divestiture in their analysis of a matched sample.
More recently, Chiu and colleagues (2022) empirically observed that CEOs’ commitment to the status quo is indeed negatively related to divestiture activity and that this relationship is strengthened by CEO equity ownership and duality.
Interestingly, they did not find CEO expert power (measured as divestiture experience) to be a significant moderator.
In light of these findings, our study’s finding on CEO tenure’s moderating role provides further support for the prevailing scholarly view that CEOs’ propensity to engage in corporate restructuring strategies (such as divestiture and asset selloff) tends to decline as they stay longer in their position.
<!-- para 7 -->
In addition to the scholarly contributions, the study’s findings also have several implications for practice.
First, our findings suggest that boards advocating for corporate restructuring strategies should consider hiring ex-military CEOs, particularly if they are considering cost reductions and refocusing the core business as their primary goals.
Given their strong leadership skill and preference for strategic focus, ex-military CEOs are more likely to fit better and perform well among firms preparing for corporate restructuring.
As our findings indicate, the impact of ex-military CEOs on restructuring is likely more nuanced in that they engage in more financial (asset sell-offs) and portfolio (acquisition) restructuring compared to their non-military counterparts but not necessarily organizational restructuring.
Accordingly, corporate boards could benefit from considering CEOs’ military backgrounds in hiring decisions and executive succession planning.
Further, while we found that ex-military CEOs are more likely to engage in corporate restructuring (specifically asset sell-offs and acquisitions), the findings also have a caveat: ex-military CEOs become less committed to pursuing these strategies as they stay longer in their positions.
Consequently, it seems particularly helpful if boards create the appropriate incentives and motivations for these CEOs to ensure that they stay engaged and not become ‘stale in the saddle’ ().
Finally, our findings suggest that ex-military CEOs’ approach to corporate restructuring is nuanced.
They embrace specific types of restructuring strategies that provide [financial stability](https://www.sciencedirect.com/topics/economics-econometrics-and-finance/financial-stability) in the short term (e.g., asset sell-off) and optimal portfolios in the long run (e.g., acquisition).
Accordingly, ex-military CEOs are likely to bring a more nuanced and precise influence in their choice of restructuring strategies.
<!-- para 8 -->
Despite the findings, this study is not without limitations.
First, we relied on a large-scale empirical test of our predictions.
While this is an established approach with its strengths, it does not allow for an in-depth [qualitative study](https://www.sciencedirect.com/topics/social-sciences/qualitative-research) of ex-military CEOs.
Future research may adopt this research design to provide a deeper insight into how such CEOs bring their military background to bear in their decision-making.
Furthermore, our findings do not consider various organizational (innovation vs. efficiency orientation) and industry (e.g., environmental dynamism, munificence) contingencies.
These and other similar contingencies have the potential to reveal interesting relationships between ex-military CEO leadership and the likelihood of corporate restructuring strategies.
Finally, while we explored the military backgrounds of CEOs, it may also be important, in the future, to study how having military veterans at the board and middle management level influences the firm’s strategic choices including restructuring decisions.
