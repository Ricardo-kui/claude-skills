# ceo_regulatory_focus_and_myopic_marketing_management 句子库存

## introduction
<!-- para 1 -->
In recent years, there have been considerable practitioner interests in the role of the CEO in affecting firms’ performances and the [personality traits](https://www.sciencedirect.com/topics/social-sciences/personality-trait) that successful CEOs possess ().
When providing the motivations for their study on the mindsets of successful CEOs, McKinsey & Company commented that there is little concrete understanding of how CEOs think and what they do to obtain superior firm performance ().
<!-- para 2 -->
CEOs need to deliver results for the short term and at the same time, they are expected to create long-term value for the firm.
These two demands often conflict with each other as actions that create long-term value generally do not result in superior shareholder returns in the near term but instead take time for any improvements to show up in firm performance ().
In a McKinsey study, find that the improvement in firm performance that comes with adopting a long-term focus happens only after the CEO has left the firm, and long-term focused CEOs underperform their peers during their tenure.
Thus, CEOs tend to adopt a short-term focus when making firm decisions ().
Such short-term decision-making has led to an estimated $79bn in foregone annual earnings over the period 1996 to 2018 at S&P 500 firms () and has prompted prominent business leaders such as Warren Buffett to call for a greater focus on long-term sustainable value creation ().
<!-- para 3 -->
The marketing function has not been spared from short-termism.
The 2019 August CMO Survey found that most firms manage their marketing for the present rather than prepare for the future ().
This provides evidence that myopia, which often stems from the CEO (), cascades to the marketing function as well.
In their survey of sales executives, document that requests for myopic marketing actions often originate from the CEO.
Therefore, to shift a firm’s focus onto the long term, it is imperative to understand the mental decision-making framework of key decision-makers, particularly that of the CEO ().
<!-- para 4 -->
In this paper, we propose a cognitive model of marketing myopia.
Deciding whether to engage in myopic marketing management involves a complex trade-off of short-term gains versus long-term losses (, ).
Drawing on psychology and management research, we theorize that a CEO’s motivational mindset as measured by her/his regulatory focus affects the CEO’s perceptions of the gains versus losses surrounding a myopic marketing decision.
Regulatory focus theory is especially apt for studying myopic marketing management because the theory focuses on an individual’s motivational orientation toward obtaining gains (promotion focus) and avoiding losses (prevention focus) ().
Using a panel of over 5,000 firm-year observations from close to 700 firms that span a broad cross-section of [industries](https://www.sciencedirect.com/topics/economics-econometrics-and-finance/industry), our results support the prediction that the regulatory foci of CEOs impact firms’ propensity to myopically manage marketing spending.
In particular, a change in the CEO’s promotion focus predominance from one [standard deviation](https://www.sciencedirect.com/topics/economics-econometrics-and-finance/measure-of-dispersion) below the mean to one standard deviation above the mean increases the likelihood of myopic marketing management from 16% to 19.4%.
This economic significance is especially meaningful when the unconditional probability of engaging in myopic marketing management is 17.7% in our sample.
<!-- para 5 -->
Further building on the central paradigm of gains versus losses, we draw on the myopic marketing management literature and propose three moderator variables that have been shown to affect the actual or perceived gains and losses involved in the myopic marketing decision. show that *equity-based compensation* increases the long-term personal gains to CEOs from maximizing long-run firm value. and document that external pressure from *equity analysts* increases the salience of short-term earnings benchmark and the focus on the short-term price gains to beating earnings benchmarks.
Finally, find that the long-term firm value loss when firms engage in myopic marketing management increases with *environmental turbulence*.
We find both statistical and economic significance for the impact of our three proposed moderator variables.
Finally, we show that myopic marketing management mediates the relationship between CEO regulatory focus and future firm performance.
<!-- para 6 -->
Apart from helping to address industry challenges in better understanding firm strategies, this paper adds to an important emerging area of research that intersects upper echelons theory and marketing strategy ().
Recent papers are starting to explore the marketing impact of top executives (, ).
However, a review by shows that there is still relatively little research into how top management psychology affects the marketing strategy-making process.
Most [marketing research](https://www.sciencedirect.com/topics/social-sciences/marketing-research) focuses on the marketing actions and decisions made while neglecting the decision-makers themselves.
We provide a missing piece to extant research by showing that CEO attributes in the form of their regulatory focus affect their propensity for marketing myopia, which in turn affects firm performance.
We also uncover the conditions that moderate the relationship between CEO regulatory focus and marketing myopia.
Through understanding the impact of CEO regulatory focus on marketing myopia, we answer the call by for incorporating the role of top executives in the marketing-finance interface research.
<!-- para 7 -->
In terms of methodology, we demonstrate a promising avenue of using textual analysis of company documents to understand marketing decision-making.
The marketing strategy decision-making process has often been treated as a black box by past literature due to the lack of easily accessible data on the personalities and traits of key decision-makers.
We add to the burgeoning literature in marketing that uses text analysis techniques to uncover key decision-makers’ personality traits ().
<!-- para 8 -->
We also add to the literature on how CEO regulatory focus affects firm behaviors (). highlights the papers that have examined the impacts of CEO regulatory focus on firm strategies among U.S. public firms.
Most of the papers focus on firms’ risk-taking strategies such as acquisitions, R&D investments, CSR investments, and strategic changes.
We differ from these papers by deviating from the risk-taking aspects and focusing on how a CEO evaluates the gains and losses surrounding a non-risk-related strategy, in particular a firm’s myopic marketing decision.
We thus provide a more layered understanding of the relationship between CEO regulatory focus and firm strategies beyond risk-taking.
Our results also highlight how when time is a constraint, promotion-focused is associated with more short-term decision-making, compared to when time is a resource ().

Our results demonstrating the firm value impact of CEO regulatory focus and how myopic marketing management mediates this impact is new to the literature.
The entrepreneurship literature has shown that promotion-focused entrepreneurs are associated with better firm performance among small, private firms ().
However, we show that in the case of a public firm where there is short-term pressure from the stock market to perform (), a CEO’s promotion focus negatively affects firm performance.
This effect comes partially through influencing the CEO’s propensity for myopic marketing management.
Our results highlight the importance of considering the decision-making context when examining the impact of CEO regulatory focus on firm performance.

We use the term “myopic marketing management” to imply the adoption of a short time horizon in marketing decisions to derive desirable near-term outcomes at the expense of long-term firm performances ().
Past literature attributes the myopic marketing management tendencies of managers to stock market pressures coming from the market’s constant fixation on quarterly earnings and high stock prices (e.g.,, ), equity analysts’ coverage (, ), and short-term oriented institutional investors (, ).
<!-- para 7 -->
In this paper, we examine one form of myopic marketing management, which the literature widely discusses—the cutting of marketing and R&D expenditures to inflate current earnings and avoid reporting an earnings shortfall (e.g.,,, ).
A firm that is facing short-term earnings pressure, i.e., its earnings are falling short of performance targets, has incentives to manage its earnings upwards to mitigate the shortfall.
If the firm does not do anything promptly, it faces the prospect of reporting a failure to meet earnings targets which often results in significant stock price declines ().
In contrast, the stock market is likely to reward firms with stock price increase when the firm makes earnings announcements that are in line or better than expectations, even if the reported earnings are likely a result of earnings management ().
<!-- para 9 -->
Regulatory focus theory is a branch of self-regulation theory that describes an individual’s self-management and motivational framework ().
In regulatory focus theory, there are two types of foci, promotion, and prevention focus.
Although both regulatory foci motivate people to move toward their desired end-states, they do so through different cognitive and behavioral processes ().
<!-- para 10 -->
Self-regulation, through a promotion focus, regulates growth needs and drives an individual towards her/his goals through advancements and accomplishments ().
In contrast, self-regulation through a prevention focus regulates security needs and consists of carrying out duties and obligations through safe and vigilant behaviors.
Promotion-focused individuals actively seek out desirable end-states driven by their aspirations while prevention-focused individuals seek to avoid undesirable end-states driven by their need for security ().
Therefore, success in a promotion focus is experienced as the presence of gains, and failure is experienced as the absence of gains (non-gains) ().
In contrast, success in a prevention focus is experienced as the absence of losses (non-losses) and failure is reflected as the presence of losses.
<!-- para 11 -->
Due to their emphasis on gains, promotion-focused individuals “play to win,” and exhibit behaviors that strategically move them toward their desired end states.
The strategic inclination of a promotion-focused individual is to ensure hits and insure against errors of omission ().
Conversely, prevention-focused individuals “play to not lose” and are concerned with avoiding losses ().
As such, they are in a state of constant vigilance in which they insure against committing errors when striving for their goals (), resulting in them often trying to preserve the status quo ().
<!-- para 12 -->
Similar to prior studies on CEO regulatory focus, we consider regulatory focus as a stable trait because research has shown that individuals display consistency in their regulatory focus over time ().
However, the motivational level for an action taken because of an individual’s regulatory focus can be affected by situational factors ().
Thus, we incorporate moderating effects in our model.
In addition, the literature often treats prevention and promotion foci as independent constructs rather than as opposite ends of a continuous spectrum ().
However, prior research has shown that in situations involving a tradeoff between obtaining gains and avoiding losses, regulatory focus predominance is likely to be a better predictor of behavior ().
An individual’s regulatory focus predominance is the relative strength of one’s promotion focus to one’s prevention focus ().
Given myopic decision-making involves trading off gains and losses, we will focus on regulatory focus predominance in this paper.
<!-- para 14 -->
Upper echelons theory emphasizes the fact that an organization’s strategies are reflections of the top management’s values and cognitive biases ().
Studies have repeatedly shown that the decision-making style of a firm’s top management, particularly its CEO, determines a firm’s strategic orientation ().
In particular, myopic directives have been shown to come directly from the CEO ().
This is not surprising since CEOs often get the brunt of any negative earnings surprise compared to the other executives ().
Thus, studying the decision-making framework of the CEO is key to understanding why firms engage in myopic marketing management.
<!-- para 15 -->
Myopic marketing management involves a tradeoff between short-term and long-term gains and losses.
The decision to adopt myopic marketing management practices, therefore, depends on how CEOs weigh the gains and losses across different time horizons. show that when an individual has more time to reach a goal, the individual is more inclined towards achievement and the presence of things desired.
The argument provided is that more distant goals give individuals greater opportunities for taking risks since that there is leeway for the correction of mistakes and time itself is a resource.
Thus, given the luxury of time to define their own goals, promotion-focused individuals tend to choose temporally distant goals which involve more risk-taking but at the same time increased possibility for greater achievements.
However, in the context of myopic marketing management, the CEO needs to manage the short-term earnings pressure from the stock market ().
Therefore, in contrast to the experiments in, time becomes a constraint when CEOs make myopic marketing management decisions, and the results in are unlikely to apply here.
As such, to understand the impact of CEO regulatory focus on CEO propensity to cut marketing and R&D investments in the face of short-term earnings pressure, we build our model based on the central theme of “gains versus losses” in regulatory focus theory.
We also complement our model by looking at how the CEO’s regulatory focus affects her/his attitutude towards change versus maintenance of status quo.
<!-- para 16 -->
***Evaluations of gains and losses.*** When facing a potential earnings shortfall, the CEO has the option of either doing nothing or engaging in myopic marketing management.
If the CEO does nothing and announces an earnings shortfall, stock prices are likely to fall in the near term ().
Conversely, if the CEO chooses to myopically manage marketing spending to inflate earnings, short-term stock prices are likely to react positively to the perception of the firm beating earnings targets ().
Such short-term stock price reactions to earnings announcements are likely to become more exaggerated as the salience of earnings benchmarks increases with heightened scrutiny from equity analysts ().
<!-- para 17 -->
Therefore, myopic marketing management in the face of a potential earnings shortfall requires the CEO to confront the possibility of a stock price decline (loss) versus a stock price increase (gain) in the near term.
In the long run, myopic marketing management leads to firm value losses (), especially in turbulent environments ().
However, if no cuts to the marketing and R&D budgets is made, the long-term firm value would be maintained.
By “maintaining long-term firm value,” we are referring to the firm value that would be in place if the budgets are not cut.
In other words, in the long run, the tradeoff involves losses (firm value decline) versus non-losses (firm value maintenance).
<!-- para 18 -->
Research has shown that a promotion-focused individual places greater reliance on positive information while a prevention-focused individual relies more on negative information, especially in a highly information-loaded decision-making process such as the case of myopic marketing management decision ().
Promotion-focused individuals are motivated by potential gains and are less sensitive to negative outcomes while prevention-focused individuals are the reverse ().
As a result of their focus on achievements and gains, the promotion-focused CEOs tend to take a more optimistic view of the future () and thus are less likely to be fixated with the potential long-term losses that come with myopic marketing management ().
Therefore, we expect predominantly promotion-focused CEOs to be motivated by the short-term stock price gains that come with myopic marketing management and will be less concerned with the long-term loss versus non-loss tradeoff.
<!-- para 19 -->
The decision-making process of the predominantly prevention-focused CEO is more complex.
If s/he does not engage in myopic marketing management, s/he is faced with the likelihood of stock price falling when reporting negative earnings.
However, if s/he engages in myopic marketing management, s/he is faced with the prospect of long-term firm value declines.
Therefore, to the prevention-focused CEO, there are two reference points on which to anchor the losses - the stock price that would prevail if the firm reports earnings that meet market expectations, and the long-term firm value that would prevail if the firm continues with the pre-determined marketing and R&D budget.
Prevention-focused CEOs are likely to consider both reference points when making their decision () and weigh the relative losses in the short-term and long-term.
In particular, shows that the long-term firm value loss associated with myopic marketing management is often much more than the temporary decrease in stock price when firms make negative earnings announcements.
As such, we would expect the predominantly prevention-focused CEO to be more sensitive to the larger long-term firm value decrease due to myopic marketing actions compared to the smaller short-term price decrease due to inaction.
Thus, the predominantly prevention-focused CEO’s orientation towards avoiding negative outcomes would prevent her/him from taking any myopic marketing action that leads to greater losses ().
<!-- para 20 -->
***Change versus maintenance of status quo***.
The conclusion from the above analysis is reinforced by how regulatory focus impacts the CEO’s view towards change versus status quo maintenance.
Promotion-focused individuals are not afraid to change (), are better at recognizing and exploiting opportunities (), and consider a larger set of choice alternatives when making decisions ().
In the case of myopic marketing management, maintaining the existing level of marketing and R&D investment involves maintenance of the status quo while a reduction of the investments involves a change from the pre-established budget and strategy plus a possibility for short-term gains.
Thus, when met with a potential earnings shortfall, promotion-focused CEOs are more likely to consider the alternatives and are more willing to deviate from the pre-agreed marketing budget and engage in myopic marketing management to avoid any “errors of omission” ().
<!-- para 21 -->
In contrast, the predominantly prevention-focused CEO favors an avoidance means for managing outcomes () and they are reluctant to take actions that may move them away from the status quo for fear of committing mistakes ().
The prevention-focused individual’s sensitivity to loss and a strong preference for vigilant strategies make her/him highly reluctant to take actions that will expose her/him to potentially greater losses (, ).
Due to the larger long-term value loss from myopic marketing management compared to short-term value loss from non-action, the cutting of marketing expenses to inflate reported earnings increases the chance of “errors of commission” which the predominantly prevention-focused CEO seeks to insure against ().
Thus, we expect the prevention-focused CEO to favor the status quo of inaction and would prefer sticking to the pre-established budget and strategy.
<!-- para 22 -->
***Hypothesis.*** As discussed previously, for situations involving a tradeoff between obtaining gains and avoiding losses, regulatory focus predominance is a better predictor of behavior ().
Although the above arguments are based on looking at promotion and prevention focus as separate constructs, we assume that regulatory focus predominance effectively explains myopic marketing management propensity.
The propensity for action is driven by what dominates an individual’s mental framework which in this case whether promotion or prevention focus dominates.
As such, we hypothesize:
<!-- para 23 -->
> *H1: Predominantly promotion-focused CEOs are more likely to engage in myopic marketing management, while predominantly prevention-focused CEOs are less likely to engage in myopic marketing management.*
<!-- para 25 -->
Managerial traits do not operate in isolation but often interact with situational factors to shape how managers interpret situations and, in turn, which strategies they adopt to deal with problems during decision-making ().
Given the centrality of the gains versus losses argument, we propose three moderating factors that affect the relative magnitude of perceived/actual gains versus losses surrounding the myopic marketing management decision.
In particular, we examine the moderating roles that CEO equity-based compensation, equity analyst coverage, and environmental turbulence have on the relationship between CEO regulatory focus and myopic marketing management.
<!-- para 26 -->
***CEO equity-based compensation.*** The board of directors has the fiduciary duty of ensuring the CEO operates the firm in a way that maximizes shareholder [wealth](https://www.sciencedirect.com/topics/economics-econometrics-and-finance/wealth).
One of the tools that directors have at their disposal to align the CEO’s interests with those of outside shareholders is through the design of the CEO’s compensation ().
In particular, equity-based compensation in the form of restricted stock and stock options is often awarded to CEOs to encourage them to place greater emphasis on long-term firm performance.
This is because such stocks and options are often issued on a time-based vesting schedule and the value of the stocks and options owned by the CEOs are dependent on the future share price of their firms ().
The greater the stock price increase over the long term, the more the CEO gains. find that equity-based compensation helps encourage long-term marketing investments among firms, indicating that equity-based compensation can encourage CEOs to operate the firm with long-term firm value maximization in mind.
<!-- para 27 -->
Predominantly promotion-focused CEOs’ emphases on gains make them more susceptible to priming towards a focus on the potential long-term gains resulting from owning stocks and stock options ().
Thus, increased equity-based compensation switches the orientation of the promotion-focused CEO towards an emphasis on the long-term gains that they can obtain from their equity-based compensation.
Therefore, in the presence of the possibility of substantial long-term gains from their equity-based compensation, we will expect predominantly promotion-focused CEOs to be more likely to desist from myopic actions to maximize their personal long-term gains.
In contrast, we would not expect prevention-focused CEOs who are focused on avoiding losses to react much to the possibility of long-term gains from their equity-based compensation, and thus, they will fall back on their intrinsic motivation arising from their regulatory focus ().
Therefore, we hypothesize:
<!-- para 28 -->
> *H2: CEO equity-based compensation decreases the likelihood of predominantly promotion-focused CEOs engaging in myopic marketing management but does not affect the likelihood of predominantly prevention-focused CEOs engaging in myopic marketing management, such that the positive relationship between CEO promotion focus predominance and myopic marketing management is mitigated as the CEO compensation structure is increasingly equity-based.*
<!-- para 29 -->
***Analyst coverage.*** Financial analysts who are employed by investment banks are vital intermediaries between firms and outside investors.
They gather information about a firm from various sources, scrutinize and process the information disclosed by the firm in its financial statements, and finally evaluate the firm.
Based on these evaluations, they make forecasts about the firm’s short-term earnings and issue recommendation calls on the company stock.
Past literature has often criticized that the intense scrutiny and emphasis on short-term earnings benchmarks from financial analysts have aggravated myopic corporate behavior (, ).
Firms failing to meet analysts’ earnings expectations experience a decline in stock price while firms beating analysts’ expectations are rewarded with stock price increases even if the firms achieved the results through earnings management ().
Furthermore, papers have shown that as the number of analysts covering the firm increases, the pressure to engage in myopic actions mounts due to the increased salience of short-term earnings benchmarks (, ).
Therefore, we expect that the pressure from increased analyst coverage will likely amplify the focus on gains for a predominantly promotion-focused CEO as s/he seeks to live up to the short-term earnings expectations.
In other words, an increase in analyst coverage accentuates the promotion focus of the CEO and strengthens her/his motivation for myopic marketing management.
<!-- para 30 -->
The increased salience of short-term earnings benchmarks from increased analyst scrutiny might also increase the focus on the short-term stock price declines when firms do not meet earnings expectations, causing the prevention-focused CEO to be relatively more likely to engage in myopic marketing management.
However, this is unlikely to be the case given results from prior research that show analysts can detect future firm underperformance which leads to downgrades in their recommendations causing worse stock price declines ().
Through the process of gathering and disseminating information, financial analysts serve an essential monitoring role in the [corporate governance](https://www.sciencedirect.com/topics/social-sciences/corporate-governance) process and keep CEOs from engaging in malfeasance and rent-seeking behavior ().
Given the scrutiny from financial analysts, predominantly prevention-focused CEOs would likely desist from myopic management of marketing spending as they guard against committing errors ().
We expect predominantly prevention-focused CEOs to seek to avoid the situation where analysts will find out that they are manipulating short-term earnings and as a result for the analysts to issue downgrades in their recommendations.
Such a vigilant strategic inclination by the CEOs is likely to be intensified when more analysts are scrutinizing the stock.
Given the totality of the arguments, we posit:
<!-- para 31 -->
> *H3: Analyst coverage increases the likelihood of predominantly promotion-focused CEOs engaging in myopic marketing management and decreases the likelihood of predominantly prevention-focused CEOs engaging in myopic marketing management, such that the positive relationship between CEO promotion focus predominance and myopic marketing management is amplified as analyst coverage increases.*
<!-- para 32 -->
***Environmental turbulence.*** We hypothesize that CEOs’ regulatory focus affects their propensity for myopic marketing management due to their differences in evaluating gains and losses. show that as environmental turbulence increases, the long-term value loss from myopic marketing management increases.
This arises from the fact that environmental turbulence, defined as the level of volatility and instability in the business environment, is an important contingent factor that affects the relationship between firm strategies and organizational performance ().
<!-- para 33 -->
Turbulence or instability in the business environment can arise from rapidly changing customer preferences and expectations, technological advancements, and fierce competitive pressure ().
As environmental turbulence increases, the importance of continuous investments in marketing and R&D assets increases ().
A dynamic environment places a greater demand on firms to constantly adapt, invest, and reconfigure their resources to meet the changing environmental needs ().
Previous knowledge of customers and competitors quickly becomes outdated, requiring constant investments to better understand customer needs ().
Continuous investments in innovation and R&D are needed to keep up with the changing technology and customer demands ().
Therefore, any disruptions to marketing and R&D budgets will lead to a bigger loss in long-term firm value when environmental turbulence increases ().
<!-- para 34 -->
In the presence of environmental turbulence, the long-term value loss to myopic marketing management is higher.
Therefore, the prevention-focused CEO who weighs the near-term stock price loss associated with reporting a negative earnings announcement and the long-term firm value loss of myopic actions would be further dis-inclined to myopically manage marketing spending in the presence of environmental turbulence.
Furthermore, the turbulent environment forces CEOs to make their decisions quickly () and increases their tendency to fall back on their cognitive biases (), accentuating the effect of regulatory focus on CEOs’ decision-making.
Therefore, we expect environmental turbulence to further reduce the myopic marketing management tendencies of predominantly prevention-focused CEOs.
<!-- para 35 -->
For the promotion-focused CEO who tend to focus on positive outcomes rather than negative outcomes during decision-making (), increased long-term firm value loss from myopic marketing management when the environment is more turbulent may have no impact on the decision-making of these CEOs who are motivated mainly by gains.
However, increased environmental turbulence also implies increased opportunities for promotion-focused CEOs to exploit ().
First, when environmental turbulences are low, customer demand, technology, and competitor actions are more stable creating lesser opportunities for the promotion-focused CEOs to explore ().
Conversely, when environmental turbulences are high, they create future opportunities for the promotion-focused CEOs to make up for the firm value shortfalls due to her/his current myopic marketing actions.
Second, in turbulent environments, marketing investments are associated with bigger gains and losses than the same investments made under stable environments ().
This makes it easier for the promotion-focused CEO to make up for any potential performance shortfalls if they make the right investment decisions in the future.
Third, like the prevention-focused CEO, a turbulent environment accentuates the effect of the CEOs’ regulatory focus on their decision-making.
Thus, we expect a turbulent business environment to aggravate the myopic marketing management tendency of the predominantly promotion-focused CEO.
<!-- para 36 -->
Given the entirety of our arguments, we therefore hypothesize:
<!-- para 37 -->
> *H4: Environmental turbulence increases the likelihood of predominantly promotion-focused CEOs engaging in myopic marketing management and decreases the likelihood of predominantly prevention-focused CEOs engaging in myopic marketing management, such that the positive relationship between CEO promotion focus predominance and myopic marketing management is amplified as environmental turbulence increases.*
<!-- para 39 -->
To understand the mediating effects of myopic marketing management on the relationship between CEO regulatory focus and firm performance, we need to first understand how myopic marketing management affects future firm performance (measured using firm value change).
Myopic marketing management has been shown to lead to long-term firm value declines (, ).
Continuous investments in marketing assets are important for maintaining brand equity and firm value ().
Cutting marketing spending to boost short-term earnings harms a firm’s ability to generate long-term cash flows as market-based assets such as customer relationships and product offerings require continuous investments to develop and maintain.
Indeed, document that myopic marketing management is associated with deteriorations of market-based assets, leading to an increased incidence of product recalls.
<!-- para 40 -->
Prior research on the impacts of CEO regulatory focus has generally focused on how CEO regulatory focus impacts the different risky strategies firms undertake (see ).
However, the link between CEO regulatory focus on firm performance among large public firms has not been explored empirically. examine the performance impact of CEO regulatory focus in small, private firms and find that a promotion-focused CEO is associated with better firm performance while a prevention-focused CEO is associated with worse performance.
However, small, private firms differ from public firms ().
In particular, private firms do not face short-term earnings pressure like public firms do ().
To the extent that CEO regulatory focus predominance impacts the tendency of firms to undertake myopic marketing management, we therefore hypothesize:
<!-- para 41 -->
> *H5: Myopic marketing management has a partial mediating effect on the relationship between CEO regulatory focus predominance and future firm value change.*
<!-- para 42 -->
We expect the mediating effects to be partial because changes in firm value can be affected by many factors, not just marketing myopia.
Furthermore, as discussed before, CEO regulatory focus also affects firm risk-taking which also affects firm performance.

The myopic actions of firms are not directly observable to researchers.
Therefore, we need to infer whether firms have engaged in myopic marketing management from the accounting numbers.
We follow the procedures from, who have developed [econometric](https://www.sciencedirect.com/topics/economics-econometrics-and-finance/econometrics) techniques for identifying potential short-term behavior in managing discretionary spending.
We classify a firm as having potentially engaged in short-term actions if it simultaneously records surprisingly high earnings, an abnormally low marketing spending, and an unexpectedly low R&D spending.
Such firms are likely to have decreased their spending to inflate current period earnings.
In particular, we use the following [fixed effects](https://www.sciencedirect.com/topics/social-sciences/fixed-effects-model) autoregressive panel data forecast model, adjusted for the firm and year-specific fixed effects, to compute the expected levels of marketing spending, R&D spending, and earnings, respectively.(1) $\mathit{MKT}_{i , t} = \alpha_{\mathit{MKT} , i} + \delta_{\mathit{MKT}} \mathit{MKT}_{i , t - 1} + \sum \varphi_{\mathit{MKT} , t} \mathit{Year}_{t} + \epsilon_{\mathit{MKT} , i , t}$ (2) $\left(R \& D\right)_{i , t} = \alpha_{\mathit{RD} , i} + \delta_{\mathit{RD}} \left(R \& D\right)_{i , t - 1} + \sum \varphi_{\mathit{RD} , t} \mathit{Year}_{t} + \epsilon_{\mathit{RD} , i , t}$ (3) $\mathit{ROA}_{i , t} = \alpha_{\mathit{ROA} , i} + \delta_{\mathit{ROA}} \mathit{ROA}_{i , t - 1} + \sum \varphi_{\mathit{ROA} , t} \mathit{Year}_{t} + \epsilon_{\mathit{ROA} , i , t}$

*MKT <sub>i,t</sub>, R&D <sub>i,t</sub>* and *ROA <sub>i,t</sub>* are the marketing spending, R&D spending, and return on assets (ROA), respectively for firm *i* in year *t*.
<!-- para 9 -->
Following, *MKT <sub>i,t</sub>* is measured as sales, general, and administrative expense (SG&A) minus R&D expenditures, scaled by beginning period book assets. *R&D <sub>i,t</sub>* is defined as R&D expenditures scaled by beginning period book assets.
We measure earnings using ROA, the ratio of operating income before extraordinary items to the beginning period asset value.
<!-- para 10 -->
*α <sub>MKT,i</sub>*, *α <sub>RD,i</sub>,* and *α <sub>ROA,i</sub>* are the firm-specific intercept terms. *δ <sub>MKT</sub>*, *δ <sub>RD</sub>,* and *δ <sub>ROA</sub>* are the estimates of autoregressive coefficients for each equation, and *Year <sub>t</sub>* is a set of year fixed effects.
We use the residuals from the regressions, *ε <sub>MKT,i,t</sub>*, *ε <sub>RD,i,t</sub>*, and *ε <sub>ROA,i,t</sub>* to identify unexpected levels of marketing spending, R&D spending, and ROA, respectively, for firm *i* in year *t*.
Firms where *ε <sub>MKT,i,t</sub>* and *ε <sub>RD,i,t</sub>* are negative while *ε <sub>ROA,i,t</sub>* is positive, simultaneously, are classified as potentially engaged in myopic marketing management.
In particular, *Incidence (MMM)* is an indicator variable equals to one if *ε <sub>MKT,i,t</sub>* < 0, *ε <sub>RD,i,t</sub>* < 0, and *ε <sub>ROA,i,t</sub>* > 0, and zero otherwise.

We follow the procedure used in, who use CEO letters to shareholders to estimate the promotion and prevention foci of CEOs.
Research has provided evidence to support the claim that CEOs either personally write the letter or are highly involved in proofreading the contents ().
CEOs are personally responsible for the contents of the letters as they are responsible for the organizational outcomes promised or implied in the letters to shareholders ().
Finally, document within-person consistency in the content of the letters for the same CEO, indicating that the letters are consistently written by the same person.
<!-- para 14 -->
developed and carefully validated dictionaries to measure CEOs’ promotion and prevention foci.
We make use of KNIME Analytics Platform to mine the CEOs’ letters using these dictionaries and alternative forms of the words in the dictionaries.
We extract a total of 497,967 sentences from these letters, of which we flag 145,573 sentences to be promotion-related sentences, and 23,828 are flagged to be prevention-related sentences.
To control for the length of the annual reports, we scale the sentence counts by the total number of sentences in the CEO letter.
On average, 31% of the sentences in the CEO letter have promotion-related words while 4% of the sentences have prevention-related words.
<!-- para 15 -->
*CEO promotion focus predominance* is defined as the proportion of sentences in the CEO letter with promotion-related words minus the proportion of sentences with prevention-related words.
We standardize the proportion of sentences with promotion-related words and the proportion of sentences with prevention-related words to have means of zero and [standard deviations](https://www.sciencedirect.com/topics/economics-econometrics-and-finance/measure-of-dispersion) of one first before taking the difference.
The motivation for standardization is because there are many more sentences with promotion-related words than prevention-related words, and the standard deviation for the former is also much higher.
Finally, *CEO promotion focus predominance* is lagged one period with respect to the outcome variable such that the CEO motivational trait is measured before the measurement of the outcome variable.

We measure analyst coverage using the number of analysts covering the firm ().
Firms with more comprehensive analyst coverage come under greater scrutiny and pressure.
In particular, we measure the number of distinct analysts issuing earnings estimates within a year of the earnings announcement and then take the natural logarithm of one plus the number of analysts.
Adding one allows us to keep firms with zero analyst following.
<!-- para 19 -->
Following,, we include both restricted stock and stock option awards and measure the proportion of the CEO’s annual compensation that is in the form of such equity-based compensation.
<!-- para 20 -->
We measure environmental turbulence using the median stock [return volatility](https://www.sciencedirect.com/topics/economics-econometrics-and-finance/returns-volatility) of all firms in the same industry in the same year, where an industry is defined by the Fama-French 49 industry classification.
Stock return volatility is measured as the standard deviation of the daily stock returns over the fiscal year and is a measure of the degree of variation in the firm’s stock price over time.
Since stock prices are discounted values of future profits, variations in stock prices will reflect uncertainty about future earnings ().
Firms in more turbulent environments face more uncertainty about future performance and thus they have higher stock return volatility ().
Therefore, stock return volatility will be a good approximation of the environmental turbulence faced by the firms.
We use the industry median stock return volatility to proxy for the environmental turbulence since firms in the same industry generally face similar levels of environmental turbulence.
<!-- para 21 -->
We mean-center all the moderating variables before including them in the regressions.
This allows for easier interpretation of the coefficient on *CEO promotion focus predominance*, which is now interpreted as the impact of the CEO’s regulatory focus on the incidence of myopic marketing management for a firm with average levels of analyst coverage, CEO equity-based compensation, and environmental turbulence.
<!-- para 23 -->
We control for several variables that will impact a firm’s likelihood to cut marketing and R&D spending in response to normal business conditions ().
We control for firm size using the natural logarithm of book assets and control for the firm’s internal resources with the ratio of cash holdings to assets.
To account for investment conditions, we control for Tobin’s *Q* and sales growth.
To ensure that the CEO regulatory focus measures are not proxying for variables that correlate with the short-term pressure faced by the firm, we also control for various firm and CEO characteristics that have been shown to affect the myopic marketing actions of firms.
First, we control for book leverage ().
Next, we include the buy-and-hold stock return over the fiscal year and stock return volatility as additional controls ().
We also control for the percentage of common shares held by short-term transient institutional investors ().
In addition, we include an indicator variable for whether the CEO has marketing experience ().
Finally, we control for the natural logarithm of the CEO’s age as younger CEOs have a longer potential career horizon and therefore, are less inclined to make short-termist decisions ().
All control variables and moderating variables are lagged by one year with respect to the measurement of the spending cuts.
Continuous variables are winsorized at the 1% level in both tails to account for outliers.
<!-- para 24 -->
As *Incidence (MMM)* is a binary variable, we use a logit specification.
We include year-fixed effects to control for market-wide [economic conditions](https://www.sciencedirect.com/topics/economics-econometrics-and-finance/macroeconomic-performance) and industry-fixed effects (measured at the Fama-French 49 industry classification) to control for industry-wide practices.
Following the recommendations of, we estimate the regressions with standard errors clustered at the firm level to take into account the within-firm correlation in the residuals.
<!-- para 26 -->
We follow the recommendations of and use the change in *Total Q* from as our metric of firm performance. argue that the traditional Tobin’s Q measure which makes use of accounting-based approximations of the replacement value in the denominator is biased, especially for [marketing research](https://www.sciencedirect.com/topics/social-sciences/marketing-research) purposes.
This is because market-based [intangible assets](https://www.sciencedirect.com/topics/economics-econometrics-and-finance/intangible-asset) such as brand equity are not recorded in the firm’s accounting statements and are therefore not included as part of the denominator in the traditional Tobin’s Q calculations. *Total Q* accounts for a firm’s intangible knowledge capital and organizational capital in the denominator by cumulating the firm’s past investment in R&D and SG&A using the perpetual inventory method, and can thus overcome the various shortcomings of the traditional Tobin’s Q.
<!-- para 27 -->
The impacts of myopic marketing management are likely to be felt in the longer term ().
Therefore, we analyze changes in *Total Q* up to 3 years into the future, i.e., Year *t* + 1, Year *t* + 2, Year *t* + 3, where Year *t* is the year when we measure the myopic management action.
The change is measured relative to Year *t* -1, before any myopic actions that might affect firm performance.
Following and, we use a bootstrapping approach to conduct our mediation analysis.

The results we observed might be driven by reverse causality whereby firms which are intending to undertake myopic marketing management in the future choose CEOs who are more promotion focus.
Under this alternative hypothesis, we would expect that the results are concentrated among new CEOs as the matching of CEOs to firms’ needs would be strongest when the CEO is just hired ().
We do not find that the impact of CEO regulatory focus is concentrated among new CEOs.
Thus, the matching of CEO regulatory focus to firms’ myopic intentions is unlikely to explain our results.

Our results continue to hold under the 2SLS specification.

***Measure of CEO regulatory focus.*** We check the robustness of our results to using a word-count-based measure of *CEO promotion focus predominance* where we count the percentage of words in the annual letter that are promotion-related and prevention-related.
Second, some sentences contain multiple promotion-related words and/ or multiple prevention-related words.
To account for such cases, we consider a sentence to be promotion-related (prevention-related) only if the number of promotion-related words in the sentence is more (less) than the number of prevention-related words.
Using these two new measures, we find support for H1, H3, and H4.
The moderating effects of CEO equity-based compensation, although continue to be negative, becomes insignificant.
<!-- para 22 -->
***Sampling period.*** We check the robustness of our results to the deletion of the years 2007 to 2009 surrounding the 2008 financial crisis and year 2015 where we have very few sample observations.
We find support for H1, H3, and H4, but not H2.
<!-- para 23 -->
***Measure of environmental turbulence.*** We check that our results are robust to an alternative measure of environmental turbulence.
Following, we measure firm stock [return volatility](https://www.sciencedirect.com/topics/economics-econometrics-and-finance/returns-volatility) using the standard deviation of the return residuals from the Fama-French four-factor [pricing](https://www.sciencedirect.com/topics/social-sciences/pricing) model.
Then we define environmental turbulence as the median [stock return](https://www.sciencedirect.com/topics/economics-econometrics-and-finance/capital-market-returns) volatility across all firms in the same Fama-French 49 industry for the year.

## discussion
<!-- para 2 -->
Our study seeks to understand the role of CEO regulatory focus in influencing the propensity for myopic marketing management among public firms.
Prior research has shown that firms respond to short-term earnings pressure from the stock market, equity analysts, and short-term oriented institutional investors by adopting myopic marketing strategies (,,,, ).
In this study, we provide further insight into the decision-making process that affects firms’ propensity to react to the short-term earnings pressure by incorporating upper echelons theory from the strategy and management literature.
In doing so, we answer the calls by several review papers to better understand the role of top management in the marketing decision-making process (,, ).
<!-- para 3 -->
We show that predominantly promotion-focused CEOs who are motivated mainly by gains are more likely to engage in myopic marketing management to benefit from the temporary stock price increase, which comes from meeting or beating earnings expectations ().
Conversely, predominantly prevention-focused CEOs who are motivated mainly by avoiding losses are less prone to such short-termist actions.
These prevention-focused CEOs’ strategic actions are driven by the greater long-term firm value loss that comes with myopic marketing management as compared to the short-term stock price decline from not taking myopic marketing actions and just reporting their bad earnings ().
These results are also consistent with the predictions from regulatory focus theory where the strategic inclination of prevention-focused CEOs is to insure against acts of *commission* while that of the promotion-focused CEO is one where they insure against acts of *omission*.
Thus, predominantly prevention-focused CEOs are more inclined towards maintaining the status quo of not cutting the pre-set marketing and R&D budgets to avoid the mistake of incurring greater long-term firm value loss.
Conversely, the predominantly promotion-focused CEOs are more eager to engage in actions such as myopic marketing management that allow them to make short-term gains.
<!-- para 4 -->
Our results also add to the understanding of the impact of CEO regulatory focus on firm behavior and firm temporal orientation. document that predominantly promotion-focused CEOs have higher R&D and advertising intensity because of their focus on the huge gains that come with such risky investments compared to predominantly prevention-focused CEOs who avoid such investments due to the high potential loss.
The results in reflect the argument in that when time is a resource, promotion-focused individuals tend to have temporally distant goals because chasing such goals involve more time and risk-taking but at the same time comes with more possibility for greater achievements.
However, in this paper, we are not studying the levels of risky spending but the likelihood of decreasing such expenditures from a firm’s expected norms.
A firm can have high levels of marketing spending but may choose to temporarily reduce its spending.
Importantly, we take into account the short-term earnings pressure faced by firms and study whether firms decrease their marketing spending with the specific goal of boosting their earnings.
Our dependent variable is multi-dimensional—it not only accounts for the unexpected cuts in spending but also the unexpected increase in performance.
Thus, our dependent variable specifically measures cuts in spendings with the intention of boosting earnings numbers.
<!-- para 5 -->
Taken together with, the results show that promotion-focused CEOs may invest more in advertising and R&D on average but when faced with short-term earnings pressure to meet earnings expectations, they are more eager to take actions and cut such investments to capture any short-term gains and avoid errors of omission.
The results in our paper highlight that the link between regulatory focus and temporal orientation is much more nuanced than previously hypothesized.
It depends on the unique circumstances surrounding the decision-making, in particular, the patterns of gains and losses and whether time is a resource or constraint.
We show that when time is a constraint as in the case of a firm facing short-term earnings pressure, predominantly promotion-focused CEOs may choose the more short-term option that allows them to capture the gains.
Furthermore, given that changes in long-term firm value are distant events, this allows the promotion-focused CEO opportunities for taking risks and implement actions to correct the performance shortfalls instead ().
It would be interesting for future research to examine subsequent actions taken by the CEOs, for example, whether and how promotion-focused CEOs compensate for the temporary cuts in marketing spending in the future.
<!-- para 6 -->
In relating CEO regulatory focus to myopic marketing management, we highlight the importance of considering CEO psychological traits in understanding why firms and CEOs engage in short-term behavior that hurts long-term firm value.
We answer the call by to clarify the cognitions behind time-oriented decision-making.
We detail the mechanisms on how CEO regulatory focus affects the salience of the gains versus losses involved in myopic decision-making, and how such CEO psychological attributes interact with internal CEO equity-based compensation, external pressure from equity analysts, and environmental turbulence to affect CEOs’ myopic marketing propensities.
<!-- para 7 -->
We also extend existing literature that examines the role financial analysts play in contributing to the short-term earnings pressure faced by firms and CEOs (, ).
Our findings on the interaction between analyst scrutiny and CEO regulatory foci highlight the complicated nature of the role of financial analysts in the [corporate governance](https://www.sciencedirect.com/topics/social-sciences/corporate-governance) process.
While some papers have argued that equity analysts help monitor against wrongdoing on the part of the CEO, other papers have criticized that intense scrutiny from financial analysts causes myopic behaviors among firms.
Our results suggest that whether equity analysts improve monitoring or aggravate short-term earnings pressure depends on the CEO’s regulatory focus.
Prevention-focused CEOs are likely to respond to increased scrutiny from equity analysts by further desisting from myopic actions to avoid being caught doing the wrong thing while promotion-focused CEOs are likely to see increased analyst scrutiny as further impetus for short-term earnings pressure.
<!-- para 8 -->
Finally, we provide a more holistic understanding of how CEO regulatory focus affects firm marketing behavior and ultimately firm performance ().
Whilst studies on small, private firms have found that promotion-focused CEOs are associated with better firm performance relative to prevention-focused CEOs (), we show that predominantly promotion-focused CEOs may not necessarily be good for firm performance when there is short-term pressure to perform.
These results corroborate with those in who find that there is a dark side to predominantly promotion-focused CEOs as they tend to be embroiled in more marketing controversies.
<!-- para 10 -->
Our paper sheds light on a pressing issue that practitioners are trying to understand – how do CEOs impact firm value ().
We find that an increase in *CEO promotion-focus predominance* from the 25th percentile to the 75th percentile leads to a 6.14% reduction in the change in long-term *Total Q*.
A back-of-the-envelope calculation based on the average replacement cost of both physical and [intangible capital](https://www.sciencedirect.com/topics/economics-econometrics-and-finance/intangible-capital) given in Table 1 of implies that a 25th percentile to 75th percentile shift in *CEO promotion focus predominance* will cost the firm $102 million in long-term firm value.
And this cost is partially due to the firm myopically cutting investments in response to short-term earnings pressure.
<!-- para 11 -->
Given the huge negative value impacts of CEO promotion focus predominance on long-term firm performance, firms must take a holistic view of how CEO regulatory focus impacts the various strategies firm undertake and the circumstances when each type of CEO regulatory focus may be more valuable.
High levels of promotion focus among CEOs may be valuable for firms because these CEOs tend to explore new opportunities for future growth and they tend to work towards a more holistic approach to [stakeholder management](https://www.sciencedirect.com/topics/social-sciences/stakeholder-management) involving multiple goals ().
However, our results show that when short-term earnings pressure is acute, a predominantly promotion-focused CEO may result in the detrimental practice of myopic marketing management that hurts long-term firm value while a prevention-focused orientation may be beneficial for firm value instead.
Thus, the board of directors needs to pay special attention to the fit between CEO regulatory type and firm needs and environment.
<!-- para 12 -->
The board of directors should take into consideration a potential candidate’s regulatory focus when choosing the right CEO for the firm and the firm’s operating business environment.
The regulatory focus determines the likely marketing strategic actions the CEO will take when under short-term earnings pressure.
For example, we find that the economic impact of CEO promotion focus predominance on the likelihood of myopic marketing management is 8 percentage points when analyst coverage is at one standard deviation above the mean.
This contrasts with a close to zero economic impact when analyst coverage is at one standard deviation below the mean.
Therefore, if the firm faces intense scrutiny from equity analysts for short-term earnings performance and when continuous investments in marketing assets are especially important, firms may be better off choosing a predominantly prevention-focused CEO.
To facilitate the assessment of CEO candidates, the board could use the questionnaire from which is adapted from.
The questionnaire is also valuable to the CEOs themselves as a better self-understanding allows them to capitalize on the positive aspect of their natural tendencies and avoid the negative pitfalls.
<!-- para 13 -->
Although we hypothesize that environmental turbulence amplifies the positive relationship between CEO promotion focus predominance and the likelihood of myopic marketing management, our results do not support the hypothesis fully.
In particular, we do not find that environmental turbulence amplifies the promotion-focused CEO’s tendency to engage in myopic marketing management.
This is good news for firms operating in turbulent environments where the ability to capture opportunities and find new growth avenues is especially important ().
Thus, firms operating in highly uncertain environments might consider hiring predominantly promotion-focused CEOs over predominantly prevention-focused CEOs to capture the gains that might come with the ability to act fast and boldly in a dynamic environment ().
However, in additional results, we find some evidence that turbulent environments further reduce the prevention-focused CEOs' tendency to engage in myopic marketing management.
In particular, the likelihood of myopic marketing management drops from 22% to 12% as CEO prevention focus increases among firms with high environmental turbulence.
The corresponding drop is negligible for firms facing stable business conditions.
These results suggest the beneficial impact of prevention-focused CEOs for firms facing high environmental turbulence.
Given the higher cost of myopic marketing management when environmental turbulence is high (), further research would have to be done to examine the net impact of CEO regulatory focus on firm performance in a highly turbulent environment.
<!-- para 14 -->
To curtail the negative myopic marketing tendencies of the promotion-focused CEO especially when having a promotion focus is important, our results suggest that directors should pay greater attention to the design of effective incentive systems that cater to the CEOs’ regulatory focus.
In particular, we find that the economic impact of CEO promotion focus predominance on myopic marketing management is reduced from 6.50% to 1.08% when CEO equity compensation increases from one standard deviation below the mean to one standard deviation above the mean.
Therefore, the attenuating effect of equity-based compensation on the myopic marketing tendencies of promotion-focused CEOs highlights the efficacy of equity-based compensation in encouraging a long-term focus for CEOs who are predominantly focused on gains.
<!-- para 15 -->
Finally, the board might take a more holistic approach towards structuring the top management team and consider including individuals who are predominantly prevention focused especially when the CEO is relatively promotion focused.
This allows the CEO to draw insights from colleagues who have complementary assessments of the strategic decisions to derive more balanced strategic actions ().
In particular, the regulatory focus of the CMO may be especially important in countering/complementing the impact of the CEO’s regulatory focus on marketing actions ().
<!-- para 17 -->
We have examined a specific form of earnings manipulation.
Such actions relate to real earnings management as opposed to accounting accrual-based earnings management ().
Real earnings management involves manipulating real business activities to avoid reporting earnings losses or declines.
Accounting accrual-based earnings management, on the other hand, consists of changing accounting practices and manipulating [discretionary accruals](https://www.sciencedirect.com/topics/economics-econometrics-and-finance/discretionary-accruals) to book earnings gains.
Research has shown that real earnings management and accrual-based earnings management are substitutes ().
Future work can examine the impact of CEO regulatory focus on accrual-based earnings management.
Furthermore, in this paper, we only examine a specific form of myopic marketing decision, the cutting of discretionary marketing and R&D spending to boost performance.
Firms may also engage in myopic [revenue management](https://www.sciencedirect.com/topics/social-sciences/revenue-management) where they provide incentives to customers to bring forward sales, shifting earnings from the future to the present ().
Future work can examine how CEO regulatory focus affects the likelihood of adopting one form of myopic action over another.
<!-- para 18 -->
We have focused on the impact of the CEO.
A CEO does not operate in a vacuum.
In particular, marketing decisions are often undertaken together in consultation with the CMO, who influences marketing budgets ().
In untabulated tests, we do not find that the presence of a CMO impacts the relationship between CEO regulatory focus and myopic marketing management.
However, future work can look at how the interaction between the regulatory focus of the CMO interacts with that of the CEO to impact firm marketing strategies and decisions ().
Future research may thus also benefit from uncovering new data sources that allow the measurement of CMO regulatory focus.
<!-- para 20 -->
Although all firms, to a certain degree, face short-term earnings pressure, it is unclear why some CEOs are more likely to succumb to the demand by manipulating their earnings, compared to others.
Our study highlights the importance of examining the role of the CEO’s regulatory focus in affecting the propensity of the CEO to myopically manage marketing and R&D spending to inflate earnings.
We also uncover a channel through which CEO regulatory focus affects firm performance—through the impact of myopic marketing management.
