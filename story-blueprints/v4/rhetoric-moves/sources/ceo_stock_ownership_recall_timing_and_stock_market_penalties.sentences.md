# ceo_stock_ownership_recall_timing_and_stock_market_penalties 句子库存

## introduction
<!-- para 2 -->
Contrary to popular belief, firm executives—not regulators—decide whether and when to recall faulty medical devices in the United States.
Although the U.S. Food and Drug Administration (FDA) establishes guidelines for when to recall faulty products, they rely upon firms to actually initiate recalls ([21]).
Although some recalls occur immediately after a problem is discovered, many are delayed for weeks, months, or even years.
As described in our opening epigram, executives became aware of a potential battery and wire-connection failure in one of their pacemakers nearly two years before initiating a recall, subjecting heart patients to extended product safety and reliability risks ([54]).
Fifteen injuries and one death are believed to have occurred prior to recall initiation ([56]).
Unfortunately, despite the potential human costs, such examples of recall delays are all too common in the medical device industry.
Patients, physicians, and regulators are left wondering why this is and what can be done to shift this paradigm.
One particularly opportune area to explore is how firm leadership, such as the chief executive officer (CEO), may influence recall timing.
<!-- para 3 -->
Our review of the literature uncovered three studies that spotlight a connection between a firm's CEO and recalls, though none of them examine recall timing. [66] found that recalls are more likely to occur after a CEO is issued more stock options.
Specifically, firms whose CEOs are incentivized by stock options may take greater design, production, and distribution risks in the hopes of driving up the firm's stock price—a byproduct of which is more defective products that need to be recalled. [43] found that firms with CEOs whose compensation is weighted more toward stock and options are more likely to provide full recall remedies to consumers, perhaps in an effort to restore consumer trust and firm reputation following a recall.
Most recently, [45] found that firms with CEOs who are later in their tenure tend to steer clear of initiating recalls, seemingly to avoid the financial and reputational consequences.
We build upon this nascent stream of research by examining whether a CEO's stock ownership influences the speed with which the firm initiates medical device recalls.
Specifically, we investigate whether the amount of stock the CEO owns shapes the time-to-recall—that is, the time elapsed between when the firm first became aware of a defect and when it actually initiated a recall ([67]).
A single recall can have very different implications for the firm and for the individuals who rely on the product depending on how long the firm takes to initiate it, but such implications are not reflected in outcomes such as recall likelihood ([66]) or recall hazard ([45]).
Understanding the effects of CEO stock ownership on recall timing is thus crucial because delays put public safety at prolonged risk, especially for medical devices with dangerous defects.
<!-- para 4 -->
Using novel data on 2,144 medical device recalls across 50 firms from 2002 to 2015 and recurrent-event accelerated failure time models, we find that greater CEO ownership stakes are associated with slower recalls, and this effect is accentuated for high-severity recalls.
A one-standard-deviation increase—or a 2% increase—in the percentage of shares owned by the CEO is associated with a 26-day delay in recall initiation.
This indicates that firms with CEOs who own greater amounts of stock may, intentionally or unintentionally, allow dangerous medical devices to remain in the market longer.
To explore the mechanisms underlying these recall delays, we employ mixed-effects models that separate the within- and between-effects of CEO stock ownership and use two alternative measures of CEO stock ownership.
We also provide several robustness checks that offer additional support for our findings, including a coarsened exact matching analysis that leverages exogenous variation in CEO stock ownership stemming from a change in the CEO, reverse causality tests that demonstrate that CEO stock ownership is not influenced by prior recalls, and Cox proportional hazard models and generalized linear models as alternative modeling approaches.
<!-- para 5 -->
We then turn to the consequences of recall delays by examining whether the stock market penalizes firms differently depending on how long they take to initiate a recall and whether this penalty varies with recall severity.
Although related studies have explored the influence of recall announcements on stock market penalties in general ([36], [ [7](https://research.ebsco.com/c/p7fgln/viewer/html/stvdyakxez#bib7) ]) and for medical devices in particular ([60]), the findings have been mixed.
Moreover, no studies of which we are aware have examined the stock market implications of _time-to-recall_ , likely because data on when a firm first becomes aware of a defect have not been readily available for analysis.
We find that delaying the initiation of a recall significantly magnifies the stock market penalty experienced by the recalling firm, particularly for devices with severe defects.
For example, waiting an extra three weeks to initiate a recall nearly doubles the stock market penalty, on average—an effect that is most prominent for high-severity recalls and not significant for low-severity recalls.
We include two supplementary event study analyses to build further confidence in our results.
We conclude with a post hoc analysis that demonstrates partial mediation in the relationships we study; that is, CEO stock ownership influences stock market returns following a recall through a longer time-to-recall, particularly for high-severity recalls.
<!-- para 6 -->
Our study provides theoretical contributions for scholars and practical implications for boards of directors and policymakers.
For researchers, we contribute to operations management research examining product recalls by demonstrating how variation in a CEO's stock ownership influences the speed with which recalls are initiated.
In doing so, we demonstrate how CEO-specific factors are associated with a wider array of recall-related phenomena and thereby enrich the nomological network surrounding recalls.
We also contribute to research on the stock market penalties attributable to recalls by highlighting the key role of time-to-recall.
For practice and public policy, our findings point to a need for corporate governance mechanisms that mitigate the recall-slowing effects of CEO stock ownership.
Additionally, we provide insight for the FDA by identifying a previously undiscovered explanation for recall delays that may assist them in identifying recalcitrant firms.
<!-- para 7 -->
We preface our theorizing with an overview of how recall decisions are made in the medical device industry to demonstrate the potential influence that CEO stock ownership may have on how much time elapses before a recall is initiated.
We acknowledge that the precise details of the process by which recalls are decided upon within the firm are relatively obscured from researchers.
To shed some light into this "black box," we interviewed the Global Vice President (VP) of Quality at one of the world's largest medical device companies, the founder of a medical device consulting company with more than 30 years of experience across six medical device firms, and an associate director at the FDA who has held several responsibilities, including analyzing complaints, recalls, and other aspects of quality management specifically for medical devices.
We supplement our interviews with insights from studies that explore medical device recall decisions (e.g., [ [2](https://research.ebsco.com/c/p7fgln/viewer/html/stvdyakxez#bib2) ], [48]).
<!-- para 8 -->
In general, medical device recall decisions follow a three-step decision-making process: problem identification, managerial team recommendation, and executive decision.
For a medical device recall to even be considered, a device must first be identified as problematic.
Such identification comes primarily from reliability engineers who identify potential product defects, although it may also come from patients or physicians ([ [2](https://research.ebsco.com/c/p7fgln/viewer/html/stvdyakxez#bib2) ]).
Reliability engineers monitor product failures in the marketplace and elevate defects that are systemic in nature to a team of managers responsible for recall recommendations.
This team comprises middle-level managers from various functions, including quality, manufacturing, legal, regulatory, reliability, marketing, and research and development.
The team analyzes the failure data to determine whether the failures are random (which provides a potential off-ramp) or systemic (which warrants a recall) and evaluates the number of potentially affected units on the market.
They then make a recommendation to the executive responsible for recall decisions, which is typically the VP of Quality or another executive who reports directly to the CEO.
Although the team's recommendation is taken into consideration, the final decision is usually left to this executive.
In some cases, the CEO may ultimately make the decision.
If the executive decides that a recall is warranted, the firm must notify the FDA and key customers.
The defective medical devices are then removed from the market, replaced, and/or repaired, as appropriate.
<!-- para 9 -->
Although this process is formalized in most medical device firms, there is considerable divergence in how and how long the recall decision-making process actually plays out ([67]).
This is because each recall decision is unique and requires careful consideration.
Recall decisions put firms—and firm leaders—in a precarious position as they try to balance the recall's implications for the firm and its shareholders with the implications for physicians and patients.
For example, recalling too slowly can put patients at prolonged risk and threaten the firm's reputation, whereas recalling too quickly can result in excessive costs or errors, such as recalling the wrong device or needlessly recalling a device without a defect.
Recall decisions thus involve executive autonomy and discretion ([48], [49]), which may open the door for the CEO's personal interests to directly or indirectly play a role.
Indeed, our interviews indicated that there are multiple avenues through which the CEO may influence the recall decision-making process.
The VP of Quality (or executive responsible for recalls) typically meets with the CEO on a recurring basis to discuss potential defects, recalls, and implications for customers and firm finances.
The CEO has a fiduciary duty to, at the very least, be aware of potential recalls under consideration because recalls—particularly severe ones—can impact the firm's finances.
Our interviews indicated that the CEO's role in recall decisions typically goes beyond awareness to involvement—the CEO oversees the decision-making process, sets the tone for how and how quickly the decision is made, and may even make the final decision.
<!-- para 10 -->
Medical device recalls differ in their level of severity, and these levels are established by the FDA based on the harm that may result from a faulty device.
Class I and Class II recalls involve severe defects; they can cause temporary illness, serious injury, or death.
An example of a Class I recall, which can lead to death, is a defective heart rate monitor used in a surgical ward that may inaccurately signal cardiac arrest ([18]).
A leaking catheter tubing system is an example of a Class II recall, which may lead to serious, but temporary, harm ([19]).
In contrast, Class III recalls involve low-severity defects, such as a bone implant with labeling that was printed with offset text ([13]).
They do not pose a health risk, but nonetheless violate FDA regulations ([16]) and are negative events that firms still aim to avoid.
Although CEOs are involved in both types of recall decisions, our interviews indicated that CEOs tend to be more heavily involved in recall decisions for devices with severe defects (i.e., Class I and Class II) than those for devices with nonsevere defects (i.e., Class III), given the implications for firm finances and for public health and safety.
That is, the CEO tends to be more directly involved in high-severity recall decisions, whereas the CEO's influence on low-severity recalls typically stems from the tone and expectations she sets for how recall decisions are made.
Overall, however, the CEO has a meaningful influence on the recall decision-making process, regardless of severity.

## theory
<!-- para 3 -->
Research suggests that CEOs meaningfully influence many firm decisions, ranging from strategic decisions,,such as innovation (Galasso and Simcoe 2011) and acquisitions (Chatterjee and Hambrick 2007), to operational decisions, such as forecasting (Chen et al. 2015) and design, production, and distribution (Wowak et al. 2015), even when decision-making processes are formalized.
With regard to recalls in particular, the CEO possesses more information than external stakeholders about a potential defect well before a recall is initiated.
This information gap opens the door for significant discretion that is subject to limited oversight, which can allow the CEO to influence recall decisions intentionally or unintentionally.
Indeed, scholars have found that certain CEO characteristics and incentives are associated with the number of recalls (Wowak et al. 2015), what recall remedies firms offer (Liu et al. 2016), and the likelihood of a recall (Mayo et al. 2022).
We theorize that the CEO will also influence how quickly a recall is initiated.
Although recalls put CEOs in a precarious position with conflicting motivations, we expect that greater CEO stock ownership will result, on average, in slower recalls
<!-- para 4 -->
Scholars across a variety of fields attribute executives’ tendency to delay bad news—such as the existence of defective products that need to be recalled—to a range of incentives, including reputational and career concerns (Baginski et al. 2018), personal financial interests (Khurana et al. 2017), and hidden cognitive biases (Kahneman and Tversky 1979).
Although some CEOs push for quick disclosure, research suggests that CEOs generally tend to “withhold bad news and gamble that subsequent events will allow them to ‘bury’ the bad news” (Kothari et al. 2009, p. 242) or completely avoid announcing the bad news.
Although it is risky, many executives admit to delaying the disclosure of bad news to analyze the information and develop a recovery strategy or in the hope that the situation will either disappear or improve before disclosure becomes absolutely necessary (Graham et al. 2005).
Building on this body of work, we theorize about three different ways in which a CEO’s ownership stakes may influence time-to-recall.
<!-- para 5 -->
First, although they may not even realize it, CEOs with large stock ownership stakes are likely to have the firm’s financial interests—particularly the stock price— subtly lurking in the back of their minds as they make important decisions (Walters et al. 2008).
This mindset may lead them to intentionally or unintentionally delay bad news as long as possible to protect the firm’s value and reputation.
Sometimes, these delays are simply a consequence of the firm’s due diligence, as the recall team and executives carefully analyze the data and try to pinpoint the reason for the failure.
Other times, these delays are the result of attempted recall avoidance, wherein the CEO is aware that a recall will harm the firm and explores possible off-ramps to avoid the recall (Meier 2005).
For example, a CEO might hope that the passage of time will reveal that a medical device was
<!-- para 6 -->
not actually faulty, but, instead, that problems arose because physicians needed better training on how to use the device, thereby removing the need to recall.
The passage of time may also reveal that initial failures were just random occurrences, so future failures will dissipate and obviate the need to recall.
The exploration of possible off-ramps to avoid a recall and protect the firm’s stock price may result in a longer time-to-recall.
<!-- para 7 -->
Second, CEOs with larger ownership stakes may be viewed as more powerful and less approachable than CEOs with smaller ownership stakes (Combs et al. 2007).
Employees, such as reliability engineers or managers who make recall recommendations to executives, may be fearful to report a defect or make a recommendation to the CEO until absolutely necessary.
Although outside of the medical device industry, the Boeing 737-Max recall offers a helpful example because employee testimony was made public.
When the jet’s design flaws were first discovered, certain reliability engineers were “afraid to speak up out of fear for their jobs” (Gates et al. 2019), which is indicative of a firm culture in which a CEO is perceived as unapproachable to bearers of bad news.
Such intrafirm dynamics may result in recall delays that are unintentional on the CEO’s part but are nonetheless tied to the CEO’s stock ownership stakes within the firm (Combs et al. 2007).
<!-- para 8 -->
Third, although stock ownership can help align the CEO’s personal financial interests with the firm’s interests (Jensen and Murphy 1990), it can also discourage the CEO from announcing bad news to delay or prevent the potential loss in her own personal wealth that will result from the announcement (Kothari et al. 2009).
The announcement of a recall can directly impact the CEO’s personal wealth, so the temptation to delay bad news may grow as the CEO has greater incentives to protect her/his own financial well-being (Baginski et al. 2018).
Overall, whether it is intentional or unintentional, and whether it is due to the firm’s financial interests, the CEO’s power in the firm, or the CEO’s personal financial interests, we posit:
<!-- para 10 -->
_CEO stock ownership is positively associated with the time_ - _to_ - _recall such that the greater the stock ownership of the CEO_ , _the slower the recall._
<!-- para 12 -->
We now examine whether the effect of CEO stock ownership on time-to-recall differs for high-severity and low-severity recalls.
This aligns with Ball et al. (2018b), which treated high severity (i.e., Class I and II) and low severity (i.e., Class III) not on a continuum, but, rather, as different categories of recalls.
It also aligns with our interviews, which indicated that CEOs influence how all recall decisions are made, but they tend to be more heavily involved in high-severity recall decisions than low-severity ones.
We thus examine whether or how the influence of CEO stock ownership on time-to-recall differs for high-severity versus low-severity recalls.
<!-- para 13 -->
High-severity recalls (i.e., Class I and II), by definition, pose significant health risks.
As an example, consider LeMaitre Vascular’s 2013 graft recall.
The grafts were intended to replace sections of damaged or malfunctioning arteries, and LeMaitre Vascular discovered that blood leaked from the surface of the graft after implantation (FDA 2013).
Given the potentially dangerous consequences, the FDA assigned a Class I recall designation.
Class II recalls can also be quite dangerous.
For example, Boston Scientific recalled its implantable cardioverter defibrillator (ICD) because it was at risk for premature battery depletion (FDA 2021a).
A depleted battery can mean serious harm for individuals who depend upon the ICD for life-sustaining therapy.
Such severe recalls are painful for firms because of the financial and reputational ramifications, including increased costs, lost sales, litigation, negative media attention, tarnished credibility, reduced investor confidence, and diminished brand equity (Jarrell and Peltzman 1985, Thirumalai and Sinha 2011, Fuhr et al. 2013, Mukherjee and Sinha 2018, Mukherjee et al. 2021).
<!-- para 14 -->
Although high-severity recalls put CEOs in a precarious position, we expect that the recall-slowing effects of CEO stock ownership will be greater for high-severity recalls, given the significant ramifications for the firm and for public health and safety.
From a behavioral perspective, such preferences are consistent with prospect theory.
Although they may not realize it, individuals are more likely to be drawn to riskier courses of action when hoping to avoid further losses in a high-stakes situation (Kahneman and Tversky 1979).
This is the case when considering high-severity recalls because they immediately yield negative experiences for the recalling firm, the CEO, and shareholders once they are initiated.
Thus, although it is risky, the tendency to delay such negative experiences as long as possible is consistent with loss aversion—that is, the unwitting human tendency to try to avoid losses via actions that an objective observer might view as gambles with low chances of success.
<!-- para 15 -->
In contrast, low-severity recalls (i.e., Class III) are less of a threat because they do not pose any health risk (Ball et al. 2018b).
As an example, consider Teleflex’s 2012 catheter recall.
Teleflex discovered that a label was accidentally omitted from the catheter’s internal packaging (FDA 2012).
The firm could have easily ignored this defect because it is unlikely that physicians inserting the catheter would have noticed the omission.
Lowseverity recalls such as this one typically attract limited attention and thus may less adversely impact the firm and the CEO.
Employees are also less likely to be fearful of reporting such defects to the CEO because of their limited ramifications.
We thus posit:
<!-- para 17 -->
_The recall_ - _slowing effect of CEO stock ownership is stronger for high_ - _severity recalls than for low_ - _severity recalls._
<!-- para 19 -->
Our theorizing above indicates unfavorable outcomes for public health, as delayed recalls of defective medical devices can harm the individuals who rely on them.
However, it is not clear how such delays may impact the firm and, by association, its CEO.
We now examine a less obvious but possible consequence of slow recalls: stock market penalties.
The underlying logic is that the market may recognize a delayed recall’s potential impact on the firm in terms of costs, future revenues, credibility, and reputation, and the stock price is adjusted downward accordingly.
Although one study of medical device recalls suggests that recall risks may already be “baked into” the stock price, thereby negating a market reaction (Thirumalai and Sinha 2011), it did not explore the implications of time-to-recall for stock market returns.
<!-- para 20 -->
As time-to-recall increases, defective medical devices remain on the market longer, subjecting patients to extended risks.
Delays in recall initiation also increase the likelihood that recovering public trust and goodwill after the recall will be more costly and difficult (Hora et al. 2011).
It is also more likely that physicians or patients will notice the defect and develop a negative impression of the product and firm the longer the firm takes to recall.
Reputationally, delays in recall initiation will likely garner additional media attention, as delays signal to investors that the firm may not prioritize public health and safety.
We thus expect larger stock market penalties to arise as the time-to-recall increases.
Stated formally:
<!-- para 22 -->
_Time_ - _to_ - _recall is positively associated with stock market penalties such that the longer the time_ - _to_ - _recall_ , _the larger the stock market penalty._
<!-- para 24 -->
Building on the idea that high-severity and low-severity recalls are distinct categories and thus differ in their ramifications, we theorize that the effect of time-to-recall on stock market penalties will differ between high-severity and low-severity recalls.
Anecdotally, high-severity recalls come with substantial costs.
For example, industry estimates suggest that one high-severity recall can cost a medical device firm as much as $600 million ([26]), and this estimate does not include the expensive litigation that usually follows.
It is reasonable to presume that such costs will only increase the longer the firm takes to initiate a recall.
When a recall is delayed, the stock market is more likely to recognize the extent of the resulting damage to the recalling firm's value and thus adjust the firm's stock price downward.
In contrast, low-severity recalls have more limited ramifications that do not grow as dramatically as the time-to-recall increases.
For example, consider Allergan's 2019 breast implant recall.
Allergan initiated a recall after discovering that the barcodes on its implants and sizers were unreadable by some scanners—a defect that was deemed harmless, but nonetheless violated FDA regulations ([20]).
Such recalls are unlikely to garner as significant of a reaction from the stock market, even if they are delayed, because they have a less dramatic impact on the firm's bottom line.
We thus posit:
<!-- para 26 -->
_The stock market penalty associated with time_ - _to_ - _recall is larger for high_ - _severity recalls than for low_ - _severity recalls._
<!-- para 28 -->
We leveraged six data sources to test our hypotheses.
First, we obtained data on medical device recalls via a Freedom of Information Act (FOIA) request to the FDA.
The data set provided by the FDA included the date on which a firm became aware of a defect, the date on which the recall was initiated, the firm's name, the number of affected units in distribution, and the recall severity classification (i.e., Class I, II, or III).
We obtained additional recall-level data from the FDA's Medical Device Recall database and firm-level financial data from the COMPUSTAT Fundamentals Annual, COMPUSTAT Execucomp Annual Compensation, Wharton Research Data Services (WRDS) Thomson Reuters 13-F Stock Ownership, and Institutional Shareholders Services databases.
Data for the event study analysis were obtained from the Center for Research in Security Prices (CRSP) database and from the Kenneth R.
French security data library.\[ [^9] \] The intersection of these data sets resulted in a sample of 2,144 recalls initiated by 50 publicly traded medical device firms across 14 years (2002–2015 inclusive).
<!-- para 30 -->
We measured *CEO Ownership* as the proportion of a firm's outstanding stock held by the firm's CEO.
For each firm *i* in year *t* , we calculated *CEO Ownership* as the percentage of shares outstanding held by the CEO, as reported in the Execucomp database.
We centered and standardized the measure to enhance coefficient interpretability.
<!-- para 31 -->
We used *CEO Ownership* as our primary measure because it is a broad, comprehensive measure that reflects the three related, but distinct, mechanisms we theorized about in Hypothesis 1—firm financial interests, CEO power, and CEO financial interests.
First, firms use equity incentives to help align the CEO's interests with the firm's interests (\[66\]), and research indicates that direct stock ownership is one of the most effective tools to do so (\[37\]).
Second, research suggests that a CEO's stock holdings are an indicator of power, so a CEO with large stock ownership stakes may be viewed as more powerful within the firm (\[25\]).
Third, a CEO's stock ownership stakes directly impact her personal wealth (\[55\]).
Overall, prior studies conclude that the percentage of the firm's outstanding stock that the CEO owns is key to understanding the incentive consequences of stock ownership (\[37\]), which is why we use it as our primary measure, although we examine alternative measures in supplementary analyses.
<!-- para 33 -->
We measured *Time-to-Recall* —the dependent variable for Hypotheses 1 and 2—as the number of days from defect awareness to recall initiation (\[67\]).
When a firm decides to initiate a recall, the FDA requires the firm to provide the defect awareness date—that is, "the date and circumstances under which the product deficiency or possible deficiency was discovered" (\[23\]).
The defect awareness dates are verified by the FDA during plant inspections (\[24\]), and firms are subject to penalties for misreporting information in recall reports.

We used four different time windows surrounding the recall announcement.
All four windows use one day prior to the recall announcement date for the beginning of the window, but vary with respect to the end of the window.
Specifically, we examined windows up to 10 days after the recall to explore support for Hypotheses 3 and 4—that is, *CAR(−1,0)* , *CAR(−1,1)* , *CAR(−1,5)* , and *CAR(−1,10)* .
<!-- para 44 -->
We measured *Recall Severity* using the FDA's recall classifications.
For each recall, the FDA assigns a severity classification based on the health hazard of the product being recalled (\[16\]).
Class I and II recalls involve severe defects that can cause temporary illness, injury, or death, whereas Class III recalls involve defects that do not pose health risks.
Consistent with FDA definitions and extant research (\[ [^11] \]), we categorized Class I and II recalls as high severity and Class III recalls as low severity.
In line with our theorizing and with research precedent, we split the sample into high-severity and low-severity recalls and analyzed the two categories separately (\[ [^11] \]).
<!-- para 46 -->
We controlled for several important recall-related characteristics.
First, a firm's recall decision may be influenced by previous recalls. *Recall Experience* was measured as the natural logarithm of the count of recalls that firm *i* initiated in the previous year (\[ [^11] \]).\[ [^10] \] We also controlled for the number of units recalled (\[60\]).
This is particularly important to control for in our study because the stock market's reaction to a longer time-to-recall could simply be an artifact of more units being recalled, rather than the speed at which the firm initiated the recall.\[ [^11] \] *Units Recalled* was measured as the natural logarithm of the number of units recalled provided in our FDA data set.
We also controlled for heterogeneity across medical devices by including *Medical Device Class* , which is an ordinal variable that corresponds to the class of the medical device, as designated in the FDA's Medical Device Recall database.
Class 1 medical devices, such as bandages, have the lowest risk level, whereas Class 3 medical devices, such as pacemakers, have the highest risk level.
We accounted for the relatedness of recall events within and across firms with common risk exposures by including *Recall Cause* as a control variable.
It is a nominal variable that represents the FDA-determined cause for each recall—design, labeling, manufacturing process, packaging, software, or other—as listed in the FDA's Medical Device Recall database.
<!-- para 47 -->
We also controlled for firm-level financial variables shown to influence recalls and stock ownership.
Following previous research (\[60\]), we controlled for firm *Sales* using the natural logarithm of total sales.
We also controlled for firm performance using *Return on Equity,* which is calculated as the natural logarithm of the ratio of net income to equity.
We controlled for *Cash Holding Ratio* (i.e., the ratio of cash holdings to total assets) and *Debt Ratio* (i.e., the ratio of total debt to total assets) because firms with high debt and/or large cash holdings face differential pressures from stockholders (\[58\]).
In terms of CEO characteristics, we included *CEO Age* —the age of the CEO in year *t* as reported in the annual proxy statement—and *CEO Tenure* —the number of years elapsed between year *t* and the year the individual became CEO (\[66\], \[45\]).
Moving to the board of directors, we controlled for three characteristics shown to influence executive decision making: *Female Board Representation* —the percentage of female directors (\[67\]); *Board Independence—* the percentage of directors not employed by the firm (\[51\]); and *CEO-Chair Separation* —a dichotomous variable equal to one if the CEO is not the board chair and zero if the CEO chairs the board (\[66\]).
<!-- para 48 -->
We controlled for *Institutional Investor Ownership* using the Herfindahl-Hirschman Index (HHI) available through the WRDS Thomson Reuters 13-F Stock Ownership database.
This measure is the sum of the squared ownership percentage of each institutional investor's ownership stakes in each firm *i* in each year *t* .
As a concentration ratio, the measure captures the extent to which a firm's stock ownership is clustered among a small number of institutional investors (\[58\]).
Although we expect executives to be eager to please stockholders, they will likely be even more responsive to the extent that institutional investor ownership is concentrated—that is, a small number of institutional investors hold a large percentage of the firm's shares (\[32\]).
We also controlled for *Activist Investor Ownership* as the percentage of outstanding shares owned by investors who have targeted firms in FDA-regulated industries.
Activist investors are a powerful subset of institutional investors whose stated intent is to bring about material change in the operations and outcomes of firms whose stock they own (\[58\]).
In line with \[58\], we identified activists as all institutional investors who have filed a Schedule 13D (or 13D/A) related to concerns, dispute, or control at any firm in industries regulated by the FDA.
Lastly, we included firm and year fixed effects in all models to account for time-invariant omitted variable bias that may exist within firms and across years.

We tested Hypotheses 1 and 2 using accelerated failure time (AFT) models with a Weibull distribution for the underlying failure rate.
AFT models are frequently used to examine how predictor variables that change across time influence the time to an event of interest (\[ [^12] \], \[31\])—in our case, *Time-to-Recall* .
We used a Weibull distribution because it is one of the more commonly used and parsimonious distributional forms for the underlying failure rate (\[ [^14] \]).
Firms in our data set experience multiple recalls, so we used recurrent-event AFT models with robust standard errors (\[ [^14] \], \[31\]).
The AFT estimation follows the generalized equation:
<!-- para 55 -->
$$ \mathrm{Log}(t_{i}) = \beta_{0} + \beta X_{i} + u_{i} $$
<!-- para 56 -->
where ti is the elapsed time-to-recall in days for firm i, β0 is an intercept term, b is a vector of regression coefficients, Xi is a vector of covariates, and ui is an error term with a Weibull distribution.
Cox proportional hazard (CPH) models are also commonly used to examine the hazard of a failure and benefit from requiring no specification of the underlying distribution (Box-Steffensmeier and Jones 2004).
In robustness checks, we present consistent support for Hypotheses 1 and 2 using CPH models as well as generalized linear regression models
<!-- para 57 -->
To test Hypotheses 3 and 4, we used generalized linear models (GLMs) with *CAR* as the dependent variable.
We used GLMs rather than ordinary least squares (OLS) because GLMs are robust to nonconstant error variance and assumptions related to error distribution (\[59\]).
We included the same set of control variables as our recurrent-event AFT models as well as the natural logarithm of *Time-to-Recall* .\[ [^12] \]

Before examining Hypotheses 3 and 4, we examined a key component of the event study—the stock market's reaction to the recall event itself, independent of time-to-recall.
A related study to ours, \[60\], does not find a significant stock market reaction to medical device recalls.
However, our data set includes a significantly larger number of recalls over a much longer time period (223 recalls across three years in \[60\], compared with 2,144 recalls across 14 years in our study).
Thus, it is possible that we may find a significant reaction to the recall event—independent of time-to-recall—because we have a larger sample with more variation in recalls, firms, and years.
Table A2 in the online appendix provides the daily abnormal returns surrounding the recall event, and Table 3 provides the mean CARs, Patell *t* -tests, median CARs, Wilcon Signed Rank tests, percent of positive CARs, and sign tests to assess the significance of the CARs (\[49\]).
Because CARs are known to be skewed, we focus on the results of the nonparametric test results in columns ( [^14] ) and ( [^16] ).
In contrast to \[60\] findings, the CARs are significantly different from zero for all recalls and high-severity recalls across event windows (−1,0), (−1,1), and (−1,5), but not (−1,10).
This suggests that the stock market responds significantly negatively to medical device recall announcements, especially those that are most severe, but the effect dissipates as the number of days following recall initiation increases.

Next, we examined Hypotheses 3 and 4 by incorporating time-to-recall as a predictor of stock market returns.
Table 4 presents GLM results across the four event windows described in Section 4.2.
A positive beta coefficient indicates a stock price increase, whereas a negative coefficient indicates a stock price decrease.
Thus, a negative and significant coefficient on time-to-recall provides support for Hypothesis 3.
Columns ( [^9] ), ( [^12] ), ( [^15] ), and (10) report the results for all recalls across varying time windows.
The coefficient on *Time-to-Recall* is negative and significant for *CAR(* − *1,0)* , *CAR(* − *1,1)* , and *CAR(* − *1,5)* but is not significant for *CAR(* − *1,10)* , which indicates that the effect of time-to-recall appears to dissipate as the number of days following recall initiation increases.
We interpret the practical implications of this finding using the smallest ( *CAR(* − *1,0)* ) and largest ( *CAR(* − *1,5)* ) significant effect sizes to provide a range of the potential penalty associated with recall delays.
A one-standard-deviation increase in time-to-recall is associated with a stock market penalty ranging from −1.82% to −4.46%.\[ [^14] \] To further understand the practical implications, we examined how these market penalties change across quartiles of the *Time-to-Recall* measure for the smallest and largest significant effect sizes.
The range of penalties is presented in Table 5, which illustrates meaningful increases in the stock market penalty across quartiles.
For example, Table 5 indicates that moving from the first quartile (10 days) to the second quartile (33 days)—an approximately three-week delay in time-to-recall—is associated with an increase in the stock market penalty ranging from 82% to 124%.
In sum, the stock market responds negatively to a longer time-to-recall, lending support to Hypothesis 3.

We conducted three additional analyses to probe the mechanisms underlying the relationship between CEO stock ownership and recall timing. **First, we used mixed-effects models to explore the within-firm and between-firm effects of CEO stock ownership, and the results are reported in Table 7.\[ [^15] \] Model ( [^10] ) indicates that the within-component of *CEO Ownership* has a positive and significant relationship with time-to recall (β = 0.14, *p* < 0.001), whereas the between-component is not statistically significant.
Likewise, Model ( [^11] ) indicates that the within-component *CEO Ownership* has a positive and significant relationship with time-to recall for high-severity recalls (β = 0.16, *p* < 0.001), whereas the between-component again does not have a statistically significant effect.
The results suggest that the effect of CEO stock ownership is driven by the within-component rather than the between-component.
That is, it is not the difference in CEO stock ownership between firms, but, rather, a relative increase in stock ownership for a given CEO within the same firm that explains recall delays.
Interestingly, Model ( [^12] ) indicates that the between-component of *CEO Ownership* is negative and significant for low-severity recalls (β = −0.58, *p* < 0.05).
This result may indicate an openness to quickly recall products with limited repercussions, although the small sample size caveats this result.
Perhaps firms whose CEOs have large ownership stakes view low-severity recalls as a chance to demonstrate their concern for public safety without harming the firm or their personal wealth.**

Following extant research (\[37\]), we used *CEO Ownership* as our primary measure because it is a broad, comprehensive measure that reflects the three mechanisms we theorized about in Hypothesis 1: firm financial interests, CEO power, and CEO financial interests.
To probe these three mechanisms at a more granular level, we replicated our recurrent-event AFT analyses using two alternative measures of CEO stock ownership—specifically, *CEO Equity-Based Compensation* and *CEO Ownership (Monetary).* We measured *CEO Equity-Based Compensation* as the proportion of the CEO's total compensation comprising stock awards and options based on the grant date fair value, as reported by the firm following the Financial Accounting Standards Board's FAS 123R standard.
We measured *CEO Ownership (Monetary)* as the monetary value of the percentage of shares outstanding held by the CEO based on the firm's market capitalization.
Figure 1 details each measure and its alignment with our theorized mechanisms based on extant research.
As it illustrates, both our primary measure and the two alternative measures inherently reflect the CEO's own financial interests. *CEO Equity-Based Compensation* also proxies for the firm's interests because firms use equity incentives to help align the CEO's interests with the firm's interests (\[66\], \[43\]), whereas *CEO Ownership (Monetary)* also proxies for the CEO's power within the firm because the dollar value of a CEO's stock holdings may reflect the CEO's power (\[25\], \[30\]).
Thus, although our primary measure is comprehensive and reflects all three mechanisms, the alternative measures help us to examine whether, indeed, all three mechanisms contribute to recall delays.

<!-- para 38 -->
Our finding that greater CEO stock ownership is associated with a longer time-to-recall may be subject to endogeneity.
It is possible that CEO stock ownership may be influenced by past recalls or by ongoing quality problems in which the CEO is aware of the defect, but the recall has not yet been initiated (although this may qualify as illegal insider trading).
We address this endogeneity concern by exploiting an exogenous shock in our data—a change in a firm's CEO.
A change in the CEO is an exogenous shock to the amount of stock owned by a CEO, contingent upon one key criterion: Past recalls should not predict the likelihood of the previous CEO's departure.
This ensures that the exogenous change in stock ownership created by a change in the CEO is not explained by past recalls, which would only propagate the risk of endogeneity.\[ [^16] \] However, if greater *CEO Ownership* continues to predict a longer time-to-recall, even for this exogenously generated category, then we contend that endogeneity concerns related to the relationship between CEO stock ownership and recalls are significantly reduced.
<!-- para 39 -->
To test this, we first used coarsened exact matching (CEM)-weighted regression analysis (\[35\]).
CEM uses pretreatment matching variables to generate strata in which treatment and control observations are matched.
This matching process generates CEM weights that capture the level of similarity within each stratum between the treatment and control observations.
Using CEM weights in a regression is parallel to estimating treatment effects for each stratum and averaging those treatment effects across treated observations.
In our study, the treatment group consists of recalls in which a CEO was new, whereas the control group consists of recalls in which a CEO was not new.
To use this treatment, we first needed to identify recalls in which there was a change in the CEO.
We assign the variable *CEO Flip* as a one for all recalls in which the CEO is different than the CEO for the previous recall at the same firm and zero otherwise.
Importantly, we only assigned this measure as a one if the CEO was in the role *prior* to a given recall's defect awareness date, not the recall initiation date.
This distinction ensures that our treatment group only captures recalls in which the incoming CEO was in the role for the entire time-to-recall period, not recall observations in which the new CEO inherited recall decisions from the prior CEO. *CEO Flip* returned to zero when there was a subsequent change in the new CEO's shares, typically occurring one year after the CEO's start date as part of an annual compensation change.
<!-- para 40 -->
We matched each observation in the treatment group to those in the control group based on three pretreatment variables— *Sales* , *CEO Tenure* , and *CEO Age* —that address firm and CEO characteristics that may influence the likelihood of a change in the CEO (\[50\], \[34\]).
This process yielded 22 matched strata containing 150 treated observations and 987 control observations for a total of 1,137 observations across 35 firms from 2004 to 2015; 1,007 observations were not matched in any stratum and thus were excluded from the analysis.
The results were robust to using various combinations of our control variables as pretreatment matching variables.
The CEM-weighted recurrent-event AFT model results presented in Table A4 in the online appendix suggest that greater CEO stock ownership is associated with a longer time-to-recall for all recalls and for high-severity recalls, but not for low-severity recalls, although the small sample size precludes us from confidently drawing strong conclusions about low-severity recalls.
Overall, however, we find similar results when compared with Table 2: Greater CEO stock ownership is associated with a longer time-to-recall, particularly for high-severity recalls—even when the change in CEO stock ownership is exogenously driven.
<!-- para 41 -->
However, we still need to demonstrate that a change in the CEO is not explained by past recalls.
Table A5 in the online appendix presents the logistic regression results, with *CEO Flip* as the dependent variable.
We observe that the number of recalls a firm initiated in the past year and the firm's average time-to-recall do not predict the likelihood of a change in the CEO.\[ [^17] \] This helps bolster the validity of our CEM analysis and build confidence in the exogeneity of CEO ownership changes attributable to a new CEO.
We also used panelized fixed-effects models to examine whether the firm's past recall counts and recall timing influence the CEO's ownership stakes by using the number of recalls a firm initiated in the past year as well as the firm's average time-to-recall in the past year to predict CEO stock ownership (whereas we predicted *CEO Flip* in Table A5 in the online appendix).
We present the results in Table A6 in the online appendix.
The results indicate that the number of recalls a firm initiated and a firm's average time-to-recall over the past year do not predict CEO ownership.
These results were also robust to the inclusion of a cumulative measure of *Recall Experience* , which helps to further alleviate endogeneity risks related to CEO stock ownership and the firm's overall reputation of quality.
<!-- para 43 -->
We next examine whether our results related to Hypotheses 1 and 2 might be associated with our chosen econometric model.
In our primary analysis, we used recurrent-event AFT models because they are specifically designed to examine how time to an event—in our case, time-to-recall—is influenced by time-varying predictors (\[ [^14] \]).
However, one challenge associated with AFT models is specifying the underlying failure rate.
It is possible that our choice of a Weibull distribution may influence our results, so we examined the robustness of our results using CPH models.
CPH models address somewhat different questions than those of our study—the beta coefficients in a CPH model indicate the instantaneous hazard rate of an event occurring at any point in time based on time-varying predictors.
Given that our theorizing focuses on the *time-to-recall* , not the *hazard* of a recall, we used recurrent-event AFT models as our primary modeling approach.
Out of an abundance of caution, we replicated our analysis using CPH models.\[10\] The results presented in Table A7 in the online appendix are consistent with our primary results presented in Table 2.
A negative coefficient in a CPH model is analogous to a positive coefficient in an AFT model.
That is, a lower hazard of an event (CPH model) is consistent with a longer time to the event (AFT model).
The negative and significant coefficient in Table A7 thus supports our theorizing that greater CEO ownership stakes are associated with a lower hazard of a recall—that is, a longer time-to-recall, especially for high-severity recalls.
<!-- para 44 -->
Although survival analysis models such as AFT and CPH may be preferred to linear regression when there are time-varying covariates (\[ [^12] \]), as is the case in our study, generalized linear models can also be used to examine the time to failure for duration data.
Thus, to enhance confidence that our modeling approach does not substantively influence our conclusions, we replicated our survival analysis models using generalized linear models and the logarithmically transformed time-to-recall as the dependent variable.
The results presented in Table A8 in the online appendix are consistent with our primary results presented in Table 2.
Specifically, CEO stock ownership continues to have a positive, significant effect on time-to-recall, especially for high-severity recalls.
<!-- para 46 -->
Lastly, we examine potential endogeneity and modeling concerns surrounding our event study.
One advantage of an event study is that it is premised on event exogeneity.
In our case, a recall announcement is an exogenous shock to the stock market, so our event study comparing expected market returns against actual market returns can generally presume upon exogeneity.
However, it is possible that a recall announcement might not be exogenous if the public is aware of issues related to the defect before the recall is announced.
For example, it could be the case that patients or physicians encountered issues with a medical device and were aware of potential defects before a recall was initiated.
It could also be the case that someone inside the firm advertently or inadvertently revealed issues related to the defect to the public before the recall was initiated.
To examine this concern, we leveraged the richness of our recall data to conduct an additional event study that uses the defect awareness date in place of the recall-initiation date as the date of the event.
Table A9 in the online appendix provides the mean CARs, Patell *t* -tests, median CARs, Wilcon Signed Rank tests, the percent of recalls with positive CARs, and sign tests to assess the significance of the CARs.
The results indicate that the CARs are not statistically significantly different from zero in any time window for any severity level.
This suggests that there is no significant response from the stock market surrounding the defect awareness date, which helps to build additional confidence in the validity of our event study findings and the exogeneity of the recall initiation date.
<!-- para 47 -->
Our second event date robustness check is related to the recall announcement.
We used the recall announcement date as the event—or "Day 0"—in our event study analysis because a firm must immediately notify the FDA and potentially affected parties when it initiates a recall.
However, this announcement is date-stamped but not time-stamped.
Although many recalls are announced before the end of the trading day, it is possible that the recall could be publicly announced after the end of the trading day or on a weekend.
We thus replicated our event study analysis using the subsequent trading day following a recall announcement as the event date.
The results are reported in Tables A10 and A11 in the online appendix and are in line with our primary results presented in Tables 3 and 4.
These results help to build additional confidence in the robustness of our event study findings.
<!-- para 49 -->
Our post hoc analysis addresses implied relationships in our theorizing and results—CEO ownership may influence stock market returns through time-to-recall.
To examine this possibility, we used an explicit mediation approach that explores evidence of indirect effects (\[53\]).
The explicit mediation method simulates multiple draws of indirect effects that are the product of two coefficients—the coefficient for the relationship between the independent variable and the mediator and the coefficient for the relationship between the mediator and the dependent variable.
Evidence of mediation is identified by examining the 95% confidence interval for the mediation pathway.
If the interval does not contain zero, mediation is supported.
To conduct this analysis, we used a Markov Chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) simulation method with 20,000 draws (\[44\], \[52\], \[62\]).\[11\] Table A12 in the online appendix presents the results across event windows.
The results indicate that time-to-recall partially mediates the relationship between CEO stock ownership and stock market returns for event windows *CAR(* − *1,0)* , *CAR(* − *1,1)* , and *CAR(* − *1,5)* for all recalls.
Overall, as CEO stock ownership increases, time-to-recall increases, and this delay leads to greater stock market penalties.
<!-- para 50 -->
Our theorizing also implies that recall severity influences the indirect effect of CEO ownership on stock market returns through time-to-recall in two ways—the recall-slowing effect of CEO ownership is larger for high-severity recalls, and the stock market penalty associated with a longer time-to-recall is larger for high-severity recalls.
The results reported in Table A12, columns ( [^12] ) and ( [^14] ), in the online appendix indicate that the indirect effect holds for high-severity recalls across event windows *CAR(* − *1,0)* , *CAR(* − *1,1)* , and *CAR(* − *1,5)* , but not for low-severity recalls.
That is, as CEO stock ownership increases, time-to-recall increases, and this delay leads to greater stock market penalties—particularly for high-severity recalls.
To further probe the influence of recall severity, we also conducted a moderated mediation analysis that used the full sample of recalls and included the interactions of *Recall Severity* × *CEO Ownership* and *Recall Severity* × *Time-to-Recall.* Specifically, we ran a moderated mediation analysis\[12\] that jointly examined how recall severity influences the direct effect of CEO stock ownership on time-to-recall and the indirect effect of CEO stock ownership on stock market penalties through time-to-recall (\[65\]).
Table A13 in the online appendix presents the results, which are consistent with Table A12 in the online appendix and indicate that time-to-recall partially mediates the relationship between CEO stock ownership and stock market returns for event windows *CAR(* − *1,0)* , *CAR(* − *1,1)* , and *CAR(* − *1,5)* , and this indirect effect is significantly moderated by recall severity.

## discussion
<!-- para 2 -->
Ideally, the speed with which a faulty medical device is recalled should be driven predominantly by the defective product's potential to harm consumers that rely on these devices for their health and safety.
However, our analyses reveal that CEO stock ownership may be, at least in part, a reason why defective medical devices stay on the market longer than perhaps desired to the detriment of patients, the firm, stockholders, and, ironically, the CEO.
Our study thus offers three theoretical contributions and three implications for practice and public policy.
<!-- para 3 -->
Beginning with theoretical contributions, our study complements previous research examining the CEO's influence on recall-related outcomes, such as the number of recalls (\[66\]), recall remedies (\[43\]), and recall hazard (\[45\]).
For example, our findings complement the findings of \[66\] that CEOs incentivized by stock options make riskier decisions, which result in more recalls and greater recall likelihood.
When considered alongside our findings, it appears that recalls are more likely, more frequent, and initiated more slowly in firms who incentivize CEOs with stocks and options.
Likewise, our results complement the finding of \[45\] that longer-tenured CEOs tend to avoid initiating recalls when possible.
In combination, these findings suggest that recalls are less frequent and initiated more slowly as CEO tenure increases (\[45\]), which highlights another important CEO characteristic in the recall domain.
Although previous studies offer important insights into outcomes such as the number of recalls or recall likelihood, a recall can have very different implications for the firm and, importantly, society, depending on how long the firm takes to initiate it.
We thus contribute by expanding understanding of the CEO's influence on recall-related phenomena to include time-to-recall.
This is an important contribution because delays in recall initiation put public safety at heightened risk, but the factors contributing to these delays are not well-understood.
<!-- para 4 -->
In doing so, we enhance what is currently known about recall decision-making speed (\[67\]) and executives' tendency to delay bad news (\[40\], \[ [^9] \]).
We find that, similar to the tendency to delay negative earnings forecasts, firms whose CEOs are incentivized with greater stock ownership tend to delay the initiation of medical device recalls.
This finding is persistent across multiple measures of stock ownership—that is, increasing the CEO's stock ownership stakes, the stock-based proportion of the CEO's compensation, and the dollar value of the CEO's stock ownership stakes lead to a longer time-to-recall.
A one-standard-deviation increase—or a 2% increase—in the percentage of shares owned by the CEO is associated with defective medical devices remaining in the market for an additional 26 days.
Such delays are associated with consequences that may be detrimental to public health and safety and, ironically, the firm's interests and the CEO's own interests.
Although such recall-slowing effects are in line with prospect theory's predictions of risk-seeking behavior when losses loom large (\[39\]), this relationship was previously undiscovered in the domain of recall decision making.
Most concerning is that the recall-slowing effects of CEO stock ownership are strongest for high-severity recalls, which reveals that the recall delays associated with CEO stock ownership—whether intentional or unintentional—may increase public health risks.
<!-- para 5 -->
Our findings suggest that *recall timing* may be an important facet of understanding how recalls impact shareholder value.
We thus contribute important nuance to interdisciplinary scholarship examining the stock market implications of recalls (\[36\], \[61\]), which, to date, has provided mixed evidence.
Whereas \[60\] found no significant stock market reaction to medical device recalls, our study indicates that the stock market may, in fact, notice and respond negatively to medical device recalls.
The negative and significant market value loss following a medical device recall is attributable to both the nature of the recall (i.e., recall severity) and the CEO's stock ownership.
Specifically, we find that recall severity directly influences the market reaction to a recall and accentuates the market reaction to a longer time-to-recall.
However, the influence of CEO stock ownership on the market reaction to a recall is more subtle, acting only indirectly through a longer time-to-recall.
<!-- para 6 -->
Given the significant societal costs associated with failing to quickly recall faulty medical devices, we extend our theoretical contributions into three suggestions for practice and public policy.
First, the FDA has called for a recall process that is as swift as possible (\[17\]), and our findings offer timely insights for the FDA in achieving this aim.
In particular, our findings reveal an ownership characteristic of firms that are more likely to have slower recalls: high CEO ownership stakes.
Given that CEO compensation information is readily available for public firms, a key implication for the FDA is to consider using this information to identify firms that may need additional oversight in initiating recalls of medical devices with dangerous defects, such as additional plant inspections or more stringent new product submission regulatory reviews to ensure product quality.
<!-- para 7 -->
Second, for boards of directors, our findings indicate a potential need for closer scrutiny of recall decision making, especially of executives with high ownership stakes.
Interestingly, many medical device firms in our sample have established permanent product quality decision-making committees intended to scrutinize recall decisions (e.g., \[ [^13] \] and \[46\]).
However, it is unlikely that these board committees are cognizant of the potential unintended consequences of greater CEO stock ownership revealed by our study.
Although there are many benefits of CEO stock ownership, such as the alignment of incentives (\[55\]) and feedback effects (\[37\]), it may also create ethical conundrums.
Recalling too slowly may harm patients and firm value, whereas recalling too quickly may help firm value but may also lead to excess costs, unnecessary recalls, and resultant trauma for affected patients.
Our study thus offers additional criteria to help board committees carefully determine the level and form of CEO compensation, while simultaneously overseeing product quality decision making by executives.
<!-- para 8 -->
Finally, for CEOs, our study offers empirical evidence of the stock market penalties associated with delaying recalls.
The stock market can only act as a deterrent to questionable or dubious practices if actors have knowledge of how their actions impact stock prices (\[ [^15] \]).
Until now, the stock market consequences of medical device recalls have been underappreciated, in part because of the lack of awareness of how recall timing may influence the penalty attributable to a recall.
Our study can arm CEOs with this new knowledge and hopefully encourage quicker initiation of medical device recalls.
Doing so could result in a win-win for stockholders *and* public health and safety.
<!-- para 10 -->
Our focus on the medical device industry allowed us to provide empirical insights into why faulty medical devices sometimes take so long to be recalled—a question that has plagued researchers, patients, physicians, and regulators.
However, a limitation of our study is generalizability to other industries, such as food, cosmetics, pharmaceuticals, and automobiles.
Although our study takes an important step forward, more work is needed to examine whether and how the relationships we uncovered hold in other industries as well as for other operations decisions more broadly.
For example, future research could explore whether CEO stock ownership influences how firms manage other types of operational failures (\[63\]).
<!-- para 11 -->
Although our findings should be balanced against the benefits of incentivizing CEOs with greater stock ownership (\[37\]), they also demonstrate that CEO stock ownership is linked to recall delays.
However, one limitation of our study is that it leaves unaddressed whether recall delays are intentional or unintentional responses to stock ownership.
For example, is the recall-slowing effect simply a byproduct of greater ownership stakes, or do CEOs implicitly encourage employees to make recall decisions more slowly?
Although we conducted interviews and additional analyses to probe the underlying mechanisms, we could not examine the recall decision-making process directly or identify the underlying mechanisms with certainty.
Our use of secondary data precludes us from fully opening the black box of recall decision making.
Future research could use case studies and behavioral experiments to build upon our study and explore the black box of recall decisions more completely.
<!-- para 12 -->
Although our unique data set sheds important light on time-to-recall, the defect awareness dates used to calculate our time-to-recall measure are self-reported by firms.
It is possible that firms report dates that are later than the actual defect awareness date.
However, our industry interviews and regulatory guidance from the FDA indicate that defect awareness dates are subject to external verification by the FDA during recurring plant inspections, and firms face serious penalties for misreporting.
Nonetheless, we cannot completely rule out the possibility of self-reporting bias.
Moreover, firms may have different approaches to recall decisions.
Although our inclusion of firm fixed effects is beneficial in this regard, it may not account for all variation in recall decision making.
Finally, although our numerous robustness checks and control variables help to alleviate endogeneity concerns, they are nonetheless a limitation of our research.
