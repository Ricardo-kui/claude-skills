# ceo_tenure_and_recall_risk_management_in_the_consumer_produc 句子库存

## introduction
<!-- para 2 -->
From the perspective of the C-suite, “recall” is a dreaded word.
Rarely do good things follow when product recalls are under consideration.
In addition to the faulty products that may necessitate a recall, there are numerous negative implications that may ensue from a recall initiation.
These include damage to the firm's financial performance (Chen et al. [2009](#poms13576-bib-0027), Pruitt and Peterson [1986](#poms13576-bib-0097), Thirumalai and Sinha [2011](#poms13576-bib-0111)), market reputation (Isidore [2014](#poms13576-bib-0065), Tabuchi [2010](#poms13576-bib-0110)), and even the CEO's career (Liu et al. [2016](#poms13576-bib-0079)).
Direct recall costs include repairing or replacing defective inventory, which can reach millions, if not hundreds of millions of dollars (Jarrell and Peltzman [1985](#poms13576-bib-0068), Shah et al. [2017](#poms13576-bib-0102)).
No less significant are indirect recall costs, which include negative stock market reactions (Davidson and Worrell [1992](#poms13576-bib-0041), Thirumalai and Sinha [2011](#poms13576-bib-0111)), legal actions (Daughety and Reinganum [1995](#poms13576-bib-0039)), and the cost consequences of recall-related negative media coverage (Liu and Shankar [2015](#poms13576-bib-0080), Rhee and Haunschild [2006](#poms13576-bib-0100)).
While there are potential upsides of a recall, such as firm learning (Haunschild and Rhee [2004](#poms13576-bib-0058)) and goodwill with customers and regulators (Ahluwalia et al. [2000](#poms13576-bib-0002), Dawar and Pillutla [2000](#poms13576-bib-0042)), firm leaders, and particularly CEOs, may nonetheless seek to avoid recalls when possible, or at least avoid the blame for them.
Exploring this possibility is the focus of our study.
<!-- para 3 -->
In fact, research outside of operations management (OM) indicates that CEOs may manage risks related to negative events via at least two mechanisms: avoiding the negative event altogether (Baginski et al. [2018](#poms13576-bib-0007), Davidson et al. [2004](#poms13576-bib-0040), Kothari et al. [2009](#poms13576-bib-0072), Skinner [1994](#poms13576-bib-0104)) or avoiding the blame for the negative event (Adams and Ferreira [2007](#poms13576-bib-0001), Baginski et al. [2018](#poms13576-bib-0007), Bornemann et al. [2015](#poms13576-bib-0018), Chemmanur and Fedaseyeu [2017](#poms13576-bib-0026), Davidson et al. [2004](#poms13576-bib-0040), Kothari et al. [2009](#poms13576-bib-0072), Pourciau [1993](#poms13576-bib-0094), Song and Thakor [2006](#poms13576-bib-0107), Wells [2002](#poms13576-bib-0116)), and that the chosen risk management mechanism may depend on the CEO's tenure.
The contexts of these empirical CEO turnover and risk management studies are predominantly in finance (Bornemann et al. [2015](#poms13576-bib-0018), Davidson et al. [2004](#poms13576-bib-0040), Weisbach [1995](#poms13576-bib-0115)) and accounting (Ali and Zhang [2015](#poms13576-bib-0003), Baginski et al. [2018](#poms13576-bib-0007), Murphy and Zimmerman [1993](#poms13576-bib-0083), Pourciau [1993](#poms13576-bib-0094), Wells [2002](#poms13576-bib-0116)); see Table [^1].
These studies indicate that early in their tenure, CEOs may accelerate the timing of negative financial news to get such problems out of the way at a time when they can more easily blame the previous CEO (Bens [2002](#poms13576-bib-0014), Pourciau [1993](#poms13576-bib-0094)), leading to less negative consequences for both the firm (Strong and Meyer [1987](#poms13576-bib-0109)) and the incumbent CEO (Bornemann et al. [2015](#poms13576-bib-0018), Davidson et al. [2004](#poms13576-bib-0040)).
Later in their tenure, when blaming a predecessor is less feasible, CEOs may simply hide problems (Baginski et al. [2018](#poms13576-bib-0007), Kothari et al. [2009](#poms13576-bib-0072)), to avoid the consequences from the negative news.

\* We searched for either of the phrases “risk management” or “firm performance” and either “CEO tenure” or “CEO turnover” in “A” level journals for Accounting, Finance, Management and Operations.
We reviewed the titles and abstracts of any paper with more than 200 citations for relevance to our study and selected the relevant studies to CEO turnover, discretion, and risk management.
<!-- para 6 -->
We apply this CEO tenure and risk management decision-making research stream to an OM setting by examining whether CEO tenure impacts recall decisions.
Specifically, we posit that early in tenure, a CEO may initiate recalls more frequently, either to “clean up shop” or because blame can be placed on the prior CEO, particularly when the predecessor was forced out of the role.
Conversely, later in tenure, CEOs may avoid initiating recalls altogether, particularly when the defect underlying the contemplated recall contains more discretion.
<!-- para 7 -->
In this work, we build upon a notable related study, Wowak et al. ([2015](#poms13576-bib-0119)).
They explore the FDA-regulated food, pharmaceutical, and medical device industries, and find that stock options offered early in a CEO's tenure may incent the CEO to make riskier product design, manufacturing, or distribution decisions, necessitating hard-to-avoid future severe recalls.
We extend the insight gained from Wowak et al. ([2015](#poms13576-bib-0119)) in two key ways.
First, we examine how CEO tenure may influence the recall decision itself, as opposed to the underlying reason for the recall.
In doing so, we contribute to the burgeoning research stream examining recall decision-making (Ball et al. [2018](#poms13576-bib-0010), Wowak et al. [2021](#poms13576-bib-0120)).
Second, by studying the consumer product industry, as opposed to tightly controlled FDA-regulated industries, we explore the CEO tenure and recall relationship in a significantly less regulated environment.
This juxtaposition allows us to develop meaningful and practical recommendations that arise from cross-industry comparisons.
<!-- para 8 -->
While we focus on CEO tenure, there are other factors that may also influence CEO risk management decisions.
These include board of directors oversight (Baliga et al. [1996](#poms13576-bib-0009), Dalton et al. [1999](#poms13576-bib-0125), Hazarika et al. [2012](#poms13576-bib-0059), Krause et al. [2014](#poms13576-bib-0074), Westphal [1999](#poms13576-bib-0117)) including board size (Dalton et al. [1999](#poms13576-bib-0125)), board composition (Hazarika et al. [2012](#poms13576-bib-0059)), and CEO-board chair duality (Baliga et al. [1996](#poms13576-bib-0009)) as a few examples.
While we control for such factors in our study, we focus on CEO tenure because it can provide underlying explanations for risky CEO decisions whereas board-related factors help provide solutions that may regulate self-serving CEO behavior.
<!-- para 9 -->
Using data on 125 publicly traded consumer product firms, 307 CEO appointments, and 584 recalls during the 25-year period from 1992 to 2016, we find that the hazard of a recall is 77% higher early in a CEO's tenure, or approximately their first three years on the job, compared to later in tenure.
We theorize that this may occur because CEOs can more easily blame predecessors in this early phase.
This is supported empirically, as we find that the early tenure recall hazard is higher when the previous CEO was forced out of their job.
We also find that the low hazard of a recall later in a CEO's tenure is more prominent for recalls that involve significant discretion, symptomatic of recalls that can be more easily avoided.
In fact, we find that the relatively low hazard of a recall later in a CEO's tenure is only present for the more discretionary recalls, while the relationship between CEO tenure and recall hazard is absent when examining recalls that have less discretion.
Robustness checks indicate consistent support for our results after examining model selection, measurement sensitivity, sample selection, reverse causality, and alternative explanations.
<!-- para 10 -->
This study contributes to the literature examining CEO tenure and risk management (Adams and Ferreira [2007](#poms13576-bib-0001), Baginski et al. [2018](#poms13576-bib-0007), Bornemann et al. [2015](#poms13576-bib-0018), Chemmanur and Fedaseyeu [2017](#poms13576-bib-0026), Davidson et al. [2004](#poms13576-bib-0040), Kothari et al. [2009](#poms13576-bib-0072), Pourciau [1993](#poms13576-bib-0094), Skinner [1994](#poms13576-bib-0104), Song and Thakor [2006](#poms13576-bib-0107), Wells [2002](#poms13576-bib-0116)) in the context of product recall decision-making (Ball et al. [2017](#poms13576-bib-0011), Haunschild and Rhee [2004](#poms13576-bib-0058), Shah et al. [2017](#poms13576-bib-0102), Thirumalai and Sinha [2011](#poms13576-bib-0111), Wowak et al. [2015](#poms13576-bib-0119)), by showing that CEO tenure impacts risk management decision-making well beyond financial reporting problems, even to the point of creating public health and safety risks via faulty consumer goods.
Furthermore, to the best of our knowledge, this study is the first in this stream of literature to show that the link between CEO tenure and risk management decision-making is influenced by the level of discretion, in particular in an operational context involving product recalls that are either low or high in discretion.
Understanding the role of discretion leads to specific regulatory policy suggestions that may reduce the potential manipulation of the timing of recalls by reducing discretion.
By doing so, we contribute to the literature in industry studies and public policy through industry-specific observations about an operational problem that may be used to strengthen firm governance and regulatory policy (Joglekar et al. [2016](#poms13576-bib-0069)).
Specifically, we provide policy suggestions for the Consumer Product Safety Commission (CPSC), the regulatory body responsible for managing consumer product industry recalls.
We also propose a firm governance solution that emerges from a comparison of the firms in our sample against prototypical firms in other recall-intensive industries.

## theory
<!-- para 2 -->
In section [2.1](#poms13576-sec-0003), we build on attribution theory to propose a relationship between CEO tenure and recalls, leading to a high hazard early in tenure and a low hazard afterward.
This leads to Hypothesis 1.
In sections [2.2](#poms13576-sec-0004) and [2.3](#poms13576-sec-0005), we present theoretical mechanisms that may explain circumstances under which the high hazard early in tenure is even higher, leading to Hypothesis 2, and the low hazard later in tenure is even lower, leading to Hypothesis [^2].
<!-- para 4 -->
We leverage attribution theory to help explain the relationship between CEO tenure and risk management in recall decision-making.
Attribution theory is a framework that explains how people attribute blame or credit following noteworthy events (Heider [1958](#poms13576-bib-0061), Weiner [1985](#poms13576-bib-0114)).
Attribution theory is framed by studies finding evidence that individuals may take credit for successes and deflect blame for failures (Kelley and Michela [1980](#poms13576-bib-0070), Weiner [1985](#poms13576-bib-0114), Zuckerman [1979](#poms13576-bib-0124)).
For example, Zuckerman ([1979](#poms13576-bib-0124)) shows that people are more likely to attribute success to their own ability, while blaming failures on others.
This pattern of behavior is found in research conducted within many contexts, including politics (Nielsen and Baekgaard [2015](#poms13576-bib-0089)), crisis management (Coombs [2007](#poms13576-bib-0030), Coombs and Holladay [2006](#poms13576-bib-0031)), educational achievement (Graham [1991](#poms13576-bib-0055)), and firm leadership decisions (Martinko et al. [2007](#poms13576-bib-0081), Pfeffer [1977](#poms13576-bib-0093)).
While CEOs may have multiple reasons for initiating recalls, attribution theory suggests that an underlying explanation for how CEOs manage such risky decisions may be tied to blame avoidance.
<!-- para 5 -->
Studies find that the effort to attribute blame to others following a negative event is strongest when expectations are high, such as with CEOs (Kelley and Michela [1980](#poms13576-bib-0070)).
Specifically, studies of executive behavior indicate that blame is often attributed to other executives following negative events (Boeker [1992](#poms13576-bib-0017), Bornemann et al. [2015](#poms13576-bib-0018), Davidson et al. [2004](#poms13576-bib-0040), Park et al. [2014](#poms13576-bib-0091), Pourciau [1993](#poms13576-bib-0094), Wells [2002](#poms13576-bib-0116)) and that early in a CEO's tenure, blame may be more easily attributed to predecessors (Bornemann et al. [2015](#poms13576-bib-0018), Davidson et al. [2004](#poms13576-bib-0040), Pourciau [1993](#poms13576-bib-0094), Wells [2002](#poms13576-bib-0116)).
For example, Pourciau ([1993](#poms13576-bib-0094)) finds that early tenure CEOs move up negative spending items to attribute responsibility to their predecessor.
Similarly, Wells ([2002](#poms13576-bib-0116)) studies the 100 largest public Australian firms from 1984 to 1994 and finds a drop in firm earnings in the first year of a CEO's tenure followed by a sharp and subsequent increase in earnings afterward.
Wells argues that this is due to CEOs attributing abnormal earnings adjustments to unresolved past problems caused by the previous CEO.
Studies of incoming CEOs in the banking sector find that CEOs may take large losses early in their tenure while they are able to attribute these problems to prior CEOs (Bornemann et al. [2015](#poms13576-bib-0018), Davidson et al. [2004](#poms13576-bib-0040)).
<!-- para 6 -->
These cited studies identifying blame attribution as a mechanism of CEO risk management focus primarily on finance and accounting.
We did not identify any CEO tenure and risk management studies that examine the attribution of blame for operational failures, such as the recall of defective products that may harm consumers.
We expect that the temptation to initiate more recalls early in tenure and few recalls later in tenure will be especially evident in the context of our study, the consumer products industry, for two reasons: consumer product recall rarity and CPSC regulatory strategy.
<!-- para 7 -->
Consumer product recalls are quite rare, occurring only once every five years per firm in the time of our study (CPSC [2019](#poms13576-bib-0034)).
This rarity is in contrast with other recall-intensive industries in the United States.
In the same time period: auto firms initiated one recall every two months on average (NHTSA [2019](#poms13576-bib-0087)); medical device firms initiated one recall every four months on average (FDA [2019](#poms13576-bib-0050)); pharmaceutical firms initiated one recall every six months on average (FDA [2019](#poms13576-bib-0050)); and food firms initiated one recall every eight months on average (FDA [2019](#poms13576-bib-0050), USDA [2019](#poms13576-bib-0112)).
The fact that consumer product recalls are rare is a key reason we study CEOs, because research indicates that infrequently occurring negative events are associated with direct CEO approval and oversight (Smith et al. [1994](#poms13576-bib-0106)).
This contention is supported by examples in the press that point to direct CEO approval of consumer product recalls (Harrison [2008](#poms13576-bib-0057), Rastegar [2011](#poms13576-bib-0098)).
<!-- para 8 -->
The regulatory strategy of the CPSC is also considerably less stringent than the strategies espoused by the three other recall regulators in the United States: The National Highway Safety Traffic Administration (NHTSA), the Food and Drug Administration (FDA), and the U.S. Department of Agriculture (USDA).
For example, the CPSC conducts no regularly scheduled manufacturing plant audits or quality inspections (CPSC [2019](#poms13576-bib-0034)).
In contrast, the FDA and USDA conduct detailed, multi-day quality inspections at the facilities they regulate (FDA [2019](#poms13576-bib-0050), USDA [2019](#poms13576-bib-0112)), which include all food, drug, and medical device facilities.
Additionally, the CPSC practically never mandates a recall, leaving the decision up to the volition of managers.
In fact, the CPSC has only mandated six recalls in the 25 years we study, which equates to 0.19% of all recalls during that time (CPSC [2019](#poms13576-bib-0034)).
Hence, in the consumer products industry, a mandated recall is not a substantial threat.
In contrast, although NHTSA does not regularly inspect facilities they regulate, NHTSA does mandate nearly 20% of all auto industry recalls (NHTSA [2019](#poms13576-bib-0087)).
This actual threat of a mandated recall can help to reduce discretion and may compel firms to recall in closer proximity to when the defect was actually discovered (Ducharme [2019](#poms13576-bib-0047)).
The non-intrusive strategy of CPSC, combined with the rarity of consumer product recalls, may create an environment in which consumer product CEOs are tempted to “time” recalls in a manner consistent with the attribution theory studies cited above.
Thus, we hypothesize:
<!-- para 9 -->
Hypothesis 1.The hazard of a recall is high early in a CEO's tenure and low afterward.
<!-- para 11 -->
We next propose a moderation hypothesis to explore evidence for a potential mechanism explaining the early tenure relationship postulated in Hypothesis 1.
In particular, we examine whether the high hazard of a recall early in tenure is influenced by characteristics of the previous CEO.
If CEOs initiate recalls early in their tenure in an attempt to attribute blame to their predecessor, this effect is likely to be stronger when the previous CEO was forced out (Baker and Xuan [2016](#poms13576-bib-0008), Coombs and Holladay [2006](#poms13576-bib-0031), Murphy and Zimmerman [1993](#poms13576-bib-0083), Nielsen and Baekgaard [2015](#poms13576-bib-0089), Zuckerman [1979](#poms13576-bib-0124)).
This may be because CEOs who are forced out of the position have a lower reputation than CEOs who leave voluntarily (Ertugrul and Krishnan [2011](#poms13576-bib-0048), Shen et al. [2010](#poms13576-bib-0103)).
In the context of attribution theory, a lower reputation may create a more “blame-able” target (Calder and Burnkrant [1977](#poms13576-bib-0022), Coombs [2007](#poms13576-bib-0030), Laczniak et al. [2001](#poms13576-bib-0075), Nielsen and Baekgaard [2015](#poms13576-bib-0089), Zuckerman [1979](#poms13576-bib-0124)).
Following a forced CEO turnover, the new CEO may use the lower reputation of a forced-out predecessor to more easily attribute the blame for recalls.
Limited research outside OM supports this idea.
For example, Murphy and Zimmerman ([1993](#poms13576-bib-0083)) find that when a prior CEO was forced out, the current CEO is significantly more likely to accelerate the timing of financial losses into the early years of their tenure.
Thus, we hypothesize:
<!-- para 12 -->
Hypothesis 2.The high hazard of a recall early in a CEO's tenure is stronger when the previous CEO was forced out compared to when the previous CEO was not forced out.
<!-- para 14 -->
We next explore a potential mechanism that may help to explain the low hazard of a recall later in a CEO's tenure.
If the hazard of a recall is low later in a CEO's tenure, when it is difficult to blame a predecessor, it may indicate one of two things.
First, it may mean that product quality is improving the longer a CEO is in his or her position.
Alternatively, it may indicate that quality problems that should lead to a recall do not.
While we examine the former reason in robustness checks as an alternative explanation for our results, we offer arguments for the latter below that build upon signaling theory.
<!-- para 15 -->
Theoretical support for managing risk by avoiding disclosure of a negative event is found in signaling theory.
Signaling theory (Connelly et al. [2011](#poms13576-bib-0029)) proposes that voluntary disclosures may act as signals to the market that can enhance or damage an organization's reputation (Verrecchia [1983](#poms13576-bib-0113)) and that negative events signal to the market that firms and their leaders are not competent.
Consequently, when it is more difficult to blame a predecessor, the CEO may avoid recalls altogether, if such an option exists, to avoid sending negative market signals (Jacobs [2014](#poms13576-bib-0066)).
Research outside of operations has found this to be the case, as firm leaders, particularly CEOs, avoid disclosing negative financial information (Connelly et al. [2011](#poms13576-bib-0029), Graham et al. [2006](#poms13576-bib-0054), Healy and Palepu [1993](#poms13576-bib-0060), Matsumoto [2002](#poms13576-bib-0082), Reuer et al. [2012](#poms13576-bib-0099), Skinner [1994](#poms13576-bib-0104)), excessive executive compensation (Byrd et al. [1998](#poms13576-bib-0021), Chung et al. [2015](#poms13576-bib-0028), Laksmana [2008](#poms13576-bib-0076)), and poor governance news (Bear et al. [2010](#poms13576-bib-0013), Certo [2003](#poms13576-bib-0023), Certo et al. [2001](#poms13576-bib-0024)) given the opportunity.
For example, Weisbach ([1995](#poms13576-bib-0115)) finds that late tenure CEOs are less likely to sell a financial investment at a loss, to avoid the blame for having made the investment.
Graham et al. ([2006](#poms13576-bib-0054)) demonstrate that, when expectations cannot be lowered by blaming others, executives are likely to hide underperformance problems.
<!-- para 16 -->
If the failure to initiate potentially necessary recalls explains the low hazard of a recall later in a CEO's tenure, then the underlying discretion inherent in the recall decision should influence this low hazard rate.
Relatedly, recalls associated with more serious defects may be harder to avoid because they may be less discretionary than recalls associated with less serious defects.
In fact, recall research indicates that when product defects are more severe, there is less discretion whereas when defects are less severe, there can be significant discretion in deciding to recall (Ball et al. [2018](#poms13576-bib-0010)).
If the low hazard of a recall later in a CEO's tenure is accentuated by discretion, it would lend support to our proposed mechanism that consumer product recalls may go uninitiated as CEO tenure increases.
Thus, we hypothesize:
<!-- para 17 -->
Hypothesis 3.The low hazard of a recall later in a CEO's tenure is stronger for high discretion recalls compared to low discretion recalls.

## methods
<!-- para 3 -->
To investigate the relationship between CEO appointments and recalls, we use data on all voluntary product recalls and all CEO turnovers for all consumer products firms publicly traded on US exchanges for the 25-year study period from 1992 to 2016.
The study period begins in 1992 because this is the first full year for which we have both CEO and firm financial data.[<sup>1</sup>](#poms13576-note-1001_162 "Link to note") We obtained recall data from the CPSC (CPSC [2016](#poms13576-bib-0032)), industry data from the US Census annual survey of manufacturers, board data from Compustat BoardEx database, CEO data from the Compustat Execucomp database, and firm financial data from the Compustat financial database.
CPSC recall data include the recall date, firm name, and a recall description text.
Execucomp data include the firm name, the CEO name, gender, and start date.
Finally, to examine the CEO's operational background, we use the Bloomberg database.
The intersection of these data sources leads to a sample of 125 firms that experienced 584 recalls and 307 CEO appointments across a 25-year period.
Consumer products cover an extremely wide variety of goods, so a sizeable subset of the public is potentially impacted by consumer product recalls.
The expansive list of products recalled in our sample include toys, air conditioning units, clothes, furniture, power tools, electronics, and many others.
<!-- para 5 -->
Because our objective was to examine how CEO tenure is associated with the hazard of a future recall, we use a hazard model.
Hazard models estimate the hazard rate of an event occurring based upon independent variables that change across time, using time-to-event as the dependent variable.
Hazard models use two measures to estimate hazard rates, a time measure and a failure measure.
We measure time as the elapsed days from the first observed data point for the firm to each subsequent event for the firm (Box-Steffensmeier and Jones [1997](#poms13576-bib-0019), Craig et al. [2016](#poms13576-bib-0035), Dhanorkar et al. [2017](#poms13576-bib-0045)).
In our study, the first available data point for each firm is the earliest record available in Compustat's Execucomp database.
We measure the total time in days from this date to one of two types of events in our data, a CEO turnover or a recall.
We treat recalls as failures (failure measure = 1) and CEO turnovers as non-failures (failure measure = 0).
Because many firms in our sample experience more than one recall, we use a recurrent-event hazard model with clustered standard errors at the firm level (Box-Steffensmeier and Jones [1997](#poms13576-bib-0019)).
We assume an exponential distribution for the underlying hazard rate as it assumes that failures are memoryless after controlling for explanatory variables, making it one of the more parsimonious distributions in parametric hazard modeling (Box-Steffensmeier and Jones [1997](#poms13576-bib-0019)).
However, to ensure that this modeling choice is not the underlying reason for our results, we demonstrate that results are robust to two other commonly used distributions, Weibull, and Gompertz (see Online Appendix Table [A1](#support-information-section)).
<!-- para 6 -->
There are two main ways to handle the time measure in a recurrent-event hazard model such as ours.
One way is to allow the time measure to continue to grow after each event for a given firm (Lee and Wang [2003](#poms13576-bib-0077), Prentice et al. [1981](#poms13576-bib-0096)); that is, time to an event is always measured since the beginning of the data for a given firm.
The other approach is to reset the time to zero after each failure for a given firm (Lee and Wang [2003](#poms13576-bib-0077), Prentice et al. [1981](#poms13576-bib-0096)); that is, time is measured since the last failure.
We chose the former method because longer panels like ours tend to have a large number of failures within a firm (in our data, there are 4.7 average recalls per firm) and may therefore be better suited toward a continuously incremented time measure due to shared variance that develops within a firm with multiple failures (Allison [1984](#poms13576-bib-0004), Lee and Wang [2003](#poms13576-bib-0077), Skrondal and Rabe-Hesketh [2004](#poms13576-bib-0105)).
We evaluate the robustness of this methodological choice in section [5.1](#poms13576-sec-0016).
<!-- para 9 -->
*Time of Recall—Time of Recall* is the number of days elapsed from the first recorded date in Compustat Execucomp to each recall for a firm.
<!-- para 11 -->
*CEO Time* — *CEO Time* is the number of days from each individual CEO's start date until the date of the recall.
This measure resets each time a new CEO is appointed.
Because we hypothesize two different mechanisms that dominate in early and late phases of tenure respectively, we divide this time into tertiles.
Using tertiles provides a means of measuring diverging effects of early and late tenure as separate indicator variables in the same regression model, with an equal number of observations in each category.
We treat the middle tertile as the reference category.
The first tertile is *Early Tenure*, and spans 0–2.8 years, and the last tertile is *Late Tenure*, and spans 6.7–48.6 years.
<!-- para 13 -->
*Previous CEO Forced—* To examine the moderating effect of the previous CEO, we measure whether the previous CEO was forced out.
To do so, we follow the three-step approach from Goldman and Haung ([2015](#poms13576-bib-0053)), which mirrored the process developed by Parrino ([1997](#poms13576-bib-0092)) used in similar studies examining forced CEO turnovers (Fisman et al. [2014](#poms13576-bib-0051), Goldman and Huang [2015](#poms13576-bib-0053), Nguyen [2012](#poms13576-bib-0085)).
To quote Goldman and Huang ([2015](#poms13576-bib-0053), p. 1111): “First, we classify the departure as forced if the *Wall Street Journal* reports that the CEO was fired, forced out, or departed as a result of unspecified policy differences.
Second, we classify the departure as forced for all other departing CEOs under the age of 60 who were not reported as leaving because of death, poor health, acceptance of another position, or retirement.
Finally, we investigate these forced departures further using additional news sources to verify that the CEOs did not leave voluntarily.” We followed this same approach exactly.
We assign the variable *Prev*. *CEO Forced* a value of 1 when the previous CEO was forced out and 0 otherwise.
In the study period, there were 114 forced CEO turnovers and 193 unforced turnovers.
<!-- para 14 -->
*Discretion—* To measure discretion, we develop a novel measure of recall discretion that we believe can help contribute to the research of recall decision-making and recall discretion.
Recent studies that explore product recall discretion (Ball et al. [2018](#poms13576-bib-0010), Wowak et al. [2021](#poms13576-bib-0120)) use data from industries regulated by the FDA, which independently classifies recalls as high or low severity.
In these studies, a high severity recall is treated as low discretion, and a low severity recall is treated as high discretion.
In the consumer products industry, no such regulator-provided severity classification exists.[<sup>2</sup>](#poms13576-note-1002_186 "Link to note") In fact, little contextual information is provided in CPSC recall announcements that help to differentiate severity in order to gauge discretion.
Therefore, we develop an alternative means to differentiate discretion by exploiting financial disclosure requirements for material events.
Generally accepted accounting principles (GAAP) require that when a firm event is serious enough to materially impact the firm's financials, it must be disclosed in annual financial filings with the Securities and Exchange Commission (SEC), and, when it is not, no such disclosure is required (FASB [2017](#poms13576-bib-0049)).
We use this SEC reporting requirement to distinguish between recalls that have significant discretion, which are the recalls that are not serious enough to require disclosure in financial statements, and those that have little discretion, which are disclosed in financial statements.
For each firm in the study, we obtained all available 10-K filings for all years of the study period.
We searched for the presence of the word “recall” in each 10-K and reviewed all 10-Ks that contained the word to match against the recalls within our data set.
If a specific recall is mentioned in any of the firm's 10-K filings, we designate it as *Low Discretion*, otherwise we code the recall as *High Discretion*.
In the study period, there were 129 low discretion recalls and 455 high discretion recalls.[<sup>3</sup>](#poms13576-note-1003_188 "Link to note")

*Industry Characteristics* —This category has two control variables, *Dynamism* and *Munificence*, which describe the overall environment facing the industry, and which may impact firm behavior toward quality (Dess and Beard [1984](#poms13576-bib-0044), Wowak et al. [2015](#poms13576-bib-0119)).
We consider firms to be the in the same industry if they share the same Global Industry Classification Standard (GICS) 4-digit code.
We construct the measures exactly as done by Dess and Beard ([1984](#poms13576-bib-0044)).
<!-- para 20 -->
*Firm Characteristics* —This category has 14 control variables.
Each of these variables is intended to control for an aspect of the firm that may influence its prior recall and turnover history (Brunello et al. [2003](#poms13576-bib-0020), Thirumalai and Sinha [2011](#poms13576-bib-0111)), including firm performance (Altman [1968](#poms13576-bib-0005), Bhandari [1988](#poms13576-bib-0015), Chaney and Devinney [1992](#poms13576-bib-0025), Dalton and Kesner [1983](#poms13576-bib-0036), Wowak et al. [2015](#poms13576-bib-0119)), board oversight (Hazarika et al. [2012](#poms13576-bib-0059)), operational complexity and size (Damanpour [1996](#poms13576-bib-0037), Hora et al. [2011](#poms13576-bib-0063), Palepu [1985](#poms13576-bib-0090), Shah et al. [2017](#poms13576-bib-0102), Thirumalai and Sinha [2011](#poms13576-bib-0111)), or firm age (Fortune and Mitchell [2012](#poms13576-bib-0052)).
<!-- para 21 -->
*CEO Characteristics* —This category has eight control variables.
Characteristics of the CEO that may influence their decision-making include the CEO's incentives (Wowak et al. [2015](#poms13576-bib-0119)), personal characteristics (Powell and Ansic [1997](#poms13576-bib-0095), Wowak et al. [2015](#poms13576-bib-0119), [2021](#poms13576-bib-0120)), and their relationship to the firm and the board (Datta and Guthrie [1994](#poms13576-bib-0038), Donaldson and Davis [1991](#poms13576-bib-0046), Zajac [1990](#poms13576-bib-0121)).
<!-- para 22 -->
To control for skewed distributions and to aid in interpretability, we log-transform, then center and standardize all non-indicator variables, except industry *Dynamism* and *Munificence*, which are not right-skewed.
In addition, we control for unobservable time-invariant firm, product type, and year variation by including *firm, product type,* and *year fixed effects* in every model.
Please see Online Appendix [A2](#support-information-section) for the method used to define product types from the data.

<!-- para 27 -->
To ensure that our results are robust to our chosen empirical modeling strategy, we evaluate two alternatives to our recurrent-event hazard modeling approach.
The first alternative model is a hazard model that resets the time measure for failure within a firm.
In our primary analysis, we allowed the time of recall to continue to grow from the beginning of the panel until the end for a given firm.
However, an alternative approach is to reset the time measure to zero after each recall for a given firm.
The results of this alternative model specification are presented in Online Appendix Table [A3](#support-information-section).
Column 3 of Table [A3](#support-information-section) indicates consistent support in comparison to Table [^6] as the *Early Tenure β =* 0.31 (*p <* 0.05) and the *Late Tenure β = –* 0.74 (*p <* 0.001).
The second alternative model is a logistic regression.
Previous research has leveraged logistic regression as a means to the confirm results of a hazard model.
This is because logistic regression is somewhat simpler than hazard modeling as it considers only whether or not a failure occurred, rather than accounting for the time to failure (Staley et al. [2017](#poms13576-bib-0108)).
For this analysis, we used the failure measure from the hazard model as the dependent variable but did not use the time of recall aspect of the dependent variable from the hazard model.
Results of this alternative model are shown in Online Appendix Table [A4](#support-information-section).
Column 3 of Table [A4](#support-information-section) demonstrates consistent results in comparison to Table [^6], indicating that having an early tenure CEO is associated with an increased likelihood of a recall (*β =* 6.56, *p <* 0.001), while a late tenure CEO is associated with a decreased likelihood of a recall (*β =* –6.37, *p <* 0.001).
In aggregate, the results of these alternative specifications help build confidence that our hazard modeling strategy is robust to alternative methodological choices.
<!-- para 29 -->
*CEO Time* is the central measure of our model.
We separate *CEO Time* into tertiles to create a division of time with equal observations in each category that allows us to evaluate the effects of *Early* and *Late Tenure* on recall hazard in the same regression model.
However, to ensure our results are robust to alternative measures of *CEO Time*, we incorporate two additional *CEO Time* measures.
First, we replace the variables *Early* and *Late Tenure* with a linear effect of the natural logarithm of *CEO Time*.
The results are presented in Online Appendix Table [A5](#support-information-section), column 3.
The relationship between the natural log of CEO Time and the hazard of a recall is negative and significant (column 3, Table [A5](#support-information-section), *LN CEO Time β =* –0.49, *p <* 0.001), indicating that the hazard of a recall decreases as tenure increases, consistent with Hypothesis 1.
We also replace the CEO tenure tertiles with quartiles.
The results of the quartile model are presented in Online Appendix Table [A5](#support-information-section), column 5, in which we use the second quartile (2.1–4.6 years) as the reference category.
In this analysis, we observe that the hazard of a recall during the first quartile, from 0 to 2.1 years, is positive and significant (*β =* 0.34, *p <* 0.001), which closely parallels the results from Table [^6] of the first tertile from 0 to 2.8 years.
Similarly, the hazard of a recall in the third quartile of CEO tenure, from 4.6 to 7.8 years, is negative and significant (*β = –* 0.58, *p <* 0.001) as well as the fourth quartile, from 7.8 to 48.6 years (*β = –* 1.20, *p <* 0.001).

We plot the hazard of a recall under the tertile, linear, and quartile specifications in Figure [2](#poms13576-fig-0002).
As in Figure [1](#poms13576-fig-0001), we plot CEO time along the *x* -axis.
In all three specifications, the recall hazard is highest in the first two to three years of tenure and decreases steadily and significantly afterward.
These results shown in Figure [2](#poms13576-fig-0002) provide overall support for Hypothesis 1 and indicate that our finding that the hazard of a recall is high early in a CEO's tenure and decreases afterward is unlikely to be an artifact of using tertiles to measure CEO time.

## discussion
<!-- para 2 -->
While past research has examined the relationship between CEO tenure and recall occurrences (Wowak et al. [2015](#poms13576-bib-0119)), to the best of our knowledge, this study is the first to demonstrate a relationship between CEO tenure and recall decision-making.
Our results indicate that the hazard of a recall is high early in CEO tenure, especially when the previous CEO was forced out, and low later in CEO tenure, especially when the recall contains significant discretion.
We distill these findings to three theoretical contributions, one firm governance recommendation, and four public policy recommendations for the CPSC.
