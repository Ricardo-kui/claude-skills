# certo2016_a_tale_of_two_effects_using_longitudinal_data_to_c 句子库存

## introduction
<!-- para 1 -->
Managerial Summary: Strategy research examines two important sources of variation over time: what is occurring within the firm (e.g., Do firms perform better over time when investing more in R&D?) and what is occurring between firms (e.g., Do firms investing more in R&D outperform firms investing less in R&D?).
These two sources may be similar or different in both direction and magnitude, and when significant differences exist in either direction or magnitude researchers must carefully consider the implication of these differences to their theoretical rationale and statistical testing.
Our paper highlights the benefits of theorizing and testing these two sources of variance, providing scholars the ability to broaden both the theoretical and empirical contribution of their research.
It is important to understand this distinction when considering how research results might inform managerial decision making.
<!-- para 2 -->
---
<!-- para 3 -->
Since the early 2000s, strategy scholars have increasingly relied on longitudinal data to test theorized relationships.
In 2004, approximately 15 percent of articles published in SMJ involved longitudinal data.
By 2014, more than half of articles published in SMJ involved longitudinal data.
During that period, researchers used longitudinal data to test a variety of topics including sustainability strategies (e.g., Bansal, 2005), firm reputation (e.g., Basdeo et al., 2006), merger waves (e.g., Haleblian et al., 2012), strategic alliances (e.g., Koka and Prescott, 2008), downsizing (e.g., Love and Nohria, 2005), and CEO compensation (e.g., Bodolica and Spraggon, 2009).
<!-- para 4 -->
Despite this remarkable increase, strategy researchers have not fully capitalized on the fact that longitudinal data are multilevel in nature.
Longitudinal data include two types of variance: within-unit (e.g., within-firm, within-person, etc.) and between-unit (e.g., between-firm, between-person, etc.) variance. [^1] These two types of variance correspond to two different relationships: within-firm and between-firm relationships.
This allows researchers to consider not only whether certain strategic behaviors across firms lead to different performance outcomes (i.e., between-firm effect) but also whether changes in strategic behaviors within a firm lead to changes in the firm’s performance (i.e., within-firm effect).
<!-- para 5 -->
Multilevel research in organizational behavior and psychology suggests that relationships among constructs often differ across levels (Chan, 1998).
Although constructs in this research often involve individuals nested within teams, the same issues pertain to longitudinal data (i.e., Does the within-firm relationship between R&D and performance mirror the corresponding between-firm effect?).
Cross-level isomorphism occurs when relationships between constructs at lower levels (e.g., individuals) mirror the corresponding relationships at higher levels (e.g., teams) (Chen, Bliese, and Mathieu, 2005; Tay, Woo, and Vermunt, 2014).
Firebaugh (1978) points out, though, that aggregated variables often measure different constructs from the analogous variables at lower levels.
Accordingly, Bliese (2000: 368) suggests that in organizational research “true isomorphism is probably quite rare.”
<!-- para 6 -->
If this lack of isomorphism also holds in strategic management, many between-firm relationships will differ in magnitude and/or direction from their within-firm counterparts.
We have considerable evidence—in the form of published Hausman tests—to support the premise that these relationships differ in many cases.
When analyzing longitudinal data, researchers typically invoke a Hausman test (Hausman, 1978) to examine the extent to which a set of relationships based on within-firm variance (e.g., fixed effects models) differ from a set of relationships based on a combination of both within- and between-firm variance (e.g., random effects models).
A significant test indicates that the two sets of relationships differ statistically.
We reviewed all longitudinal studies in SMJ from January 2014 to October 2015 that used a Hausman test.
We found that 66 percent of these studies revealed statistically significant differences between the two sets of relationships.
<!-- para 7 -->
It is this evidence of different relationships across levels—coupled with the fact that strategy scholars have largely ignored exploring these potential differences—that motivates our work.
Unfortunately, decisions in strategy research about which longitudinal analysis to perform are driven largely by Hausman test results as opposed to theory.
When a Hausman test is statistically significant, strategy researchers overwhelmingly rely on fixed effects models.
The disadvantage of this approach, however, is that fixed effects models simply discard all available between-firm variance.
Consequently, using fixed effects models prevents researchers from gaining any insights about between-firm relationships.
<!-- para 8 -->
Through our work we provide two contributions to all strategy researchers, regardless of their theoretical orientations.
First, we highlight the theoretical implications associated with distinguishing between within- versus between-firm relationships.
We use two of the most common theoretical perspectives in strategy, the resource based view (RBV) (Barney, 1991) and agency theory (Jensen and Meckling, 1976), to illustrate the importance of this distinction.
Our second contribution is methodological in nature.
We propose that strategy scholars employ the hybrid approach, which is a technique used by scholars in other disciplines to disentangle within-versus between-firm relationships, when analyzing longitudinal data (Allison, 2005; Schunck, 2013).
Simply stated, this technique combines the advantages of fixed effects models with those of random effects models and allows scholars to compare within- versus between-firm relationships among constructs.
<!-- para 9 -->
After explaining the intuition underlying this technique, we reconsider Chen and Miller's (2007) investigation of R&D intensity to show how the hybrid approach provides information above and beyond a traditional fixed effects model.
Our analyses demonstrate substantive differences in within- versus between-firm effects.
As Bliese (2000) points out, a within-firm effect that statistically differs from a between-firm effect does not imply that either effect is "wrong." Instead, such findings indicate only that scholars cannot generalize an estimate of the within-firm effect to the between-firm effect and vice versa.
<!-- para 10 -->
We agree with Chen et al.'s (2005) suggestion that investigations of cross-level similarities and differences contribute to theoretical development.
If between-firm relationships mirror within-firm relationships, it adds to the parsimony and scope of theories.
In contrast, between-firm relationships that differ from within-firm relationships indicate potential theoretical boundary conditions.
Regardless of the outcome, then, tests of cross-level similarities and differences help to test and potentially extend existing theoretical frameworks.
We propose that more specific theory, hypotheses, and analysis will lead to more compelling theory building and testing in strategy research.
<!-- para 13 -->
When examining cross-sectional data, hypotheses and statistical findings involve between-firm effects.
A researcher examining the relationship between corporate social performance and financial performance, for example, might hypothesize: ‘Corporate social performance is positively associated with financial performance.’ Using a cross-sectional sample to test this hypothesis explicitly adopts a between-firm orientation, and a positive relationship implies that firms investing more in corporate social performance are associated with higher levels of financial performance as compared to firms investing less in corporate social performance.
<!-- para 14 -->
Scholars relying on longitudinal data, however, must recognize that the variance they seek to explain exists at two levels: within- and between-firm.
Within-firm variance represents change occurring within firms over time, while between-firm variance denotes change that occurs between firms over time.
Both variance components provide important insights depending on the theory proposed.
For example, a researcher may wish to examine how within-firm changes in an independent variable influence the dependent variable.
Conversely, a researcher may wish to examine how between-firm differences in an independent variable influence the dependent variable.
As Curran and Bauer (2011: 586) summarize in their review of psychology research, longitudinal data:
<!-- para 15 -->
“...simultaneously contain information about both between-person and within-person differences...
Such data provide the opportunity to identify relationships that hold within persons as well as relationship that hold across persons.
Both types of relationships can have important implications for theory.”
<!-- para 16 -->
Researchers in other disciplines such as education, sociology and psychology have examined how relationships change across levels (Blalock, 1984; Enders and Tofighi, 2007; Raudenbush, 1989).
Scholars use the term cross-level isomorphism to reference when a relationship at one level mirrors the relationship at another level (e.g., Chan, 1998).
In contrast, the direction and/or magnitude of a relationship may differ across levels.
The term ecological fallacy refers to instances when researchers mistakenly assume that a relationship at one level mirrors the relationship at another level (Robinson, 1950).
Perhaps the most intuitive example of an ecological fallacy involves the relationship between body mass and life expectancy in mammals (Curran and Bauer, 2011).
Mammalian species with higher body masses (e.g., whales) are associated with longer life expectancies as compared to species with lower body masses (e.g., mice); this is a between-animal effect.
At the same time, though, within each species there is a negative relationship between body mass and life expectancy (e.g., heavier mice are not expected to live as long as lighter mice).
<!-- para 17 -->
In the context of micro organizational research, Bliese (2000) suggests that cross-level isomorphism is uncommon, but strategy researchers have yet to examine this issue.
We believe that incorporating the distinction between within- and between-firm effects could allow strategy researchers to extend theoretical frameworks in novel ways.
As Curran and Bauer (2011: 587) note, “Failing to recognize the important distinction between these effects can result in consequential errors of inference.” In the following discussion, we examine how this distinction may extend both RBV and agency theory, two of the most popular theoretical frameworks in strategic management (Boyd et al., 2012).
<!-- para 19 -->
The resource-based view (RBV) of the firm (Barney, 1991; Peteraf, 1993; Wernerfelt, 1984) represents one of the most influential theories in strategic management.
The central premise of this theory is that firms with resources that are valuable, rare, costly to imitate, and non-substitutable will enjoy a sustainable competitive advantage.
Highlighting the prominence of RBV, Barney, Ketchen, and Wright (2011: 1300) suggest that RBV “is widely acknowledged as one of the most prominent and powerful theories for describing, explaining, and predicting organizational relationships.”
<!-- para 20 -->
Despite the influence of RBV in strategy research, the empirical literature remains relatively silent with respect to distinguishing between within- versus between-firm effects.
Ndofor, Sirmon, and He’s (2011) examination of firm resources and firm performance among high-tech firms provides an example of the opportunities and challenges that arise with this distinction. [^2] Using RBV as a theoretical framework, the authors suggest that the breadth and complexity of a firm’s resource portfolio will increase rarity, thus improving firm performance.
<!-- para 21 -->
Accordingly, the first hypothesis in this study states that, “The breadth of a firm’s technological resources will positively affect its performance” (644, emphasis in the original).
Similarly, Hypothesis 2a in the paper states that, “The complexity of a firm’s competitive behavior will positively affect its performance” (644, emphasis in the original).
<!-- para 22 -->
While both of these hypotheses are consistent with RBV logic, the use of longitudinal data complicates the theoretical relationship.
A within-firm interpretation of the first hypothesis suggests that a firm increasing the breadth of its technological resources will enjoy higher performance in the future relative to what it experienced in the past.
In contrast, a between-firm interpretation of this hypothesis suggests that firms with greater breadth of technological resources will outperform other firms with less breadth.
Similar interpretations also apply to the second hypothesis.
<!-- para 24 -->
Agency theory provides another context in which to examine how between- and within-firm effects may differ (Jensen and Meckling, 1976).
One of the central concepts in agency theory is that agents may make decisions that are inconsistent with the desires of shareholders (Fama and Jensen, 1983).
Scholars have examined how board composition (Dalton et al., 1998), CEO compensation (Devers et al., 2007), and ownership structure (Dalton et al., 2003) influence firm performance (for a review of agency theory see Dalton et al., 2007).
<!-- para 25 -->
Wowak, Mannor, and Wowak’s (2015) investigation of CEO compensation provides an example of the opportunities and challenges associated with distinguishing between within- and between-firm effects.
According to their theoretical development, CEO stock option compensation may be associated with risk-taking.
One manifestation of this risk-taking involves releasing products to the public too quickly, a practice that may result in product safety problems.
Following this logic, their first hypothesis states that: “CEO stock option pay will positively influence the likelihood of subsequent product safety problems” (1085).
<!-- para 26 -->
This hypothesis, which was tested with longitudinal data, does not specify the level at which this relationship may exist.
A within-firm interpretation of this hypothesis suggests that increasing CEO stock option pay will lead to more product safety problems for the firm in the future than it has experienced in the past.
In contrast, a between-firm interpretation of this hypothesis indicates that firms with higher levels of CEO stock option compensation will be associated with more product safety problems as compared to firms with lower levels of CEO stock option compensation.
Taken together, these relationships may differ, and an examination of these differences may help to advance theory regarding these constructs.
<!-- para 28 -->
The examples above illustrate how hypotheses may be agnostic to the multiple relationships that could potentially exist.
While we focused on both agency theory and RBV, these arguments could apply to any other framework in strategic management.
The RBV example is perhaps the most intuitive to understand.
Testing Ndofor et al.'s hypothesis with within-firm variance provides no insight regarding the relative position of a firm's resources with respect to its competitors.
Similarly, testing the hypothesis with only between-firm variance provides no insight with respect to how a firm's resources change over time.
Finally, testing the hypothesis while combining both types of variance does not allow researchers to understand how these effects may differ.
Similar arguments, of course, also pertain to the agency theory example.
<!-- para 29 -->
As we detail later, both of the papers highlighted in the previous section employ techniques that combine both within- and between-firm effects.
Such techniques assume that these effects are identical, which makes it impossible to disentangle within- versus between-firm effects.
Approaches that combine variance are less precise than alternative approaches that isolate within- versus between-firm variance.
In the following sections, we switch our focus to empirical issues associated with longitudinal data.
We highlight three general approaches to analyzing longitudinal data: those that combine within-firm and between-firm effects, those that examine only within-firm effects, and those that separate within-firm and between-firm effects.
<!-- para 32 -->
To understand the empirical complexities associated with the analysis of longitudinal data, it is important to first recognize how the nesting of observations over time introduces two types of variance for all dependent, independent, and control variables: between-firm and within-firm variance.
A high level of between-firm variance suggests that firms differ to a large degree with respect to a variable (i.e., some firms are associated with high scores and other firms are associated with low scores).
In contrast, a high level of within-firm variance indicates that firms differ a great deal with respect to the variable over time (i.e., some firm-year observations for a firm are associated with higher values and other firm-year observations for the same firm are associated with lower values).
Equation 1 helps to explain the idea of within versus between variance in longitudinal variables:
<!-- para 33 -->
$$ x_{it}=v_{i}+e_{it} $$
<!-- para 34 -->
(1)
<!-- para 35 -->
In this equation, the independent variable $(x_{it})$ consists of a between-firm component that remains stable over time $(v_i)$ and a within-firm component $(e_{it})$ that varies each time period.
Intuitively, as the variance in $v_i$ relative to $e_{it}$ increases, the proportion of between-firm variance in a variable increases.
Although Equation 1 refers to an independent variable, the same relationship applies to dependent (and control) variables.
<!-- para 36 -->
To examine the relative influence of between versus within variance in variables, researchers in other more micro-focused disciplines have relied on intraclass correlation coefficients (ICCs) (Bliese, 1998; Klein et al., 2000).
These coefficients express the between-group variance in a variable as a percentage of the overall variance (i.e., between-group plus within-group variance) in a variable.
Higher ICC values imply larger (smaller) proportions of between-group (within-group) variance, while lower values suggest lower (higher) proportions of between-group (within-group) variance.
LeBreton and Senter (2008) review several different types of ICCs (e.g., ICC(1), ICC(k), etc.) Following their guidance, we used ICC(1) values in our analyses.
Figure 1 illustrates the relationship between $v_i$, $e_{it}$, and the ICC of $x_{it}$.
<!-- para 39 -->
Although researchers in other fields have employed ICCs to assess the relative between-group versus within-group variance in the context of nested data, strategy researchers have largely ignored this technique with respect to longitudinal data.
For longitudinal research, ICCs represent the between-firm variance in a variable expressed as a percentage of the variable's overall variance (i.e., between-firm plus within-firm variance).
When searching SMJ between 2004 and 2014, we searched for "ICC," "intraclass correlation," "within variance," "within-firm variance," "between variance," and "between-firm variance." Of all studies published in SMJ during this time, less than five percent mentioned one of these terms.
<!-- para 40 -->
These differences in relative within- and between-firm variance have important implications for data analysis.
As Curran and Bauer (2011: 586) summarize in their review of psychology research that when analyzing longitudinal data, “the statistical models fitted to these data must be carefully specified to avoid confounding the two sources of variability.” Consistent with Curran and Bauer’s admonition to psychology scholars, strategy researchers must also take great care in understanding how analytical techniques take into account within- and between-firm variance.
<!-- para 42 -->
It is widely known among strategy researchers that using OLS regression is inappropriate for analyzing longitudinal data (e.g., Certo and Semadeni, 2006).
As a consequence, strategy researchers have applied several techniques to analyze longitudinal data and account for between-firm variance.
These techniques can be classified into two broad groups: 1) techniques that incorporate both between- and within-firm variance, and 2) techniques that incorporate only within-firm variance when estimating coefficients.
<!-- para 43 -->
**Both between- and within-firm variance.** Random effects models represent one of the most popular techniques for analyzing panel data in strategy.
Stated simply, random effects models combine both within- and between-firm variance when estimating the effects of independent variables on dependent variables.
This approach entails using maximum likelihood to introduce a random panel-level term representing the variation in intercepts between firms.
As shown in Equation 2, random effects models examine the relationships between the dependent $(y_{it})$ and independent variable $(x_{it})$ with an error term $(e_{it})$ that varies over time.
To account for the nested nature of the data, this approach also includes a firm-level disturbance, $(u_{i})$, that remains constant over time:
<!-- para 44 -->
$$ y_{it}=\beta_{1}x_{it}+u_{i}+e_{it} $$
<!-- para 45 -->
(2)
<!-- para 46 -->
A fundamental assumption of random effects models is that independent variables are not correlated with either the firm-level disturbance $(u_{i})$ or the error term that varies over time $(e_{it})$.
<!-- para 47 -->
Although it is not widely known among strategy researchers, multilevel models (also known as mixed models, hierarchical linear models, or HLM) represent a substantively similar approach for analyzing longitudinal data.
In fact, a comparison of the equations underlying random effects (e.g., Wooldridge, 2002) to multilevel models (e.g., Singer, 1998) illustrates the substantive equivalence of the two approaches.
In fact, multilevel models and random effects models report essentially the same results.
In Stata, using -xtreg, re- (e.g., random effects model) to estimate a model reports results that are almost identical to those from a model estimated with -mixed, mle- (e.g., multilevel/mixed model).
Models estimated with the default -mixed-command are also substantively similar (yet not identical) to these models.
<!-- para 48 -->
Strategy researchers have also used GEEs to analyze nested data.
Unlike mixed or random effects models, GEEs do not model a firm-level disturbance (i.e., $u_{i}$).
Instead, GEEs account for the dependence that may accompany nested observations by allowing researchers to specify the correlation structure for the nested observations of the dependent variable (Zorn, 2001).
Despite this subtle difference, GEEs also include both between- and within-firm variance.
<!-- para 49 -->
As Ballinger (2004) points out, users must specify a number of options when using GEEs.
Using Stata’s default xtgee options (distribution of the dependent variable as Gaussian, an identity link function, and an exchangeable within-firm correlation structure), such models will report results similar to those reported by random effects or mixed models.
Because GEEs use both between- and within-firm variance, it is important for strategy scholars to recognize that random effects, mixed/multilevel models, and certain types of GEEs report substantively similar results.
<!-- para 50 -->
Mixed variance approaches offer two primary advantages.
First, using both within- and between-firm variance increases the total amount of variance in the analysis.
This increase in variance improves model efficiency and, thus, statistical power.
Second, mixed variance approaches allow researchers to model two types of dependent and independent variables: those that change over time and those that remain invariant over time.
<!-- para 51 -->
The primary disadvantage of mixed variance models involves the potential correlation between the independent variables and the group-level disturbance, $u_{i}$.
Specifically, such approaches assume that the independent variables are uncorrelated with this disturbance.
When an omitted variable, for example, creates a correlation between an independent variable and the group-level disturbance, mixed models will report biased results.
Stated differently, the between-group variance is contaminated.
As we develop later, this correlation represents an example of endogeneity at the firm level.
Related to this issue, another disadvantage of mixed variance approaches is that such models assume that the between- and within-firm relationships are identical, because both effects are represented by the same coefficient (Rabe-Hesketh and Skrondal, 2012).
<!-- para 52 -->
**Within-firm variance.** Fixed effects models, which use only within-firm variation in the dependent and independent variables, have become the most popular technique for analyzing panel data in strategy research.
These models circumvent the primary disadvantage of mixed models involving the correlation between an independent variable and the group-level disturbance.
Specifically, fixed effects models subtract averages for each variable and disturbance in Equation 2, which results in the following equation:
<!-- para 53 -->
$$ (y_{it}-\bar{y}_{i})=\beta_{1}(x_{it}-\bar{x}_{i})+(u_{i}-\bar{u}_{i})+(e_{it}-\bar{e}_{i}) $$
<!-- para 54 -->
(3)
<!-- para 55 -->
Equation 3 shows that by discarding between-firm information and instead focusing only on within-firm information, the panel-level disturbance $(u_{i})$—which is constant over time—disappears.
Consequently, fixed effects models eliminate any bias that may result from a correlation between the panel-level disturbance and an independent variable (Allison, 2005).
The attractive feature of the fixed effects method, then, is that this technique poses no restrictions on the relationship between the firm-level disturbance and the independent variables in a model (Allison, 2005).
Equation 3 also illustrates that due to the group-mean centering, the only variance remaining is within-group variance; this is why fixed effects models are commonly referred to as within estimators (Allison, 2005).
<!-- para 56 -->
Despite this important advantage, the primary disadvantage of fixed effects involves model efficiency.
Specifically, fixed effects models are less efficient than random effects, because this approach discards the between-firm variance and by definition utilizes less information when computing estimates.
In addition, fixed effects models control, but are not able to estimate coefficients, for variables that do not vary over time.
This shortcoming results from the fact that fixed effects models only incorporate within-firm variation.
When there is no within-firm variation in an independent variable, it is impossible to estimate within-firm effects.
<!-- para 57 -->
**Hausman test.** When choosing between fixed effects versus random effects models, researchers typically employ a Hausman test.
This test compares the coefficient estimates from a random effects model to those from a fixed effects model.
A statistically significant Hausman test indicates that the set of coefficients from the fixed effects model is statistically different from the set of coefficients corresponding to a random effects model.
Stated differently, a statistically significant Hausman test indicates that at the model level the within-firm relationships (i.e., fixed effects) differ from the relationships that are based on both within- and between-firm variance (i.e., random effects).
In the presence of a statistically significant Hausman test, researchers rely on the fixed effects instead of random effects estimates.
Researchers assume that the different results must stem from between-firm variance that is correlated with the firm-level disturbance.
<!-- para 59 -->

Based on the limitations associated with fixed and random effects models, Allison (2005) introduced what he terms the hybrid approach (Schunck, 2013).
This technique combines the advantages of both within- and between-firm approaches.
Specifically, this approach splits each Level 1 independent variable into two variables: 1) a group-centered variable $(x_{it} - \bar{x}_i)$ and 2) a variable representing the group mean $(\bar{x}_i)$.
This approach involves using random effects models to estimate coefficients representing both the within- and between-firm effect of each independent variable.
These are shown in Equation 4:
<!-- para 64 -->
$$ y_{it}=\beta_{1}\bar{x}_{i}+\beta_{2}(x_{it}-\bar{x}_{i})+u_{i}+e_{it} $$
<!-- para 65 -->
(4)
<!-- para 66 -->
With this technique, the group-centered variable incorporates only within-group information.
As such, the estimate of $\beta_{2}$ associated with a group mean-centered variable is identical to the coefficient estimates associated with a fixed effects model.
As Rabe-Hesketh and Skrondal (2012) point out, $\beta_{2}$ cannot be correlated with the panel-level disturbance ( $u_{i}$) because the panel-level disturbance does not vary within firms.
<!-- para 67 -->
In this analysis, the group mean of the independent variable represents the between-firm effect of the independent variable.
The inclusion of this variable helps to control for between group variance in the independent variable that could potentially influence coefficient estimates for the variable in question as well as all other variables in the model.
<!-- para 68 -->
The chief advantage of Allison’s hybrid approach involves the ability to compare and contrast within- and between-firm influences of independent variable by examining $\beta_{1}$ and $\beta_{2}$.
Suppose a researcher collected longitudinal data to examine the relationship between R&D expenditures and firm performance.
A fixed effects model would only report the within-firm estimate of this relationship, while a random effects model would report a relationship of the combined within- and between-firm estimate of the relationship.
In contrast, the hybrid approach would separate and report both the within-firm estimate (i.e., the coefficient for the mean-centered variable) and the between-firm estimate (i.e., the coefficient for the group mean).
<!-- para 69 -->
Another advantage of this approach is that it allows researchers to implement a modified Hausman test.
As Schunck (2013) describes, researchers can use contrast tests to examine whether the set of coefficients for the between effects differ from the set of coefficients for the within-firm effects.
Moreover, researchers also have the flexibility with this approach to test these differences one variable at a time.
In this sense, the modified Hausman test disaggregates the different relationships that are tested simultaneously with the traditional Hausman test.
<!-- para 71 -->
Previously we highlighted how a between- vs. within-firm framework may help to better understand both agency theory and RBV.
In this section, we briefly review the analyses used by both Ndofor et al. (2011) and Wowak et al. (2015) to test these theoretical perspectives.
Ndofor et al. (2011) used GEEs to test how resource breadth and competitive complexity influenced firm performance among a sample of 69 firms over a five-year window.
This approach combines (i.e., mixes) within- and between-firm relationships, so the results are ambiguous about how between-versus within-firm relationships may differ.
<!-- para 72 -->
Wowak et al. (2015) used a sample of 386 CEOs over an eight-year window to examine the relationship between CEO compensation measures and subsequent product recalls.
The authors also used GEEs, which combine within- and between-firm variance, to test the hypotheses.
Like Ndofor et al. (2011), the results are ambiguous about how these between-versus within-firm relationships might differ.
<!-- para 73 -->
In both papers, the authors might have used the hybrid approach with either GEEs or random effects models to disentangle these potentially different relationships.
Alternatively, they might have each employed fixed effects methods, which would have isolated within-firm relationships.
This approach can only be used, however, if the theory and hypotheses focus on within-firm relationships.
Because they did not employ such methods, however, the results do not allow researchers to understand how between- versus within-firm relationships may differ.
Separating these relationships would help researchers to better understand the theoretical implications of the associated results.
As we develop in the discussion section, Allison (2014) notes some potential problems when using hybrid methods in the context of nonlinear models.
As such, the hybrid method may be more complicated in the context of Wowak (2015) et al.'s examination of product recalls.
<!-- para 75 -->
Researchers in strategic management have used several techniques to analyze panel data.
While some rely on approaches that use only within-firm variance, others rely on approaches that use both within- and between-firm variance.
In the following discussion, we reexamine Chen and Miller's (2007) examination of R&D intensity to demonstrate these approaches.
In addition, we illustrate how the hybrid approach helps to explore new theoretical relationships.
<!-- para 77 -->
**Overview.** To illustrate the potential benefits of employing the hybrid approach, we reexamine Chen and Miller's (2007), which we refer to as C&M, study of the situational and institutional determinants of R&D search intensity.
R&D intensity represents a critical strategic outcome as it reflects both future organizational growth potential (Gentry and Shen, 2013) and the ability for the firm to acquire and accumulate new knowledge (Mahoney and Pandian, 1992).
C&M provided new insights into the behavioral theory of the firm and proposed new drivers of R&D investment behavior.
In particular, their study considered how R&D decisions may depend on the situation a firm faces (i.e., whether the firm is above or below performance aspiration levels), the firm's proximity to bankruptcy, and the firm's possession of slack resources.
C&M also examine interactions of these factors; however, we focus on the main effect hypotheses in this example.
<!-- para 78 -->
**Hypotheses.** Although the authors developed several hypotheses, for illustrative purposes we focus on two, Hypotheses 2 and 3.
C&M hypothesize that firms threatened by bankruptcy may decrease R&D search intensity given the limitations of financial distress.
Thus, the authors posit a negative relationship between bankruptcy proximity and R&D search intensity.
More formally, C&M hypothesize (Hypothesis 2) that: “R&D search intensity decreases with firms’ proximity to bankruptcy” (370, emphasis in original).
<!-- para 79 -->
Complementing the focus on proximity to bankruptcy, the authors also suggest that firms with more slack resources (e.g., capacity, employees, financial resources) are more capable of carrying out search activities.
As such, the authors hypothesize a positive relationship between slack resources and R&D search intensity.
More formally, they hypothesize (Hypothesis 3) that: “R&D search intensity increases with firms’ slack resources” (370, emphasis in the original).
<!-- para 80 -->
We reproduced the specific hypotheses to underscore the lack of theoretical specificity regarding within- versus between-firm relationships.
Specifically, it is difficult to identify whether these relationships are between- or within-firm relationships.
Nevertheless, the sentence preceding Hypothesis 2, offers a between-firm theoretical logic: “We expect that firms threatened by bankruptcy will be less likely than other poor-performing firms to search for new technologies” (370).
In contrast, however, in the sentence preceding Hypothesis 3 the authors provide a within-firm logic: “We can expect a positive relation between the amount of slack resources and the firm’s search intensity” (370).
Taken together, then, these two hypotheses introduce theoretical ambiguity because one is supported using between-firm logic while the other is supported using within-firm logic.
<!-- para 81 -->
**Analytical strategy.** Finally, it is important to note C&M's (2007) analytical strategy.
Although the hypotheses lacked theoretical specificity, the authors used fixed effects models to understand the relationships of interest.
In other words, this analytical strategy allowed the authors to primarily understand within-firm relationships.
In the following sections, we illustrate how the hybrid approach helps to disentangle these different relationships.
<!-- para 83 -->
**Sample.** To obtain estimates as similar as possible to the original study, we followed C&M's original sampling approach.
Using Compustat, we focused on manufacturing firms listed in the 4-digit SIC codes between 2000 and 3999 from 1989 to 2001.
We removed industries with less than five firms in a given year to avoid biasing the industry-average R&D intensity measure.
Following the original sampling approach, we also eliminated firms with R&D intensity greater than 1.
After these two screens, our sample consisted of 38,022 firm-year observations as compared to 35,970 firm-year observations reported by C&M.
Finally, following C&M's approach, we removed extreme outliers that were more than four standard deviations above the yearly mean for our focal ratio measures.
Following this procedure, our final sample was reduced to 26,631 firm-year observations compared to 25,110 firm-year observations in C&M's final sample.
We contacted the authors about these potential differences in sample size.
They generously provided us with their Stata log file to help us better understand how they eliminated outliers.
This log allowed us to largely replicate their results.
We are grateful to the authors for their assistance.
<!-- para 84 -->
**Variables.** We follow C&M’s approach to calculate the variables of interest.
R&D search intensity, the dependent variable, is R&D expenditures divided by sales.
Performance is return on assets (ROA) with a one-year lag.
We use Altman’s (1983) Z as the indicator of distance from bankruptcy.
We calculate Altman’s Z as $(1.2 \times \text{working capital divided by total assets}) + (1.4 \times \text{retained earnings divided by total assets}) + (3.3 \times \text{income before interest expense and taxes divided by total assets}) + (0.6 \times \text{market value of equity divided by total liability}) + (1.0 \times \text{sales divided by total assets})$.
Slack is the combination of current ratio, measured as current assets divided by current liabilities, and the working capital to sales ratio.
We standardize these two variables and sum them to produce a composite measure of slack.
Industry R&D is the average R&D search intensity for each industry while excluding the focal firm, and industry growth is the percentage change in industry sales from t-1 to t.
Time fixed-effects were included in all models to control for contemporaneous effects (Certo and Semadeni, 2006).
<!-- para 85 -->
We also measure aspiration levels at the firm and industry.
Firm aspiration was measured as performance relative to past performance (t-2).
Industry aspiration was operationalized as the median performance of firms in the same 4-digit SIC industry relative to past performance (t-2).
Following C&M, we use two indicators for these aspirations.
For underperforming firms ( $I_{1}$), the indicator equals one if the firm’s past performance falls below aspirations.
Using this approach, $1-I_{1}$ indicates outperforming firms with performance exceeding the aspirations.
In effect, C&M adopt an approach that is similar to spline regression to test these two variables.

## theory
<!-- para 2 -->
Our first recommendation involves distinguishing between within- and between-firm theoretical constructs and relationships.
As we noted earlier, micro scholars have employed this practice for over a decade to examine individual-level data nested in groups and teams (Klein and Kozlowski, 2000).
We believe that adopting this practice in the context of longitudinal data might help to isolate different effects and stimulate theoretical development.
This includes using theory to develop hypotheses that clearly articulate whether the constructs of interest involve within-firm and/or between-firm relationships.
From our reexamination of C&M, this could have taken the form of specific hypotheses not only about the direction of the within-firm and between-firm effects, but also the importance of one relative to the other.
In other words, researchers could state, for example, that although both the within and between components both significantly affect the dependent variable, one is significantly higher than the other.
The value to future theory and research is that scholars can more accurately describe the theoretical dynamics of their area of research.
<!-- para 3 -->
The proper theoretical specification of these relationships is of the utmost importance to strategy scholars.
Failing to specify these relationships (or incorrect specification) can result in flawed analytical choices.
Together, these outcomes may result in ambiguous or incorrect inferences about longitudinal data.
To be sure, any such results will inhibit theoretical development.
In the worst case, inferences from such data may send scholars down the wrong theoretical path.
As Popper (1959: 108) eloquently states, “the testing of a theory depends upon basic statements whose acceptance or rejection, in its turn, depends upon our decisions.
Thus it is decisions which [sic] settle the fate of theories” (ital. in original).
<!-- para 5 -->
Second, researchers should calculate ICC(1) values to better understand the between-firm variance in their variables of interest.
Our replication of C&M illustrates the important role of ICC(1) values.
Techniques that utilize only within-firm variance become less powerful (statistically) when variables primarily have between-firm variance.
Likewise, techniques that incorporate between-firm variance become less powerful with variables with low amounts of between-firm variance.
It is not surprising, then, that the between-firm effects of the variables with low ICC(1) values (i.e., industry growth) were insignificant when utilizing the hybrid method.
The low amounts of between-firm variance in some independent variables resulted in extremely low levels of statistical power.
<!-- para 6 -->
We should add one additional point about ICC(1) values and analysis decisions.
The ICC for the dependent variable in C&M was .72, which indicates that approximately 72 percent of the variance in the dependent variable was between firms.
By using a fixed-effects model, though, the analyses only relied on less than 30 percent of the variance in the dependent variable.
A hybrid approach, though, would have allowed the authors to capitalize on both variance components.
<!-- para 8 -->
Our third recommendation involves aligning analytic techniques with the hypothesized theoretical constructs.
Researchers interested purely in within-firm effects should utilize fixed effects models or Allison’s hybrid approach.
Nevertheless, both of these techniques lack the statistical power of other approaches incorporating between-firm variance (particularly when the independent variables contain high degrees of between-group variance).
In contrast, researchers interested in between-firm effects should utilize the hybrid approach, which isolates this effect.
When using this approach, researchers should test potential differences between the between- and within-firm coefficients and comment on these differences (if they exist).
We believe that isolating these potentially different effects can improve theorizing in strategy research.
<!-- para 9 -->
If researchers do not hypothesize differences between the within- and between-firm effects, they can utilize random effects or mixed (i.e., multilevel) models as the researchers did in our examples from the RBV and agency theory.
It is important to acknowledge, however, the assumptions of these models and to test their appropriateness using a Hausman test.
Nonetheless, we recommend a hybrid approach, because researchers would be able to compare and contrast these potentially different effects.
At the very least, when using a traditional random effects model we recommend that authors should also report the results of the hybrid approach to help interpret the different types of effects.
<!-- para 10 -->
Finally, Bell and Jones (2015: 134) go so far as to state that, “there are few, if any, occasions in which FE modeling is preferable to RE modeling.” Accordingly, we encourage scholars to think carefully about the theoretical rationale underlying the use of fixed effects models.
In the worst-case scenario, there may be very weak link between fixed effects models and theoretical development.
<!-- para 12 -->
The partitioning of within- and between-firm effects allows for deeper and richer theoretical investigations without the need for additional data collection.
In our view, this presents a substantial opportunity for theoretical advancement through more careful methodological application.
It is imperative to understand that these differences could be the result of endogeneity at either level (or both levels).
<!-- para 13 -->
Revisiting our examination of C&M helps to illustrate how endogeneity may influence results.
Our findings suggest that the between-firm effect of organizational slack (i.e., the mean level of a firm’s slack over the entire sample period) on a firm’s R&D intensity greatly exceeds that of the within-firm effect of organizational slack (i.e., the within-firm changes in organizational slack each year).
The question becomes, then, does an omitted variable influence either of these variables?
<!-- para 14 -->
When considering potential endogeneity, strategy researchers must consider whether the endogeneity exists with the between-firm or within-firm (or both) variables.
Because between-firm variables do not change over time, omitted variables that remain stable over time may result in endogenous between-firm variables.
Organizational culture, which is relatively stable over time (Schein, 2010), represents one omitted variable that might create endogenous between-firm variables in the context of random effects models.
In contrast, fixed effects models (and the within-firm components of hybrid models) are susceptible to endogenous time-varying independent variables.
<!-- para 15 -->
The distinction between these different types of relationships is important, because the Hausman test used for longitudinal research tests only endogeneity at the second level.
In other words, many studies may rely on fixed effects models when it is the within-firm effects that are more biased than the between firm-firm effects.
While this is beyond the scope of this paper, future research can attempt to disentangle these complicated relationships.
<!-- para 16 -->
Baum (2006) provides a useful roadmap for scholars investigating panel data with potential endogeneity.
He suggests that the Hausman-Taylor estimators (Stata's -xthaylor-) are designed to help researchers confront situations wherein one or more independent variables are correlated with the panel-level disturbance (i.e., u).
In contrast, he proposes that researchers can employ alternative instrumental variable procedures (e.g., Stata's -xtivreg-) when one or more independent variables are correlated with the normal disturbance (i.e., e).
He indicates that such procedures can be utilized with either fixed or random effects.
Of course, the key to any such approach involves finding valid instruments, which is difficult.
<!-- para 18 -->
**Non-linear models.** Our focus in this paper was on linear models with continuous dependent variables, but a logical question involves possible extensions of the hybrid approach to other types of dependent variables.
Allison (2005) reviewed the appropriateness of the hybrid method when using binary and count variables with non-linear models.
He recently updated his recommendations with a blog post (Allison, 2014).
Simply stated, he suggests that the hybrid method is not as effective in non-linear (e.g., logistic, Poisson, and negative binomial models) as compared to linear contexts.
At the same time, however, he indicates that the hybrid method provides better results as compared to traditional mixed models with continuous variables.
He closes by suggesting that future research may provide even better ways to employ hybrid models in non-linear contexts.
<!-- para 19 -->
**Interactions.** The hybrid model also has implications for scholars interested in testing interactions.
Hybrid models allow scholars to test theoretical relationships focused on the interaction of within and between components of variables (Bell and Jones, 2015).
Once any variable is separated into its within- and between-firm components (what Preacher, Zhang, and Zyphur (in press) refer to as the “decomposed-first” strategy), those components are statistically independent and can be interacted with each other, with other non-hybrid variables, or with the within or between components of another variable.
This approach provides for theory and hypothesis testing that is both nuanced and dynamic (Subramanian et al., 2009).
<!-- para 20 -->
Revisiting the example of R&D expenditures and firm performance discussed earlier, it may be that within-firm changes in R&D expenditures have more positive effects on firm performance among firms that have lower averages levels (e.g., between-firm effect) of R&D expenditures.
In contrast, within-firm changes may not be as influential for firms that already have higher average levels of R&D expenditures.
Although this is speculative, the example demonstrates the many possibilities that result from isolating these different effects.
Schunck (2013) provides an accessible overview of testing interactions with the hybrid model.
In addition, scholars could examine potential curvilinear relationships using the between or within components.
Staying with the R&D example, a researcher might theorize that there is a curvilinear (e.g., inverted U-shaped) influence of R&D expenditures within the firm on firm performance.
They could then theorize that this curvilinear relationship is positively moderated by R&D expenditures between firms, such that the inverted U-shaped relationship is more pronounced as R&D expenditures between firms increases.
Again, we provide these examples as illustrations of the more nuanced and dynamic theorizing and hypothesis testing that can occur using the hybrid model.

## results
<!-- para 2 -->
Following the original study, we initially use fixed effects models to test the hypotheses.
For comparison purposes, we also report results from a random effects model.
We then employ the hybrid method to examine the between- and within-firm relationships for each variable.
Using this approach, we calculate the group mean for each independent and control variable.
These variables remain constant within each firm over time.
We also calculate group-mean centered scores for each variable by subtracting each variable from its group mean.
These variables vary within each firm over time.
We then included the group mean and group-mean scores for all of the variables in a random effects model.
Including both sets of coefficients in a random effects model allows us to compare the between-firm effects (i.e., the coefficients for the group means of each variable) versus the within-firm effects (i.e., the coefficients for the mean-centered independent variables, see Schunck, 2013).

The hybrid method allows researchers to implement a modified Hausman test.
Specifically, researchers can directly test whether a statistical difference exists between the set of between-firm coefficients and the set of within-firm coefficients.
In addition, however, this technique also allows researchers to test each within-versus between-firm coefficient independently.
<!-- para 24 -->
For simplicity, we limit our discussion of the individual tests to the firm aspiration model, but the results are largely similar for the industry aspiration model.
Of the six independent variables, five have statistically different within- versus between-firm effects.
The between and within-firm effects for industry average R&D search intensity ( $\chi^{2}=416.60$, p=0.000), firm underperform ( $\chi^{2}=46.14$, p=0.000), firm outperform ( $\chi^{2}=127.41$, p=0.000), distance from bankruptcy ( $\chi^{2}=8.65$, p=.003), and slack ( $\chi^{2}=17.48$, p=0.000) were significantly different from one another.
While a traditional Hausman test reveals an overall difference between the two sets of coefficients, this technique allows researchers to better understand the individual variables driving such overall differences.
<!-- para 25 -->
Finally, given our focus on longitudinal data, we implemented the hybrid approach in a random effects panel regression model (Allison, 2005; Schunck, 2013).
As noted above, researchers may employ other analytic techniques, including multilevel models and GEEs, to examine longitudinal data.
To further explore the application of the hybrid approach, we examined the group mean-centered and group mean variables in multilevel and GEE models.
We used Stata's -mixed- command for the multilevel model and Stata's -xtgee- command for GEE.
Both approaches provided parameter estimates that were similar to those reported in Table 3.
We further compared each of these models using a Hausman test, which was not significant, supporting the conclusion that these models provided parameter estimates that were not statistical different from one another.

## discussion
<!-- para 2 -->
A statistically significant Hausman test or modified Hausman test suggests that the between- and within-firm effects differ from one another in the context of R&D investments.
In strategy research, significantly different effects have led researchers to dismiss the between-firm effects as biased and inherently “wrong.” We believe that this common perception has led to the ascension of fixed effects models, which examine only within-firm variance, as the most popular panel data technique in strategy.
<!-- para 3 -->
The results of our reexamination of C&M illustrate the limitations of this approach.
C&M used fixed effects models, which leave out a great deal of information about between-firm effects.
The within-firm relationships in the hybrid models provide the same coefficients as fixed effects models, but they also allow researchers to include between-firm relationships as well.
As our results indicated, at times these effects may be substantively similar, but in other times these effects may be quite different.
<!-- para 4 -->
When these effects are different, researchers must consider the possible causes.
First, the “true” effects may differ based on whether the variance is within- or between-firms.
As mentioned earlier, other disciplines refer to this as the ecological fallacy, which occurs when between-unit relationships truly differ from within-unit relationships.
Researchers must use theory to develop such differences.
<!-- para 5 -->
Alternatively, endogeneity may account for these differences.
Although few have made the connection in the strategy literature, a Hausman test in the context of longitudinal data represents a test of endogeneity.
Traditional endogeneity involves a correlation between the independent variable(s) and $e_{ii}$ in Equation 2.
We refer to this as Level 1 endogeneity.
The Hausman test examines a correlation between the independent variables and $u_{i}$ in the same equation.
We refer to this as Level 2 endogeneity.
<!-- para 6 -->
The second potential cause, then, is an omitted variable that results in Level 2 endogeneity.
For this to occur, perhaps the most obvious cause is an omitted variable that creates a correlation between the independent variable(s) and $u_{i}$.
It is important to note, though, that $u_{i}$ remains constant over time.
Consequently, the omitted variable would have to remain constant over time as well.
We discuss this in more detail when presenting our final recommendations.
<!-- para 7 -->
Complementing the Level 2 endogeneity, the third potential explanation for the difference in coefficients is due to Level 1 endogeneity.
Importantly, the longitudinal techniques we have reviewed (e.g., random effects, fixed effects, hybrid) assume that the level-one (i.e., within-firm) effects are exogenous.
Given the pervasiveness of endogeneity in strategy research (e.g., Bascle, 2008; Hamilton and Nickerson, 2003; Semadeni, Withers, and Certo, 2014), though, this is a difficult assumption to accept.
An omitted variable, however, may create a correlation between the independent variable(s) and $e_{it}$.
Because $e_{it}$ is time varying, this omitted variable would also need to vary over time.
<!-- para 9 -->
Our reexamination of C&M highlights several key points.
First, our review of the theoretical development of the hypotheses indicates a lack of precision regarding within- versus between-firm relationships.
Second our reexamination highlights that fixed effects models account for only within-firm relationships.
Finally, our implementation of the hybrid approach illustrates how researchers might distinguish between within-firm versus between-firm relationships.
Later, we discuss the theoretical implications of uncovering such differences.
