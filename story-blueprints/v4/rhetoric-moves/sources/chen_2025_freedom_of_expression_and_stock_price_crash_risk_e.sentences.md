# chen_2025_freedom_of_expression_and_stock_price_crash_risk_e 句子库存

## introduction
<!-- para 2 -->
One controversial way powerful corporations and politicians deal with negative voices against them is to use lawsuits or threats of lawsuits to silence criticism and suppress negative news (Lidsky 2000).
Although these lawsuits deter knowingly or recklessly false statements, they can be abused to suppress legitimate criticism and meaningful public discourse, which imposes significant chilling effects.

The law and economics literature (Coase 1974; Farber 1991; Rushton 2005; Hemel 2021; Krotoszynski and Roberts 2024) has long recognized the importance of free expression for the market for ideas.
However, empirical evidence of how free expression relates to information flow in capital markets is limited.
One potential obstacle is the difficulty of measuring the freedom of expression. [^2] The enactment of state anti-SLAPP statutes offers an effective setting to overcome this obstacle.
The aim of SLAPP filers is to silence their targets by forcing them into costly and lengthy legal proceedings.
The standard judicial safeguards against otherwise meritless claims do not adequately address the chilling effects of SLAPPs (Barker 1993).
A typical anti-SLAPP statute enables the courts to dismiss SLAPPs quickly and allows the defendants to recover legal costs from the plaintiffs, thereby reducing the threat of SLAPPs and protecting the freedom of expression.
<!-- para 5 -->
As Section 2 explains in detail, the anti-SLAPP statute of the state where a firm is headquartered affects the information flow of this firm more than that of firms in other states.
This statute provides stronger protection for the general public in the state hosting the firm's headquarters; that population has a collective information advantage and pays more attention to the firm because of proximity (Coval and Moskowitz 2001; Malloy 2005; Miller and Shanthikumar 2015; Baloria and Heese 2018; Kang, Stice-Lawrence, and Wong 2021).
Thus, we can conduct a difference-in-differences (DiD) analysis that exploits the staggered enactment of these statutes across states.
<!-- para 6 -->
We hypothesize that anti-SLAPP statutes reduce the stock price crash risk through two channels.
First, the statutes mitigate the chilling effects of SLAPPs.
Thus, members of the general public (including citizens, employees, investors, and journalists) are less concerned about being targeted by SLAPPs when they publicly criticize firms or disseminate bad news about them.
As a result, more bad news is revealed, and the manager has a reduced ability to hide bad news.
We call this the ability channel.
Second, the manager is perceived as actively hiding bad news and incurs a cost (for example, litigation or dismissal) when the bad news is revealed by a third party (Frenkel, Guttman, and Kremer 2020).
Anti-SLAPP statutes raise this cost by increasing the chance that a third party will reveal the withheld bad news.
As a result, the manager's incentive to hide bad news declines in the first place.
We call this the incentive channel.
Nevertheless, anti-SLAPP statutes may encourage media slants and spread rumors or misinformation, which reduces market efficiency and increases crash risk (Jia et al. 2020).
Therefore, how anti-SLAPP statutes affect crash risk is ultimately an empirical question.
<!-- para 7 -->
We adopt a stacked event-study approach to estimate a DiD regression in which each treated unit is matched to clean (that is, not-yet-treated) controls, and separate fixed effects are included for each set (that is, cohort) of treated units and its controls (Cengiz et al. 2019).
This approach alleviates concerns about biased estimates in the presence of heterogeneous treatment effects in a staggered treatment setting (Roth et al. 2023).
Our final regression sample includes 215,359 cohort-firm-year observations from 7,380 unique US firms between 1988 and 2022.
Following Chen, Hong, and Stein (2001), we measure the firm-specific crash risk using two proxies: negative conditional skewness (NCSkew) and down-to-up volatility (DUVol).
We find that enacting anti-SLAPP statutes in the state of the firm's headquarters reduces the firm's crash risk.
This effect is statistically and economically significant.
Our baseline results show that enacting anti-SLAPP statutes reduces NCSkew (DUVol) by 3.9 percent (4.1 percent) of the sample standard deviation.
We conduct a dynamic analysis and find that the decrease in crash risk occurs only after the enactment year, which supports the parallel-trends assumption (Roberts and Whited 2013).
The decrease in crash risk is significant 4 years after the enactment year, which suggests that anti-SLAPP statutes have a long-lasting effect.
<!-- para 8 -->
We conduct several cross-sectional analyses to highlight the ability and incentive channels.
Anti-SLAPP statutes should more effectively reduce the manager's ability to hide bad news when the protected local general public is more informed and the discovered bad news can be disseminated more widely.
The local public's information advantage is likely greater for firms with more uncertain operations or in smaller cities and remote areas (Coval and Moskowitz 1999; Malloy 2005; Callen and Fang 2015).
Discovered bad news is more likely to be widely disseminated in counties with stronger social ties and for firms receiving higher media attention (Fang and Peress 2009; Heese, Pérez-Cavazos, and Peter 2022; Kang, Stice-Lawrence, and Wong 2021).
Indeed, we find that the effect of anti-SLAPP statutes is stronger for these firms.
<!-- para 9 -->
Anti-SLAPP statutes increase the manager’s cost of hiding bad news by increasing the chance that investors believe he is actively withholding the news (that is, the withheld bad news is revealed by a third party).
The increase in this cost is larger when this belief is more likely to trigger shareholders' litigation or a dismissal, that is, when the manager faces a higher ex ante risk of shareholders' litigation and a dismissal.
We follow Huang, Hui, and Li (2019) to measure ex ante shareholders' litigation risk.
We inversely measure ex ante dismissal risk for the chief executive officer (CEO) by CEO connectedness (Khanna, Kim, and Lu 2015).
A CEO has less support to retain his job when he is less connected to his top lieutenants and board members.
We find a stronger effect of anti-SLAPP statutes for firms with higher shareholders' litigation risk and lower CEO connectedness.
<!-- para 10 -->
Next, we examine how the overall tone of articles and comments changes after the enactment of anti-SLAPP statutes, and we document three findings.
First, we find a significant increase in negative newspaper articles covered in the RavenPack News Analytics database.
Second, the enactment increases the negative tone of reviews posted by employees in the “Cons” section (that is, unfavorable reviews) on the Glassdoor employment website.
We do not find changes in the tone of reviews in the “Pros” section, as the enactment is unlikely to affect employees’ decision to post favorable reviews.
Third, the enactment increases the negative tone of articles and comments on Seeking Alpha, an investment-related social media site.
Furthermore, the increased negativity of RavenPack newspaper articles and Seeking Alpha articles and comments is concentrated on firms with poorer future performance, which suggests that the increased negative tone conveys more information and is likely to be welfare improving.
Overall, the evidence indicates that anti-SLAPP statutes increase the dissemination of negative news and reduce managers’ ability to hide bad news.
<!-- para 11 -->
We also examine how anti-SLAPP statutes affect managers' incentives to hide bad news by manipulating earnings and distorting investment (Kedia and Philippone 2009; Benmelech, Kandel, and Veronesi 2010).
Prior studies also show that poor financial reporting and suboptimal investment are important drivers of crash risk (Hutton, Marcus, and Tehranian 2009; Khurana, Pereira, and Zhang 2018).
Following Fang, Huang, and Karpoff (2016), we measure earnings manipulation using three proxies: performance-adjusted discretionary accruals (Kothari, Leone, and Wasley 2005), an indicator of marginally meeting or beating analysts' earnings forecasts (Jiang, Petroni, and Wang 2010), and the F-score that measures the likelihood of accounting-related fraud (Dechow et al. 2011).
We find a significant decrease in earnings management after the enactment of anti-SLAPP statutes.
We measure the distortion of investment by its deviation from its expected value (Biddle, Hilary, and Verdi 2009; McNichols and Stubben 2008).
We find a significant decrease in firms' propensity to overinvest after the enactment, though the change in the propensity to underinvest is nonsignificant.
<!-- para 12 -->
Finally, SLAPPs mostly target the dissemination of bad news rather than good news.
Thus, anti-SLAPP statutes should mostly affect a manager's withholding of bad news rather than good news.
Consistent with this notion, we find that these statutes decrease the frequency of extreme negative price changes (that is, crash events) and do not affect the frequency of extreme positive price changes (that is, jump events).
This evidence also mitigates the concerns that our main results are driven by confounding factors that affect overall information flow in capital markets.
<!-- para 13 -->
This paper makes several important contributions to the literature.
First, to the best of our knowledge, our paper is the first to provide large-sample causal evidence on the consequences of freedom of expression for capital markets.
Free expression is perceived as a fundamental constitutional right, and its potential economic consequences are intensively discussed in the law and economics literature (Coase 1974; Schauer 1978; Farber 1991; Holderness, Jensen, and Meckling 2000; Rushton 2005; Cianciaruso, Guttman, and Marinovic 2023).
Recent misinformation about the pandemic, climate change, and elections have also reignited the debate on speech law reforms (New York Times 2020; Lewin 2021).
However, we have limited empirical evidence of the economic consequences of freedom of expression.
Our study suggests that legal protection for free expression limits managers' abilities and incentives to hide bad news and reduces firms' crash risk.
Crash risk plays an important role in investors' welfare and asset pricing (Bégin, Dorion, and Gauthier 2020).
The threat that SLAPPs pose to free expression has received increasing attention from US and European legislators (Jones 2019; Bárd et al. 2020; Borg-Barthet, Lobina, and Zabrocka 2021; Prather 2023).
Thus, our evidence has important policy implications for legislators who are considering adopting or strengthening their anti-SLAPP laws.
<!-- para 14 -->
Second, we contribute to the literature on crash risk (Jin and Myers 2006).
Mechanisms that reduce information asymmetry and constrain a manager's ability to hide bad news, such as regulation of financial reporting, analysts, and government enforcement, help decrease crash risk (DeFond et al. 2015; Kim, Lu, and Yu 2019; Bauer, Fang, and Pittman 2021).
Crash risk increases with the manager's private benefits from hoarding bad news (Kim, Li, and Zhang 2011; Ali, Li, and Zhang 2019).
We show that legal protection of the general public's free expression reduces a manager's ability and incentive to hide bad news.
As freedom of expression is an important dimension of the social environment, we also extend the research on the consequences of changes in capital markets for the social environment by providing causal evidence (Callen and Fang 2015).
<!-- para 15 -->
Third, our paper relates to the literature on media’s monitoring roles (Miller and Skinner 2015).
The monitoring function of the media rests on the free expression of both the media and news sources.
Most studies along this line of research (for example, Dai, Parwada, and Zhang 2015) focus on ex post coverage and news dissemination by the media.
Several recent papers examine the effect of closures of certain types of media, such as local newspapers (An et al. 2020; Heese, Pérez-Cavazos, and Peter 2022), social media (Heese and Pacelli 2023), and Internet searching (Xu, Xuan, and Zheng 2021).
By contrast, we focus on the legal protection of free expression, which affects the ex ante incentives of all types of media and their news sources.
<!-- para 18 -->
A SLAPP is characterized as a meritless lawsuit designed to discourage people from exercising their constitutional rights of free speech and petitioning the government (Pring and Canan 1996).
Early SLAPPs were filed mainly to counteract the burgeoning environmental movement in the 1970s (Pring and Canan 1996).
Later, SLAPPs were developed to cover various scenarios, such as estate developers suing homeowners for petitioning against their development projects; public servants suing citizens for criticizing their conduct; corporate interests suing consumers or employees for whistleblowing about illegal practices; environmental organizations for challenging polluting behavior; and investors, short sellers, and media outlets for publishing negative comments (Tate 2000; Hartzler 2007; Lamont 2012; Brendel and Ryans 2021). [^3] Typical SLAPP claims include defamation, libel, invasion of privacy, abuse of process, conspiracy, and tortious interference with contract or business relationships (Pring and Canan 1996; Hartzler 2007).
<!-- para 19 -->
The motive of SLAPP plaintiffs is not necessarily to win their lawsuits but rather to harass, deter, punish, and ultimately silence the defendants (Barker 1993; Tate 2000).
The strategy entails forcing the defendants into a long, all-consuming legal procedure and wasting their resources.
A targeted party may spend months or even years defending against the SLAPP and incur a large amount of legal fees even if the defendant ultimately wins the suit. [^4] Thus, SLAPPs have significant chilling effects.
For those who lack financial resources and emotional stamina, the time and resources required to defend SLAPPs are often more than enough to silence them (Hartzler 2007). [^5] Anticipating the high costs, SLAPP targets may choose to keep silent when they receive a lawsuit threat, meaning that filing a suit may not be necessary.
Moreover, the chilling effects of SLAPPs are not limited to the suits’ targets: Those who witness SLAPPs are also chilled.
In a series of nationwide surveys, Pring and Canan (1996; Canan and Pring 1988) find that SLAPP targets and witnesses have similar responses when a hypothetical public dispute is presented to them. [^6] New York trial judge Nicholas Colabella (Gordon v.
Marrone, 590 N.Y.S.2d 649, 656 [Sup.
Ct. 1992]) points out that “[t]he ripple effect of such suits in our society is enormous.
Persons who have been outspoken on issues of public importance targeted in such suits or who have witnessed such suits will often choose in the future to stay silent.” Because of their widespread chilling effects, SLAPPs are perceived as a serious threat to the freedom of expression (Bárd et al. 2020; Borg-Barthet, Lobina, and Zabrocka 2021; UK Ministry of Justice 2022).
<!-- para 21 -->
Since SLAPP plaintiffs do not have to win the suits to silence the targets, the usual judicial safeguards that prevent meritless claims from prevailing are inadequate to address the chilling effects (Barker 1993).
In the late 1980s, many states began to address the increasing problems of certain suits by adopting anti-SLAPP statutes.
By 2022, 32 states (including the District of Columbia) had approved and enacted anti-SLAPP statutes (see Table B1 for the statutes and enactment years), though there is no federal anti-SLAPP law. [^7]
<!-- para 22 -->
Typically, the anti-SLAPP statutes enable the defending party to file a special motion to strike the SLAPP (Pring and Canan 1996; Burke 2020).
The SLAPP plaintiff then must establish evidence that there is a probability that they will prevail in the case.
Otherwise, the court will dismiss the case or reach a summary judgment (that is, the defendant wins).
The higher standard for the plaintiff to establish evidence makes it more difficult for them to continue the lawsuit and easier for the defendants to defend.
As a result, the uncertainty and the time and resources required for the defendants to defend in the courts are reduced.
Many anti-SLAPP statutes also allow the prevailing defendant to recover attorneys' fees from the SLAPP plaintiff.
This reduces the defendant's financial burden and makes it easier for a financially weak defendant to find an attorney for representation, especially when the plaintiff's claims are meritless or frivolous.
Overall, anti-SLAPP statutes reduce costs and aim to alleviate the chilling effects for SLAPP targets as well as for witnesses, who base their perception of the legal consequences of speaking out on their observation of SLAPP targets' experiences.

Anecdotes and survey evidence suggest that anti-SLAPP statutes do mitigate the chilling effects of SLAPPs. [^8] To evaluate California’s anti-SLAPP statute, the

In 16 written responses, the attorneys for both SLAPP filers and their targets rated the statute effective (Tate 2000).
Marty Singer, a prominent Los Angeles litigator, has been quoted as saying that California's statute is a "sort of deterrent" to filing defamation suits on behalf of celebrities, and that "instead of filing three to five suits a year, I think I would file 50 a year, if I didn't tell the clients how expensive it would be" (US House 2016, p. 6).
<!-- para 26 -->
Most SLAPPs involve defamation claims or other similar tort claims brought under state law (Pring and Canan 1996; Tate 2000).
Suits involving only state law claims are usually filed in state court and can be filed in federal court only if the federal court has “diversity jurisdiction,” meaning that all plaintiffs are in different states than all defendants and the claimed damage exceeds a threshold (28 U.S. Code sec. 1332).
<!-- para 27 -->
When a SLAPP is brought in state court, the anti-SLAPP statute of the state in which the court sits (that is, the forum state) generally applies. [^9] The jurisdiction rules require the forum state court to have personal jurisdiction over the defendant, which requires minimum contacts between the defendant and the forum state (Malloy 1985).
The defendant's domicile state has general personal jurisdiction as it has continuous and systematic contacts with the defendant.
The plaintiff's resident state may have specific personal jurisdiction if the defendant has purposefully directed his activities at the residents of the plaintiff's state.
If the plaintiff and defendant are in the same state, this state is most likely to be the forum state since both the general and the specific personal jurisdictions point to this state, and the anti-SLAPP statute of this state applies.
If the plaintiff and defendant are in different states, it is less certain which state will be the forum state since the general and specific jurisdictions may point to different states.
As a result, the applicability of the anti-SLAPP statute of either state is less certain.

State anti-SLAPP statutes rely on procedural mechanisms to protect substantive rights and pose a quandary for federal courts hearing state law claims (Quinlan 2014).

To summarize, the above discussion suggests that the anti-SLAPP statute of a state is more likely to apply when both the plaintiff and the defendant are in that state.
When they are in different states, the choice of law analysis generally favors the anti-SLAPP law of the plaintiff's state, though with higher uncertainty.
<!-- para 33 -->
We match a firm to the anti-SLAPP statute of its headquarters' state for two reasons.
First, as discussed above, the anti-SLAPP statute of a state is more likely to apply when both the plaintiff and the defendant reside in that state.
Thus, protection from the anti-SLAPP statute of a state is more certain when the general public in that state criticizes a firm headquartered in the same state than when it targets firms headquartered in other states.
The ability of anti-SLAPP statutes to mitigate chilling effects depends on the certainty of protection (Quinlan 2014).
As a result, a state's anti-SLAPP statute mostly alleviates the local general public's concerns about criticizing and disseminating bad news about local firms.
In addition, the chilling effects of SLAPPs ripple to witnesses.
Sociopsychology studies show that people are more sensitive to adverse events that occur closer to them and may overgeneralize the feeling of fear to unrelated areas (Bandura 1971; Tversky and Kahneman 1974).
The ripple effects of local SLAPPs are greater on local people who closely witness these SLAPPs and, as a result, overgeneralize the feeling of fear when they decide whether to reveal bad news about an unrelated firm.
Anti-SLAPP statutes also alleviate the chilling effects of these local SLAPPs on local people.
<!-- para 34 -->
Second, corporate headquarters are where corporate decision-makers reside and are the centers of information exchange between a firm and its suppliers and investors (Davis and Henderson 2008).
The literature suggests that third parties have value-relevant information not incorporated in stock prices. [^13] Third parties residing geographically close to corporate headquarters have an information advantage relative to average investors.
For example, many studies demonstrate the information advantage of investors and analysts close to a firm's headquarters (Coval and Moskowitz 2001; Malloy 2005; Huang et al. 2024).
Huang, Li, and Markov (2020) show that the power to predict a firm's future performance is stronger for opinions of employees at headquarters than for other employees.
Geographic proximity also increases media attention.
Miller and Shanthikumar (2015) show that regional newspapers cover nearby firms more often and with longer articles than national newspapers.
Baloria and Heese (2018) find that local branches of national media are more likely to cover nearby firms.
<!-- para 35 -->
Overall, the anti-SLAPP statute of a state mainly alleviates the local general public’s concerns about revealing bad news about local firms.
Since the local general public also has a collective information advantage and pays more attention to local firms, the statute is more likely to affect the hoarding of bad news by local firms than by firms in other states.
This differential effect allows a DiD analysis exploiting the staggered enactment of state anti-SLAPP statutes.

## theory
<!-- para 2 -->
The literature suggests that information asymmetry and managers' hoarding of bad news can lead to a stock price crash (Jin and Myers 2006).
Managers gain private benefits from hiding bad news and hope it will be offset by future good news (Kothari, Shu, and Wysocki 2009).
The private benefits arise from career concerns, compensation contracts, empire building, and maintaining esteem, among other considerations (Ball 2009; Kim, Li, and Zhang 2011; Ali, Li, and Zhang 2019).
The cost of hiding bad news increases as the bad news accumulates.
Above a threshold, this cost exceeds the private benefits, and the managers release the accumulated bad news, which results in a stock price crash. [^14]
<!-- para 3 -->
Frenkel, Guttman, and Kremer (2020) analyze how price efficiency is affected by disclosure by a third party, which can be any mechanism that induces the public supply of the firm's information.
In their model, information comes from the disclosure by the manager and a third party.
The third party is not incentivized to withhold bad news, so her disclosure increases bad news incorporated into stock prices.
Her disclosure may also discipline the manager's decision to hide bad news.
The manager bears a cost when the withheld bad news is revealed by the third party because he will be perceived as actively hiding the bad news.
To reduce this cost, the manager increases “precautionary disclosure” (that is, hides bad news less often; Frenkel, Guttman, and Kremer [2020], p. 178).
As a result, price efficiency further improves.
<!-- para 4 -->
In our setting, the amount of bad news that a manager can hide depends on the actions they take to hoard bad news and the amount of bad news revealed by the general public (that is, a third party such as a citizen, employee, analyst, investor, or journalist).
We hypothesize that anti-SLAPP statutes reduce crash risk by increasing the amount of bad news revealed by the general public and disciplining the manager's actions.
<!-- para 5 -->
First, protection from anti-SLAPP statutes reduces the local general public’s concerns of being targeted by SLAPPs for publicly criticizing local firms or revealing bad news or misbehavior of these firms to the media. [^15] The protection also reduces the media’s concerns when disseminating or further investigating bad news (Miller 2006; Dyck, Volchkova, and Zingales 2008; Dyck, Morse, and
<!-- para 6 -->
Zingales 2010; Heese and Pacelli 2023). [^16] As discussed in Section 2.4, the local general public also has a collective information advantage and pays more attention to local firms.
Thus, more bad news can be discovered, disseminated, and eventually publicly revealed by a third party.
As a result, it is more difficult for the manager to hide bad news.
We refer to this as the ability channel.
<!-- para 7 -->
Second, anti-SLAPP statutes may discipline the manager's action to hide bad news.
Actively withholding unfavorable information can be perceived as wrongdoing by investors and trigger litigation and dismissal (Bao, Kim, and Su 2022; Khanna, Kim, and Lu 2015).
As a result, the manager incurs reputation, litigation, and career costs, which increase with the investors' belief that the manager is actively hiding bad news.
This belief is stronger when a third party publicly reveals bad news and the manager does not disclose it.
Anti-SLAPP statutes raise the likelihood that the withheld bad news is revealed by a third party and increase the manager's cost of withholding bad news.
To reduce this cost, the manager may take less aggressive actions to hide bad news (Frenkel, Guttman, and Kremer 2020).
We refer to this channel as the incentive channel.
The above discussion leads to the following hypothesis.
<!-- para 8 -->
Hypothesis 1.
The anti-SLAPP statute of a state reduces the crash risk of firms headquartered in that state.
<!-- para 9 -->
Several forces may make anti-SLAPP statutes ineffective and add tension to hypothesis 1.
For example, the statutes may increase media sensationalism and slant, which promotes the spread of rumors and disinformation (Ahern and Sosyura 2015; Baloria and Heese 2018; Jia et al. 2020).
In addition, the lack of federal anti-SLAPP statutes may allow plaintiffs to circumvent state statutes (Horwitz 2020). [^17] Thus, the effect of state anti-SLAPP statutes on crash risk is ultimately an empirical question.
<!-- para 10 -->
The ability and incentive channels have several cross-sectional predictions.
Anti-SLAPP statutes reduce the local general public’s concerns about revealing negative news about local firms.
The manager’s ability to hide bad news should be reduced to a greater extent when the negative news revealed by the public is more informative and more widely disseminated.
Thus, the statutes’ effect on a manager’s ability to hide bad news should be stronger when the local general public has a greater collective information advantage over average investors and when the bad news discovered by the local general public is more likely to be widely disseminated.
The above discussion leads to hypothesis 2a.
<!-- para 11 -->
Hypothesis 2a.
The effect of anti-SLAPP statutes on reducing crash risk is more pronounced when the local general public has a greater information advantage relative to average investors and when the discovered bad news is more likely to be widely disseminated.
<!-- para 12 -->
The analysis in Frenkel, Guttman, and Kremer (2020) suggests that the disciplinary effect of the third party’s disclosure increases with the cost that the manager bears when he withholds bad news that is revealed by the third party.
Without this cost, the third party’s disclosure may induce the manager to hide bad news more aggressively. [^18] Thus, we expect that the effect of anti-SLAPP statutes through the incentive channel increases with this cost.
Two important components of this cost are shareholders’ litigation and dismissal (Bao, Kim, and Su 2022; Khanna, Kim, and Lu 2015).
Thus, if anti-SLAPP statutes reduce crash risk through the incentive channel, the effect should be stronger when the manager faces higher ex ante risks of shareholders’ litigation and dismissal.
The above discussion leads to hypothesis 2b.
<!-- para 13 -->
Hypothesis 2b.
The effect of anti-SLAPP statutes on reducing crash risk is more pronounced when the manager faces a higher ex ante risk of shareholders' litigation and dismissal.
<!-- para 14 -->
The ability and incentive channels also predict changes in tone in the media and a firm's decisions.
Anti-SLAPP statutes reduce the manager's ability to withhold bad news by increasing the discovery and dissemination of bad news about the firm.
As a result, we expect that articles and comments about the firm in the media have a more negative tone.
Furthermore, if these articles and comments reveal value-relevant information and improve capital market efficiency, they should target firms with poorer future performance since the managers of these firms are likely to have more bad news to hide.
The above discussion leads to hypothesis 3.
<!-- para 15 -->
Hypothesis 3.
The anti-SLAPP statute of a state increases negative media tones about firms headquartered in that state, especially when the firms have poorer future performance.
<!-- para 16 -->
Finally, if anti-SLAPP statutes reduce crash risk through the incentive channel, they should reduce aggressive hoarding of bad news by the manager.
The literature suggests that earnings management and investment distortion are important ways to hide bad news.
Kedia and Philippon (2009) suggest that managers who want to hide bad news (about low productivity) must manage earnings and overinvest to mimic high-productivity firms.
Benmelech, Kandel, and Veronesi (2010) show that managers who want to hide bad news (about growth opportunities) must distort investment policies to mimic firms with good growth opportunities.
This is because the requirements of signaling compel managers to act in a consistent manner (Kedia and Philippon 2009).
These suboptimal real decisions exacerbate crash risk by destroying firm value and inflating overvaluation.
The existing evidence also shows that poor financial reporting and distorted investment are important drivers of crash risk (Jin and Myers 2006; Hutton, Marcus, and Tehranian 2009; DeFond et al. 2015; Khurana, Pereira, and Zhang 2018).
The above discussion leads to hypothesis 4.
<!-- para 17 -->
Hypothesis 4.
The anti-SLAPP statute of a state reduces the tendency of firms headquartered in that state to manipulate earnings and distort investment.

## methods
<!-- para 3 -->
Our sample selection begins with all US firms covered in the Compustat/Center for Research in Security Prices (CRSP) merged database between 1988 and 2022.
We rely on the states of firms' historical headquarters to match the firms and the status of anti-SLAPP statutes.
We first identify the historical headquarters state of each firm using the 10-K header file, which covers observations between 1993 and 2021, and the Compustat database, which provides the data for the headquarters' state in 2022. [^19] If these historical headquarters' state data are missing, we fill the data by combining the data compiled by Heider and Ljungqvist (2015) and Bai, Fairhurst, and Serfling (2000) with the company header history file in the legacy Compustat/CRSP merged database (Bena, Ortiz-Molina, and Simintzi 2022).
Following the prior literature (for example, Li and Zhan 2019), we remove observations for financial institutions (Standard Industrial Classification [SIC] codes 6000–6999), firms in the utility industries (SIC codes 4000–4999), and those with stock prices less than 1.
After removing observations missing the measure of crash risk and control variables (explained below), the sample selection procedures lead to a sample of 91,294 firm-year observations from 9,895 unique firms between 1988 and 2022.
<!-- para 4 -->
Recent advances in econometrics suggest that the conventional two-way fixed-effects DiD design may result in a biased estimate of the treatment effect when the effect is heterogeneous (Roth et al. 2023).
The biased estimate comes from using the early treated groups as the control for the late-treated groups.
To alleviate this concern, we use the stacked event-study approach (Cengiz et al. 2019) and rely on only the not-yet-treated groups as the control sample.
Specifically, we construct a cohort for each year c in which at least one state enacted an anti-SLAPP
<!-- para 5 -->
statute.
The treatment sample of this cohort includes the observations in year  $[c - 4, c + 4]$ of the firms headquartered in the states that enacted anti-SLAPP statutes in year  $c$.
The control sample of this cohort includes the observations in year  $[c - 4, c + 4]$ of all firms headquartered in the states that had not enacted an anti-SLAPP statute by year  $c + 4$. [^20] We remove the firms that change their headquarters’ states during  $[c - 4, c + 4]$ to alleviate the concern that the anti-SLAPP statutes affect the firms’ choice of states for their headquarters.
Nevertheless, our inference is not sensitive to this research design choice.
We also require each cohort to have observations before and after the enactment year (that is, year  $c$).
We obtain the final regression sample for the main analysis after pooling the observations of all cohorts, which contains 215,359 cohort-firm-year observations from 7,380 unique firms between 1988 and 2022.
<!-- para 7 -->
We estimate the following DiD regression using the stacked cohort sample constructed above to test the effect of the anti-SLAPP statutes on crash risk:
<!-- para 8 -->
$$ \mathrm{Crash}_{i,t}=\alpha_{i,c}+\alpha_{c,t}+\beta\times\mathrm{Anti-SLAPP}_{i,t}+\mathrm{Controls}+\varepsilon_{i,c,t}, $$
<!-- para 9 -->
where i, c, and t refer to firm, cohort, and year, respectively;  $\alpha_{i,c}$ and  $\alpha_{c,t}$ represent the high-dimensional firm-cohort and year-cohort fixed effects; and Anti-SLAPP $_{i,t}$ is an indicator variable that equals one if firm i's headquarters' state has enacted an anti-SLAPP statute by year t and zero otherwise.
Following Chen, Hong, and Stein (2001), we use two variables to measure crash risk (Crash).
The first is the negative conditional skewness (NCSkew) of firm-specific weekly returns.
We define firm-specific weekly returns as the residual terms from the firm-specific regressions of the expanded market model.
The value for NCSkew is defined for each firm and year as the negative of the third moment of firm-specific weekly returns during the year divided by the standard deviation of firm-specific weekly returns raised to the third power.
The second measure is the down-to-up volatility (DUVol).
For each firm and year, we separate all the weeks with firm-specific weekly returns below the annual mean (down weeks) from those with firm-specific returns above the annual mean (up weeks).
The value for DUVol is computed as the natural logarithm of the ratio of the standard deviation of the firm-specific returns on the down weeks to the standard division on the up weeks.
Detailed definitions of NCSkew and DUVol are provided in Appendix A.
<!-- para 10 -->
Hypothesis 1 predicts the coefficient on Anti-SLAPP ( $\beta$) to be negative.
We interpret a negative coefficient estimate on Anti-SLAPP as evidence that small pieces of bad news are more difficult to accumulate so that the likelihood of revealing a large amount of bad news that had been previously concealed decreases
<!-- para 11 -->
(Jin and Myers 2006; Kim, Li, and Zhang 2011; Xu, Xuan, and Zheng 2021; Hsu, Wang, and Whipple 2022). [^21]
<!-- para 12 -->
Following Chen, Hong, and Stein (2001), we control for the following determinants of crash risk: buy and hold abnormal returns (Ret), volatility of firm-specific returns (Sigma), trading volume (DTurn), firm size (Size), market-to-book ratio (MTB), leverage (Lev), and return on assets (ROA).
All the control variables are lagged by 1 year.
Since we control for the firm-cohort fixed effects, the control variables do not include a lagged NCSkew term.
Controlling for the firm fixed effects and lagged dependent variable simultaneously may lead to biased coefficient estimates (Angrist and Pischke 2009).
Nevertheless, none of our results qualitatively change when we control for the lagged NCSkew term.
We cluster standard errors by the headquarters' state to account for arbitrary correlation of firms headquartered in the same state.

Hypothesis 2a predicts that anti-SLAPP statutes are likely to reduce managers’ ability to hide bad news to a greater extent when the local general public has a higher collective information advantage over average investors and the discovered bad news can be disseminated more widely.
To test hypothesis 2a, we construct two proxies for the local information advantage and two for the extent to which the discovered news can be widely disseminated.
We measure these proxies before the enactment year to mitigate the concern that they are endogenously affected by the anti-SLAPP statutes.
Detailed definitions are provided in Appendix A.
We then augment the baseline model (that is, regression [1]) with the interaction between these proxies and Anti-SLAPP. [^24] We remove the observations with missing values for the proxies.
<!-- para 32 -->
The first proxy of local information advantage is an indicator for whether the firm’s headquarters are in a nonmetropolitan area (that is, a small city or a remote area).
The literature suggests that the relative information advantage of local third parties (for example, investors and analysts) is greater for firms located in nonmetropolitan areas (Coval and Moskowitz 1999; Malloy 2005).
Following Malloy (2005), we define the firm as located in a metropolitan area (Nonmetropolitan = 0) if the firm’s headquarters are in one of the 20 most populous cities; otherwise, we define the firm as located in a small city or remote area (Nonmetropolitan = 1).
The inference is qualitatively similar if we use the 10 most populated cities to define Nonmetropolitan.
<!-- para 33 -->
The second proxy is earnings volatility.
The local information advantage is likely greater for firms with higher information asymmetry, which increases with the uncertainty of fundamental value.
We follow Callen and Fang (2015) and use earnings volatility to measure the uncertainty.
In each cohort, we sort firms into two groups according to their values for Earnings Volatility.

Hypothesis 3 predicts that anti-SLAPP statutes increase negative coverage of the firms in news media, especially when these firms have poorer future performance and managers have more bad news to hide.
To test hypothesis 3, we examine the tone in three media settings: the newspaper articles covered by the RavenPack News Analytics database, employees' reviews of their employers on Glassdoor, and investors' articles and comments on Seeking Alpha.
We choose to examine these three media settings as prior literature shows that articles, opinions, ratings, reviews, and comments provide meaningful information to the capital market and discipline managers (Dai, Parwada, and Zhang 2015; Green et al. 2019; Huang, Li, and Markov 2020; Dube and Zhu 2021; Campbell and Shang 2022; Chen et al. 2014; Dyer and Kim 2021; Farrell et al. 2022).
In addition, media outlets are common targets of SLAPPs and, in turn, routinely use anti-SLAPP statutes to defend themselves (Burke 2020). [^26]
<!-- para 52 -->
We also adopt the stacked event-study approach to test hypothesis 3.
For the articles and comments published in year t, we measure the future firm performance by the change of ROA in year t (that is,  $\Delta ROA_{i,t} = ROA_{i,t} - ROA_{i,t-1}$), which is usually announced in year  $t + 1$.
In each cohort and year, we sort firms into the high and low  $\Delta ROA$ groups according to the median value of  $\Delta ROA$.
Hypothesis 3 also predicts the increase in negative coverage to be more pronounced for firms with low values for  $\Delta ROA$.

The inference is qualitatively similar when we use the event sentiment score, which is based on an ex ante assessment by experts with extensive experience and backgrounds in linguistics, finance, and economics of how news articles affect stock prices.
We construct News_Sentiment $_{i,t}$ as the mean value of the standardized CSS of all articles published in year t about firm i to measure the average tone of news articles about the firm.
A higher value of News_Sentiment means a more positive tone of the newspaper articles.

Second, we retrieve the data on employees' reviews on Glassdoor, one of the most developed and popular websites to provide opportunities for employees to rate and comment on employers.
Employees usually post favorable reviews in the "Pros" section and unfavorable reviews in the "Cons" section.
For each review, we use the pretrained DistilBERT model (Sanh et al. 2020; Hilary, Tian, and Yu 2023) to estimate the probability of the review having positive and negative sentiment.
We measure the tone by the difference between positive and negative sentiment probabilities.
For each firm and year, we calculate the weighted average of the tone score of all reviews in the "Pros" section (Tones_Pros) and those in the "Cons" section (Tones_Cons).
We use the review length as the weight since Green et al. (2019) show that lengthier reviews are more informative.
Detailed definitions of Tone_Pros and Tone_Cons are provided in Appendix A.
A higher value of Tone_Pros (Tones_Cons) means that the reviews in the "Pros" ("Cons") section are more favorable (less unfavorable). [^27]
<!-- para 64 -->
We then examine the impact of anti-SLAPP statutes on Tones_Pros and Tones_Cons.
Since SLAPPs are likely to target unfavorable rather than favorable reviews, we expect that the anti-SLAPP statutes decrease values for Tones_Cons and do not affect values for Tones_Pros.
We report the results in columns 3–6 of Table 6.
The sample size is smaller because data from Glassdoor are not available before 2008.
Column 3 reports a significantly negative coefficient on Anti-SLAPP ( $-.032$,  $t = -2.42$).
Nevertheless, the coefficient on Anti-SLAPP  $\times$ Low  $\Delta$ROA in column 4 is nonsignificant (.010,  $t = 1.13$). [^28] Consistent with the notion that the anti-SLAPP statutes do not affect the employees' decision to post favorable comments, the coefficient on Anti-SLAPP ( $-.002$,  $t = -0.17$) in column 5 is nonsignificant.
The coefficient on Anti-SLAPP  $\times$ Low  $\Delta$ROA ( $-.011$,  $t = -0.75$) in column 6 is also nonsignificant. [^29]

Third, we consider the articles and comments posted on Seeking Alpha, one of the biggest investment-related social media sites in the United States, which receives significant attention from investors.
We measure the tone of an article (SA_Article_Tones) and a comment (SA_Comment_Tones) as the number of positive words minus negative words, as classified by Loughran and McDonald (2011), scaled by the total number of words (excluding stop words).
A higher value of SA_Article_Tones or SA_Comment_Tones means a more positive tone.
<!-- para 67 -->
We test the effects of anti-SLAPP statutes on SA_Article_Tones and SA_Comment_Tones and report the results in columns 7–10 of Table 6.
Again, the sample size is smaller than that of the main test because Seeking Alpha was established in 2004.
Column 7 reports a significantly negative coefficient on Anti-SLAPP (−.002, t = −2.29), and column 8 shows a significantly negative coefficient on Anti-SLAPP × Low ΔROA (−.002, t = −2.14).
For SA_Comment_Tones, the coefficients on Anti-SLAPP (−.004, t = −2.64) and Anti-SLAPP × Low ΔROA (−.002, t = −2.32) are significantly negative.
<!-- para 68 -->
Overall, the results presented in Table 6 are generally consistent with hypothesis 3: anti-SLAPP statutes reduce the general public's concerns about criticizing firms and disseminating negative news.
If the negative news is incorporated into stock prices earlier and more smoothly, the managers' ability to hide bad news is lower, and crash risk is reduced.
This evidence supports the hypothesis that anti-SLAPP statutes reduce crash risk through the ability channel.

Hypothesis 4 predicts that if anti-SLAPP statutes reduce crash risk through the incentive channel, they should also reduce managers' actions to hoard bad news, such as earnings management and investment distortion.
In this section, we adopt the same stacked event-study approach to test hypothesis 4.

If anti-SLAPP statutes reduce hoarding of bad news, the bad news is less likely to be accumulated and released altogether, which reduces the frequency of extreme negative returns (that is, crash events).
Similarly, if the statutes increase (decrease) the withholding of good news, they should increase (decrease) the frequency of extreme positive returns (that is, jump events).
Because SLAPPs mostly target the dissemination of bad news rather than good news, anti-SLAPP statutes should decrease the frequency of crash events (Crash) and not affect the frequency of jump events (Jump).
We test this prediction by examining how anti-SLAPP statutes affect Crash and Jump.
Detailed definitions of Crash and Jump are provided in Appendix A.

## discussion
<!-- para 2 -->
We use a DiD analysis to examine how state anti-SLAPP statutes affect crash risk.
Perceived as a widespread threat to freedom of expression, SLAPPs have considerable chilling effects.
Anti-SLAPP statutes decrease case uncertainty as well as the time and resources required for court defense, which thereby reduces the chilling effects.
Thus, members of the general public (for example, citizens, employees, journalists, and investors) have fewer concerns when publicly criticizing firms and disseminating negative news.
As more bad news is revealed, managers have reduced opportunities to hide bad news.
Since managers bear incremental costs when the withheld bad news is revealed by a third party, anti-SLAPP statutes also discipline managers' actions that hide bad news.
<!-- para 3 -->
We find an economically and statistically significant decrease in a firm's crash risk after the firm's headquarters' state enacted an anti-SLAPP statute.
The results are stronger when the local third parties are more informed, the discovered bad news can be more widely disseminated, and managers face a higher ex ante risk of shareholders' litigation and dismissal.
Anti-SLAPP statutes also increase negative coverage in the media, especially for firms with poorer future performance.
Finally, anti-SLAPP statutes reduce firms' earnings management and overinvestment.
Overall, our paper contributes to the literature by documenting an important economic benefit of legal protection for free expression.
Given the increasing worldwide concerns about the threat of SLAPPs, our evidence has important policy implications for legislators who are considering adopting or improving their anti-SLAPP laws.
