# chen_2025_penny_wise_and_pound_foolish_does_striving_to_meet 句子库存

## introduction
<!-- para 2 -->
Extensive prior research implies that managers have incentives to meet or beat the market's earnings expectations by manipulating accruals or real activities at the expense of long-run firm prospects (e.g., Bhojraj et al. 2009, Cohen and Zarowin 2010, Bereskin et al. 2018).
Additionally, Graham et al. (2005) provide survey evidence that managers will likely forgo an investment project that enhances long-term value when it may lead to missing the consensus analyst earnings forecast.
Besides shareholder value, recent research suggests that myopic actions stemming from fixating on meeting or beating earnings expectations have potential negative effects on the interests of other stakeholders, such as employees (Caskey and Ozel 2017) and the local community (Liu et al. 2021, Thomas et al. 2022).
We extend prior work by examining whether the pressure to reach earnings benchmarks induces managers to manipulate firms' real activities in the production process, which increases the likelihood and frequency of product recalls.
<!-- para 3 -->
Product recalls are the outcome of severe product quality failures.
U.S. federal regulations require firms to suspend sales of the product and promptly report the issue to the relevant regulatory agencies after a safety defect becomes known.
Product recalls are not only prevalent in the consumer market but also have serious adverse impacts on the recalling firms and their customers.
From 2010 to 2015, U.S. federal agencies reported at least 4,000 product recalls each year.
The recalled products are routinely associated with harmful effects on customers, such as injury or even death.
Product recalls also have major negative implications for the recalling firms.
Apart from the costs of replacing or repairing the defective products, product recalls can lead to various firm-level indirect costs, such as the loss of its goodwill, damage to its brand name reputation, the loss of sales, and even product liability lawsuits.
(Jarrell and Peltzman 1985, Hoffer et al. 1988, Barber and Darrough 1996), resulting in shareholders sustaining considerable wealth losses (Jarrell and Peltzman 1985, Pruitt and Peterson 1986, Lee et al. 2015, Kini et al. 2017).
Given the prevalence of recalls and their adverse effects on customers, recalling firms, investors, and broader society along with the emergence of several high-profile product recall events in recent years (e.g., General Motors's ignition switch recall in 2014, Volkswagen's diesel engine recall in 2015, and Samsung's Galaxy Note 7 recall in 2016), it is important to examine whether the earnings pressure that managers experience shapes their production decisions and in turn, the likelihood of product quality failures.
<!-- para 4 -->
Managers' efforts to meet or beat earnings expectations by manipulating real activities are likely to engender product recalls for several reasons.
First, managers may reduce investments in production facilities and cut discretionary expenses in production in pursuing short-term earnings targets (e.g., He and Tian 2013, Irani and Oesch 2016, Caskey and Ozel 2017).
Prior studies document that many product defects stem from insufficient investments and expenditures in the design, manufacture, and maintenance processes (Connally 2009, Taylor 2011, Shah et al. 2017).
As such, firms may have more product defects and hence, a higher likelihood of recalls if managers curtail investments and discretionary expenses in trying to reach earnings targets.
Second, in striving to meet or beat earnings expectations, managers may overproduce in order to boost earnings by reducing the cost of goods sold (e.g., Thomas and Zhang 2002, Roychowdhury 2006).
Overproduction could result in overutilization and lack of maintenance of plants, excessive employee workloads, and inadequate product quality monitoring.
To the extent that managers engage in overproduction to boost short-term earnings, we expect the product quality to deteriorate, translating into more product recalls.
<!-- para 5 -->
Nevertheless, instilling tension into the analysis, it is possible that in taking steps to facilitate meeting earnings targets, firms may benefit in the form of enjoying better access to external financing and attracting more favorable financing terms.
Relaxing their financial constraints enables firms to invest more in production, which in turn, lowers the incidence of product recalls (Kini et al. 2017).
Further, improving their financial condition enables firms to sustain investments in the maintenance process, such as strict quality control, better packaging, and adequate warnings or instructions, which may also result in recalls becoming less likely.
It is also plausible that pursuing earnings targets has no material impact on product recalls because managers may only engage in real activities management when its impact on operations is small (Graham et al. 2005).
Given the severe adverse impact that product recalls can have on recalling firms and their customers, managers facing pressure to meet or beat earnings benchmarks may, for example, refrain from cutting discretionary investment in production that can lead to serious product failures.
Accordingly, it amounts to an empirical question whether product recalls are sensitive to managers' manipulation of real activities in striving to reach earnings targets.
<!-- para 6 -->
To examine this research question, we hand collect data on product recalls from four regulatory agencies in the United States: the Consumer Product Safety Commission (CPSC), the Food and Drug Administration (FDA), the National Highway Traffic Safety Administration (NHTSA), and the U.S. Coast Guard (USCG).
We gauge the earnings target with the consensus analyst forecast and designate a firm as suspected of meeting or beating earnings benchmarks if the difference between its actual earnings per share (EPS) and the average analyst forecasted EPS is between zero and two cents.
Consistent with prior research (Cohen and Zarowin 2010, Zang 2012), we also construct an aggregate measure of a firm's real earnings management activities.
The combination of these two variables captures the firm's suspected benchmark beating through real earnings management (hereafter SBBREM).
In analyzing a sample of 11,434 firm-year observations covering the 2001–2017 period, we find that SBBREM is associated with a higher likelihood and frequency of recalls.
Reflecting the first-order economic impact according to our coefficient estimates, we find that compared with non-SBBREM firms, SBBREM firms experience a 10.92% (57.80%) increase in the likelihood (number) of product recalls relative to the sample mean.
This evidence lends support to the narrative that the pressure to meet or beat earnings targets motivates firms to orchestrate the manipulation of real activities that leads to more product recalls.
<!-- para 7 -->
In extensively analyzing whether our core results are robust, we continue to find supportive evidence.
We also undertake several tests designed to alleviate endogeneity threats to reliable identification.
The results suggest that endogeneity problems are unlikely to be spuriously responsible for the documented impact of SBBREM on product recalls.
Moreover, we conduct two falsification tests.
First, we find that managers' attempts to meet or beat earnings targets through accrual-based earnings management do not alter firms' real business operations and exert no impact on recalls.
Second, after respecifying the dependent variable to the likelihood and frequency of recalls one, two, and three years ago in successive regressions, we reassuringly find that the coefficients on SBBREM are generally insignificant.
This helps dispel the concern that our main results stem from correlated omitted variables that drive both recalls and SBBREM.
<!-- para 8 -->
Next, we explore cross-sectional variation in the relation between SBBREM and product recalls.
First, we find that the impact of SBBREM on product recalls is magnified for firms whose managers have stronger incentives to manipulate earnings.
Our evidence implies that these managers are more eager to manage earnings upward (e.g., Graham et al. 2005, Bergstresser and Phillippon 2006), which distorts firm operations and leads to more product failures.
Second, we report that the importance of SBBREM to product recalls falls for firms subject to more discipline in the form of customer power.
These results suggest that powerful customers constrain managerial opportunistic behaviors in the production process by imposing stricter monitoring and threatening to end cooperation (e.g., Patatoukas 2012, Crawford et al. 2020).
Third, we find that the role that SBBREM plays in product recalls is concentrated in firms with fewer growth opportunities.
This evidence implies that investments in product quality can lead to higher future value for firms with more growth opportunities.
Moreover, these firms experience less severe agency problems (Arikan and Stulz 2016), also reducing the impact of managerial opportunism on product recalls.
Finally, we report that the incidence of recalls has a negative impact on firms' future performance evident in their valuations and sales growth.
<!-- para 9 -->
We make several contributions to extant research.
First, we extend prior work on the consequences of earnings pressure by showing that managers' efforts to meet or beat earnings expectations through real earnings manipulation could lead to more product recalls.
Most prior research on this issue focuses on the economic implications of meeting earnings targets from the perspective of shareholders and documents that firms sacrifice long-term firm value in pursuing short-term earnings objectives (Bhojraj et al. 2009, Ertan 2022).
However, recent studies have begun to explore the fallout for other stakeholders of firms striving to meet the market's earnings expectations (Caskey and Ozel 2017, Thomas et al. 2022).
We add to prior work by showing that earnings pressure could distort the firm's production process, resulting in product quality failures.
Given that product quality is typically central to firms' long-term success and that recalls are detrimental to customers, we detect a negative impact of earnings pressure for both shareholders and customers.
Further, prior studies find that managers in striving to reach earnings targets undertake real activities manipulation (e.g., Roychowdhury 2006).
Our analysis complements prior work by documenting a severe outcome of real activities manipulation driven by managers pursuing short-term earnings targets.
Finally, we provide evidence implying that lowering product quality in attempting to reach earnings targets is not smart because the associated product recalls engender long-term negative consequences for the firm.
Accordingly, we extend prior research by quantifying how much firms are willing to sacrifice (i.e., bearing the risk of future product recalls) for engaging in real earnings management to meet or beat earnings targets.
<!-- para 10 -->
Second, we advance product recall research by analyzing the role that SBBREM plays in the incidence of recalls.
Although product recalls impose large costs on both customers and recalling firms, prior work seldom examines the determinants of recalls, likely reflecting that large-scale product recall data are not easily accessible.
As such, most studies focus on a small sample of firms or a single industry (e.g., Wowak et al. 2015, Shah et al. 2017), although Kini et al. (2017, 2022) are major exceptions.
In leveraging a comprehensive data set on product recalls, we complement prior evidence by showing that managers' opportunistic real earnings management activities in striving to meet earnings targets could lead to more recalls.
As such, our study appears to be the first to document the impact of SBBREM on product markets, which is related to everyday life and is central to firm operations.

## theory
<!-- para 3 -->
Product recalls are the outcome of severe product quality failures, which can stem from design flaws, manufacturing faults, product contamination, poor packaging, inadequate warnings or instructions, lax inspection, etc. Product recalls are prevalent in the consumer market, and these events routinely have major economic and social consequences.
For example, U.S. federal agencies report that at least 4,000 product recalls occurred each year from 2010 to 2015. [^1] Product recalls have substantial adverse effects on customers and the recalling firms.
In fact, recalled products are often responsible for customers suffering serious injuries or even death.
For example, Merck & Co. pulled its Vioxx product from the market in September 2004 after studies revealed that the arthritis medication led to a steep rise in the risk of fatal heart attacks and strokes.
FDA investigators later estimated that at the time of its recall, Vioxx had been taken by some 4 million Americans, leading to 140,000 heart attacks that were responsible for 60,000 deaths. [^2]
<!-- para 4 -->
Product recalls engender direct costs for the recalling firms, such as the costs of replacing or repairing the defective products.
Firms may also incur major indirect costs, including the loss of goodwill, damage to their brand name reputations, the loss of sales, and even exposure to product liability lawsuits (Jarrell and Peltzman 1985, Hoffer et al. 1988, Barber and Darrough 1996).
Accordingly, product recalls are costly to recalling firms, leading to their shareholders suffering a significant wealth loss (Jarrell and Peltzman 1985, Pruitt and Peterson 1986, Lee et al. 2015, Kini et al. 2017).
For example, in 2008, Takata made one of the most complex and expensive recalls in history.
The National Highway Safety Transportation Board expected the recall to cover more than 37 million vehicles involving 49.5 million airbag inflators in the United States alone.
Takata filed for bankruptcy in 2017.
The regulators had forecasted that the company could not finish the recall and fix the defective airbags until 2023. [^3] In 2016, Samsung recalled about 2.5 million units of its new Galaxy Note 7 smartphones because of a flaw in the device's batteries that made them catch fire and explode.
After the recall, Samsung's shares fell more than 8%, erasing 17 billion from the company's market value. [^4] From 2015 to 2018, the CPSC imposed civil penalties relating to recalls against companies that included at least 25 million in fines each year. [^5] According to a survey of firms belonging to the Grocery Manufacturers Association that had experienced a recall in the preceding five years, 29% of the firms reported financial losses ranging from 10 to 29 million as a result of a product recall; 5% of them incurred losses exceeding 100 million. [^6]
<!-- para 6 -->
In survey evidence, Graham et al. (2005) report that corporate executives focus intently on meeting or beating earnings targets.
Managers facing pressure from earnings expectations can either manipulate accruals or manage real activities to boost reported earnings (Roychowdhury 2006, Cohen et al. 2008, Dechow et al. 2010).
Compared with accruals manipulation, managers' real activities in exaggerating earnings may fly under the radar given that they are difficult for investors to detect in the short run, although this form of manipulation likely undermines long-term firm performance (e.g., Bhojraj et al. 2009, Kothari et al. 2016).
In a major downside, managing firm real activities in attempting to reach earnings targets may translate into severe product failures that necessitate recalls in some cases.
<!-- para 7 -->
At an operational level, managers may reduce investments in production facilities and cut discretionary expenses in the production process in responding to short-term earnings target incentives (e.g., He and Tian 2013, Irani and Oesch 2016, Caskey and Ozel 2017).
Extensive prior research implies that many product defects stem from inadequate investments and expenditures in the design, manufacture, and maintenance processes, which lead to obsolete production technology, inadequate maintenance of machinery, overutilization of plants, inadequate training, materials cost cutting, and the failure to implement rigorous quality control systems (Connally 2009, Taylor 2011, Shah et al. 2017).
Additionally, financial distress or having excessive debt in their capital structures may force firms to reduce investments in production, precipitating more recalls (e.g., Maksimovic and Titman 1991, Phillips and Sertsios 2013, Kini et al. 2017).
In analyzing the automotive industry, Shah et al. (2017) document that insufficient investments in capacity and labor tend to lead to overutilization of plants and overreliance on overtime pay and temporary workers, which in turn, increase the incidence of product recalls.
If managers cut investments and discretionary expenses in striving to reach earnings targets, we would expect their firms to suffer more production defects, resulting in a higher likelihood of product recalls.
<!-- para 8 -->
In another way to meet or beat the market’s expectations, firms may elect to overproduce in order to reduce the cost of goods sold and boost reported earnings (e.g., Thomas and Zhang 2002, Roychowdhury 2006). [^7] Importantly, overproduction likely causes overutilization and lack of maintenance of plants, excessive employee workloads, and lax product inspections, raising the likelihood of product defects.
Accordingly, if managers overproduce in pursuing earnings targets, we would expect their firms to have poorer product quality that may culminate in more recalls. [^8]
<!-- para 9 -->
In short, managers may cut investments and discretionary expenses as well as engage in overproduction in striving to reach earnings targets.
Our prediction reflects that this behavior leads to product failures becoming more frequent, which in turn, results in more product recalls.
<!-- para 10 -->
**Hypothesis 1.
Firms suspected of manipulating real activities to meet or beat the market's earnings expectations are more likely to suffer product recalls.**
<!-- para 11 -->
However, steps taken in pursuing short-term earnings benchmarks may translate into a reduction in product recalls.
By managing earnings to meet or beat market expectations, firms may secure more access to external financing and enjoy more favorable financing terms.
Watts and Zimmerman (1990) argue that firms may manage their earnings to reduce the probability of violating a loan covenant, translating into cheaper debt financing.
Burgstahler (1997) suggests that managing earnings to avoid earnings decreases and losses can increase a firm's credit rating.
Trueman and Titman (1988) provide evidence that firms exploit real activities management to smooth reported income in order to attract lower borrowing costs.
Bhojraj et al. (2009) find that firms that reduce their discretionary expenditures to beat forecasts issue more equity afterward.
Reinforcing their research, Graham et al. (2005, p. 27) find that 86.3% of surveyed executives "believe that meeting benchmarks builds credibility with the capital market." Indeed, prior research explores how the financial condition of a firm affects its ability and incentives to invest in product quality.
For example, Maksimovic and Titman (1991) argue that firms with more debt in their capital structures suffer investment distortions that might force them to cut discretionary investments in product quality.
In analyzing the supermarket industry, Matsa (2011) finds that higher leverage is associated with greater product shortfalls.
Phillips and Sertsios (2013) examine the airline industry and document that baggage handling and on-time performance fall when airlines experience financial distress.
Kini et al. (2017) report that arranging external financing on attractive terms alleviates firms' financial constraints, which enables them to invest more in production.
As such, a better financial condition stemming from meeting or beating earnings targets may lead to increased investment in production, which in turn, can result in recalls becoming less likely.
Further, improving their financial condition also enables firms to sustain investments in the maintenance process, which may also reduce the incidence of recalls.
<!-- para 12 -->
Additionally, it is plausible that the incidence of product recalls is insensitive to managers' efforts to meet or beat earnings targets.
Because recalls are the outcome of severe product quality failures, meeting or beating earnings targets may have no material impact on product recalls given that survey evidence implies that managers will only engage in real activities manipulation when its impact on firm operations is minimal (Graham et al. 2005).
Specifically, Graham et al. (2005, p. 40) highlight that interviewed the Chief Financial Officer participants admit that they are tempted to initiate real activities management "as long as the actions are within Generally Accepted Accounting Principles and the real sacrifices are not too large." Given the severe adverse impact that product recalls can have on recalling firms and their customers, managers facing pressure to meet or beat earnings benchmarks may, for example, refrain from cutting discretionary investment in production that could lead to serious product failures.
Consequently, the implications of firms relying on real activities in striving to reach earnings targets may be too small to shape the likelihood of product recalls.
It may be difficult to accept that managers eager to meet or beat the market's earnings expectations would resort to such extensive real activities manipulation that a product recall becomes more likely.
Accordingly, the role that managers' manipulation of real activities in trying to meet or beat earnings benchmarks plays in shaping the incidence of product recalls distils to an empirical question.

## methods
<!-- para 3 -->
We gather the data used in this study from multiple sources.
We hand collect data on product recalls from four regulatory agencies in the United States that govern product quality and safety there.
Data on food, drug, and medical device recalls are available from the weekly enforcement reports published by the FDA.
Data on consumer product recalls are collected from the CPSC, which covers recalls of numerous industries, such as children's products, household appliances, home furnishings, toys, nursery products, workshop tools, and yard equipment.
Data on automobile recalls are retrieved from the NHTSA.
Finally, we obtain data on recalls of boats and related products from the USCG.
Each recall announcement that regulators make covers the product being recalled, the manufacturer, the recall volume, and the recall date.
<!-- para 4 -->
We download firm financial and customer data from Compustat, analyst forecast data from The Institutional Brokers' Estimate System, stock return data from Center for Research of Security Prices, executive compensation data from ExecuComp, merger and acquisition data from Thompson Financial SDC Platinum (SDC), and institutional ownership data from Thomson Financial.
We manually match firms in the product recall data with firms in Compustat based on their names.
If searching by the parent name does not yield a match, we conduct additional searches to identify whether the name of the recalling firm matches any of its subsidiaries in Compustat.
We restrict the sample period to 2001–2017 given that the number of recall events is limited prior to 2001. [^9]
<!-- para 5 -->
After beginning with all the firm-year observations in Compustat during our sample period, we exclude firms operating in financial and utility industries (Standard Industrial Classification (SIC) codes 6000–6999 and 4900–4999).
We also exclude firm-years without any analyst forecasts because our earnings expectation measure is constructed based on analyst earnings forecasts.
Further, we follow Bhojraj et al. (2009) by removing firm-years with total assets under 10 million to ensure that the presence of microcap or penny stocks does not bias our results.
We also drop firm-years with missing values for any of the variables used in the baseline analysis.
Finally, we follow Kini et al. (2017) by excluding firms that belong to a three-digit SIC industry for which no member-whether the focal firm or a competitor-has issued a product recall during the years under study. [^10] To mitigate the effect of outliers, we winsorize all continuous variables at both the 1st and 99th percentiles.
Our final sample consists of 11,434 firm-year observations for 1,896 unique firms. [^11]

Our dependent variables are the likelihood (INCIDENCE) and frequency (FREQ) of product recalls.
Consistent with prior research (e.g., Kini et al. 2017), we specify INCIDENCE as a dummy variable equal to one for firms with at least one product recall during the year and zero otherwise.
We define FREQ as the natural logarithm of one plus the number of recalls during the year.
Our independent variable of interest is SBBREM, which reflects the interaction between suspected benchmark beating (SUSPECT) and high real earnings management (HREM).
SUSPECT is a dummy variable set to one for firms with an earnings surprise between zero and two cents in the year and zero otherwise.
Consistent with Caskey and Ozel (2017), we calculate the earnings surprise as the gap between a firm's actual EPS and the average analyst forecasted EPS. [^13] HREM is a dummy variable equal to one for firms whose real earnings management is above the industry-year median and zero otherwise.
We use SBBREM rather than stand-alone HREM because SBBREM better captures firms' opportunistic real earnings management activities.
There are two competing views on managers' incentives to engage in real earnings management according to prior research.
The signaling view suggests that managers conduct real earnings management to signal their private information to capital market participants (e.g., Gunny 2010, Al-Shattarat et al. 2022).
Considering the costly nature of real earnings management, managers manipulate real activities when the benefit of real earnings management derived from the information conveyed to the capital markets outweighs the negative impact (Zhao et al. 2012).
The managerial opportunism view holds that managers exploit real earnings management to disguise the firm's true economic performance and misinform financial statement users (e.g., Roychowdhury 2006).
Empirically, it is difficult to distinguish the signaling and the opportunistic incentives of real earnings management.
However, there is evidence that managers undertake real earnings management activities to meet or beat earnings benchmarks.
For example, Cohen et al. (2010) find that managers opportunistically use monthly advertising as a real earnings management tool to reach quarterly earnings targets.
Managers may also myopically rely on real earnings management to meet or beat earnings targets to secure private contractual benefits (Cohen and Zarowin 2010, Kothari et al. 2016).
As such, it is possible that real earnings management activities not designed for meeting or beating earnings benchmarks are mainly for signaling purposes or are not opportunistic enough to severely disrupt firms' product design and production process.
Given that recalls are the outcome of severe product quality failures, such activities may not lead to an increase in product recalls.
In this design, SBBREM fully captures the treatment effect: that is, the difference in recall likelihood and frequency between firms likely to meet or beat earnings benchmarks through income increasing real earnings management (i.e., firms with SUSPECT = 1 and HREM = 1) and the remaining firms (i.e., firms with SUSPECT = 1 and HREM = 0, firms with SUSPECT = 0 and HREM = 1, and firms with SUSPECT = 0 and HREM = 0).

Although SBBREM is intended to capture managers' opportunistic real earnings management, like many other measures in empirical studies, it is an imperfect measure.
For example, it remains possible that managers strategically use real earnings management to meet or beat earnings targets in striving to signal future performance to capital market participants.
Further, the damage that real earnings management inflicts on the firm's production process stems from not only the level of real earnings management but also how real earnings management is conducted.
Managers who are more eager to meet or beat earnings benchmarks may adopt more aggressive real earnings management methods.
For example, these managers may cut expenses nearly across the board, irrespective of whether these expenses are central to the production process (e.g., Research and Development Expenses (R&D) and quality control).
In contrast, managers with weaker incentives may manage real earnings in a milder way.
For example, they may cut expenses more selectively, including by only reducing those that are less detrimental to the firm's production (e.g., office supplies and business entertainment).
Additionally, they may manage real earnings more strategically by planning the cut in advance to minimize the disruption to firm operations.
<!-- para 80 -->
To deepen the analysis of the opportunistic nature of real earnings management, we examine the role that managerial incentives play in shaping the relation between SBBREM and product recalls.
We expect that when managerial incentives to reach earnings targets are stronger, SBBREM is better able to capture opportunistic real earnings management, which in turn, leads to more product recalls.
Prior research documents that career concerns motivate managers to orchestrate upward earnings management (e.g., Graham et al. 2005, Bergstresser and Philippon 2006).
There is also evidence that media reporting on firms' short-term performance could induce managerial short-termism, which impedes long-term corporate investment (Dai et al. 2021).
If managers cut quality-related expenditures and overproduce in pursuing earnings targets, then the impact of SBBREM on product recalls should be amplified in firms whose managers have greater career concerns and firms subject to more extensive media coverage.
<!-- para 81 -->
Consistent with prior research (e.g., Armstrong et al. 2012), we gauge executives' career concerns with the firm's ex ante takeover probability (TAKEOVER), estimated as the predicted value from the logit regression specified in Billett and Xue (2007).
Executives are routinely terminated after their firms are acquired.
To deter potential acquirers, managers in firms facing a greater takeover threat may manage earnings upward to reach earnings targets in order to keep their stock prices inflated.
We define media coverage (MEDIA) as the number of earnings-related news reports issued on the firm during the year.
Given prior research on the disciplinary role the media plays (e.g., Dai et al. 2021), we expect that managers who face more intensive earnings-related media coverage are more likely to cut quality-related expenditures and overproduce in pursuing earnings targets.
We separately bisect the sample according to the median value of one-year lagged takeover probability and media coverage.

In both cases, there is a perceptible difference between the coefficients.

Next, we evaluate whether the relation between SBBREM and product recalls hinges on firm growth.

Accordingly, we expect that the positive link between SBBREM and product recalls will dissipate in firms enjoying more growth opportunities.

Finally, we explore whether firms' future performance suffers after a product recall.
This involves specifying TOBINSQ as the firm's Tobin's $Q$, which is the ratio of the market value of assets to their book value, in year $t+3$.
The market value of assets is the sum of the market value of common stock, the book value of preferred stock, the book value of long-term debt, and the book value of current liabilities minus the difference between the book value of current assets and the book value of inventories.
We code SALEGROW as the firm's sales in year $t+3$ minus that in year $t$ divided by sales in year $t$.
In separate estimations, we regress the two firm performance measures on INCIDENCE and a series of control variables.
The selection of the control variables follows prior research examining the determinants of Tobin's $Q$ and sales growth (e.g., McConnell and Servaes 1990, Chung and Jo 1996, Daines 2001).[^30] Because firms located in different states face different laws and market environments, we include state fixed effects in both regressions (Daines 2001, Gompers et al. 2010).
<!-- para 112 -->
In the results reported in Table 9, we find that the coefficients on INCIDENCE are negative and highly significant in both columns.
This evidence implies that future performance significantly falls for firms that have a recall in the current year.[^31] Combining this evidence with the results in our baseline analysis, we can conclude that the increased product recall risk associated with SBBREM could undermine future performance, reflecting that firms can pay a heavy price in striving to reach earnings targets through real earnings management.

## discussion
<!-- para 2 -->
We examine whether managers in pursuing earnings targets through real earnings management increase the incidence of product recalls.
On one hand, managers may reduce investments in production facilities and cut discretionary expenses in production in striving to meet or beat short-term earnings benchmarks.
Insufficient investments and expenditures in the production process could lead to product defects, resulting in more recalls.
Also, managers may overproduce to boost reported earnings by lowering the cost of goods sold.
Because overproduction leads to overutilization and lack of maintenance of plants, excessive workloads for employees, and lax product quality monitoring, it likely translates into more product recalls.
On the other hand, firms that reach earnings targets may enjoy better access to external financing and attract more favorable financing terms.
By relaxing its financial constraints, the firm has more resources to spend on production, potentially leading to recalls becoming less likely.
It is also plausible that pursuing earnings targets has no material impact on product recalls given that managers may only engage in real activities management when its impact on firm operations is minimal.
Accordingly, the role that managers' manipulation of real activities in attempting to meet or beat earnings benchmarks plays in shaping the likelihood of recalls amounts to an empirical question.
<!-- para 3 -->
In analyzing a sample of 11,434 firm-year observations spanning the 2001–2017 period, we find that SBBREM engenders a higher likelihood and frequency of product recalls.
In a set of cross-sectional results consistent with expectations, we find that the importance of SBBREM to product recalls rises when executives have stronger incentives to manage earnings upward and that it falls for firms whose customers have greater power and for firms with more growth opportunities.
Other evidence implies that lowering product quality to meet or beat earnings targets leads to long-term negative consequences for the firm in the form of lower Tobin's Q and sales growth.
<!-- para 4 -->
We contribute to extant research by examining the impact of managers' activities in pursuing earnings targets through real earnings management on product recalls.
Reflecting the lack of large-scale product recall data, most existing studies focus on a small sample of firms or a single industry.
In contrast, we rely on comprehensive data on recalls to complement prior work by showing that managers' benchmark beating behaviors through real earnings management are a major determinant of product recalls.
We also extend research on the consequences of earnings pressure by providing evidence that managers' opportunistic activities in striving to meet or beat market earnings expectations distort the firm's operations to the detriment of its product quality evident in recalls.
Given that product defects can impose large costs on customers and recalling firms, our research is relevant to managers, investors, customers, regulators, and broader society by alerting them to another potential downside stemming from earnings pressure.
<!-- para 5 -->
However, an important limitation of the study stems from pooling different types of recalls together in one sample, regardless of the heterogeneity in how different agencies handle recalls and the timeline of the recalls.
We also urge readers to interpret our results with caution given the potential measurement error arising from our research design.
Finally, although we examine the impact of product recalls on Tobin's Q and sales growth, future research could deepen the analysis of this issue by exploring other economic outcomes for firms and their customers.
