# darby2025_an_agency 句子库存

## introduction
<!-- para 1 -->
Much of activist investors' initial engagement with a firm happens "behind the scenes." If that does not work, they "go public," targeting the firm via media campaigns, shareholder proposals, proxy contests, and open letters to management (McCahery et al. 2016).
For example, supply chain woes at biotechnology firm CytoDyn led activist investors to nominate new directors and call for the replacement of executives (Squire 2021).
A healthcare vice president shared concerns about the prevalence of activist investors' campaigns and noted that "attacks have become almost commonplace in the industry." In 2024, for example, a record 160 activism campaigns were launched (Herbst-Bayliss 2025), and one prominent forum predicts that eventually "most companies will be affected by [investor] activism in one form or another" (Park 2016).
<!-- para 2 -->
Most research on activist investors has examined the direct effects on targeted firms (for a review, see Ahn and Wiersema 2019). [^2] That is not the focus here because, as previous research suggests, firms almost certainly change their behavior when targeted.
Less understood are the indirect spillover effects of activist investors' attacks beyond the targeted firm, as examined outside the supply chain context by Aslan and Kumar (2016), Gantchev et al. (2019), Shi et al. (2020), and Zhu (2022).
As noted above, a spillover effect occurs when an attack on “a firm or firms in an investor’s portfolio of holdings influences actions within other firms that are not presently targeted for activism” (Shi et al. 2020, p. 1866).
Drawing on an analogy offered by Shi et al. (2020, p. 1866), “much like speeding drivers who see a car pulled over by law enforcement might slow down to avoid being pulled over themselves … firms owned by activist investors who observe those activists targeting another firm might make changes to avoid becoming the next target.” In other words, firms that are not currently targeted by activist investors but could be targeted if an activist investor becomes unhappy with them might proactively improve their behavior.
<!-- para 3 -->
Indeed, evidence from neighboring fields suggests that firms take a variety of actions in the hopes of warding off activist investors, including increasing productivity, cost, and capital allocation efficiency, and product differentiation (Aslan and Kumar 2016); increasing leverage and payout and decreasing capital expenditures and cash holdings (Gantchev et al. 2019); increasing restructuring activities and decreasing growth activities (Shi et al. 2020); and decreasing innovation investments and CEO compensation (Zhu 2022).
By examining the potential spillover effects on a prominent supply chain failure (i.e., recalls), this study pioneers consideration of activist investors and spillover effects in the supply chain context.

## theory
<!-- para 3 -->
Agency theory is one of the “usual suspects” (Cornelissen et al. 2024, p. 8) that is “commonly applied to SCM research” (Pagell et al. 2019, p. 129) because it sheds light on relationships in which one party (i.e., principal) delegates authority to another party (i.e., agent) to make decisions on their behalf (Eisenhardt 1989).
Supply chain scholars have used agency theory to explain dynamics in buyer-supplier relationships, such as supply risk management (Zsidisin and Ellram 2003), supplier sustainability risk (Hajmohammad and Vachon 2016), and supply failures (Kauppi et al. 2024).
More broadly, agency theory is used to explain the separation of ownership and control in public firms wherein there are thousands of owners (i.e., stockholders) who rely on executives to manage the firm on their behalf (Jensen and Meckling 1976).
As stockholders, activist investors delegate authority to executives who work with supply chain managers when a product defect arises (Darby et al. 2023).
<!-- para 4 -->
At the core of agency theory is the agency problem, which arises because of (i) goal incongruence—i.e., principals and agents have different aims and risk preferences, and (ii) information asymmetry—i.e., agents know more about the firm's operations than principals know (Kauppi et al. 2024).
These factors give agents—in this case, supply chain managers—the opportunity to prioritize their goals over those of principals due to monitoring challenges (Eisenhardt 1989).
For example, to protect their career interests, supply chain managers may downplay the severity of a defect or try to buy extra time to analyze the problem before initiating a recall (Darby et al. 2023).
This action may be at odds with the interests of stockholders because recalling slowly can harm consumers and the firm's financial performance (Ni and Huang 2018; Wowak et al. 2021).
<!-- para 5 -->
While many stockholders are content to rely on the board of directors to monitor executives (e.g., Brav et al. 2008), activist investors make their displeasure known if they believe that management is not acting in stockholders' interest.
Because investor activism is preceded by a public filing that receives significant attention (Shi et al. 2020), executives know when one of their stockholders has targeted another firm.
As one CEO shared, "We track share ownership and monitor both the owner and their share movement.
So, if we have someone in our stock with a history of activism, we carefully watch their actions." To avoid becoming the next target, executives take actions to signal [^3] that they have stockholders' interests in mind (Gantchev et al. 2019).
For example, quickly recalling defective products may signal to activist investors that a firm's executives are decisive (Wowak et al. 2021) and prioritize consumer well-being (Hora et al. 2011).
<!-- para 6 -->
As the ownership stakes of activist investors increase, the potential threat of becoming the next target increases (Gantchev et al. 2019).
This should lead supply chain managers, in turn, to recall more quickly to favorably manage the perceptions of activist investors who own shares in their firm.
Overall, stock ownership by activist investors who have not targeted the focal firm—but have targeted other firms—will be negatively associated with time to recall, such that the focal firm will recall more quickly as the ownership stakes of activist investors increase.
<!-- para 7 -->
**H1.** Stock ownership by activist investors who have not targeted a firm for activism—but have targeted other firms—is negatively associated with time-to-recall.
Specifically, the more stock that is owned by these activist investors, the faster the recall.
<!-- para 9 -->
Agency theory suggests that characteristics of the delegated task—in this context, managing product recalls—may influence the degree of information asymmetry between principals and agents (Eisenhardt 1989).
This may influence the extent to which agents engage in impression management tactics—such as recalling quickly—to influence the perceptions of their principals (Lu et al. 2021).
This study thus examines the potential moderating effects of two recall characteristics: defect type and severity.
Beginning with defect type, scholars have differentiated between design-related defects that stem from material, component, and performance failures due to product design (e.g., Ni and Huang 2018) and manufacturing-related defects that stem from the “methods, facilities, and controls used in the manufacturing, processing, and packaging of a product” (Ball et al. 2018, p. 60).
<!-- para 10 -->
Manufacturing-related defects tend to be better understood and easier to identify than design-related defects because firms have quality management systems in place to track potential deviations (Ni and Huang 2018).
Supply chain managers thus can respond more quickly when manufacturing-related defects arise.
Indeed, previous recall research suggests that firms recall products with such defects more quickly (Ni and Huang 2018).
In the parlance of agency theory (Eisenhardt 1989), there is less information asymmetry between principals (i.e., activist investors) and agents (i.e., executives) surrounding manufacturing-related defects.
As a result, supply chain managers may be more likely to be seen by principals as culpable because manufacturing-related recalls may reflect a lack of adequate oversight (Hora et al. 2011).
However, with low information asymmetry, principals can more easily assess whether agents are acting in the interests of stockholders (Eisenhardt 1989), so agents cannot leverage impression management tactics to the same extent.
Accordingly, the spillover effect of activist investor stock ownership may be weakened because supply chain managers have less ability to manage the perceptions of principals.
<!-- para 11 -->
In contrast, design-related defects provide stronger opportunities for ownership by activist investors to make a difference.
Design-related defects are complex and may “reveal themselves only under specific and unique combinations of conditions” (Ni and Huang 2018, pp. 75–76).
In the medical products sector, for example, each product must go through a multistage design process that involves several entities, including the FDA (FDA 2018).
Despite a rigorous and structured design process, design-related defects still happen.
For example, issues may develop over time horizons not examined or in combination with factors that were unforeseen during the design process.
Consequently, supply chain managers are less likely to be seen as culpable for design-related defects because of the complexity and number of entities involved (Hora et al. 2011), and activist investors may be less likely to attribute such defects to management’s shortcomings.
<!-- para 12 -->
Within the agency theory framework, there is greater information asymmetry between principals (i.e., activist investors) and agents (i.e., executives) surrounding design-related defects (Eisenhardt 1989).
This situation makes it more challenging for principals to assess whether agents are acting in stockholders' interests, and this ambiguity makes it easier for agents to engage in impression management tactics (Eisenhardt 1989).
This leads to an expectation that the spillover effect of activist investor stock ownership will be stronger for design-related defects as compared to manufacturing-related defects.
<!-- para 13 -->
**H2.** The spillover effect of activist investor stock ownership on time-to-recall will differ for design-related defects and manufacturing-related defects, such that the recall-quickening effect is stronger for design-related defects relative to manufacturing-related defects.
<!-- para 15 -->
Previous research suggests that the severity of an event also may influence the extent to which agents try to manage principals' perceptions (Graffin et al. 2016).
This situation results, in part, because the "potential risk/reward tradeoffs" (Eisenhardt 1989, p. 65) inherent in the principal-agent relationship grow with the level of severity.
When a defect is severe, supply chain managers may want to engage in impression management tactics to a greater extent to help keep activist investors at bay.
The severity of the underlying defect thus may strengthen the spillover effect of activist investor stock ownership on time-to-recall.
<!-- para 16 -->
The FDA categorizes medical product recalls into three classes based on the severity of the underlying defect.
Class I and II recalls involve high-severity defects that can cause illness, serious injury, or even death (FDA 2014), such as an epinephrine injection device with a faulty mechanism that fails to activate during a life-threatening allergic reaction (FDA 2017).
In contrast, Class III recalls involve low-severity defects, such as minor labeling issues, that do not pose any health risks but should be recalled because they violate regulations (FDA 2014).
Following previous studies of medical product recalls (Ball et al. 2018; Darby et al. 2023; Wowak et al. 2021), this study considers high-severity (Class I and II) and low-severity (Class III) recalls as two categories rather than points on a severity continuum.
<!-- para 17 -->
Recalls involving high-severity defects can have significant ramifications, including lost sales, increased costs, erosion of market value, and the potential for litigation (Ni and Huang 2018; Wowak and Boone 2015).
For example, the Vioxx recall mentioned above resulted in criminal charges, federal fines, and lawsuits, which ultimately cost Merck billions of dollars (Krauskopf 2007).
Beyond the recalling firm, high-severity recalls also harm supply chain partners and consumer well-being (e.g., Busse et al. 2017).
Under these conditions, executives will be eager to manage the impressions of principals—particularly activist investors who have attacked other firms—to convince them that high-severity recalls are being handled appropriately.
As a chief financial officer (CFO) told us, “The public relations from a higher severity recall will be very negative.
Along with the financial ramifications, this could cause increased pressure from activist shareholders to demand changes to the management team and/or company strategy.” Agency theory suggests that executives will want to take actions to avoid this pressure (Eisenhardt 1989).
Responding quickly when faced with high-severity defects may help mitigate the damage because it can help the firm limit the number of units on the market, the cost of the recall, and, most importantly, the number of consumers harmed (Hora et al. 2011; Ni and Huang 2018).
Such prudence also may help keep activist investors at bay.
<!-- para 18 -->
In contrast, low-severity defects do not put the firm or consumer health in jeopardy, which should make them of little interest to activist investors.
For example, a firm recalled an over-the-counter pain-relieving cream because the lot number printed on the secondary packaging differed from the lot number printed on the product.
The firm could have ignored this defect, given that the correct lot number appeared on the product, and it was unlikely that consumers would have noticed the error on the packaging.
Indeed, most firms resolve minor labeling and packaging discrepancies without initiating a recall (Wowak et al. 2021).
As such, the spillover effect of activist investor stock ownership will be weaker for products with low-severity defects as compared to products with high-severity defects.
<!-- para 19 -->
**H3.** The spillover effect of activist investor stock ownership on time-to-recall will differ for high-severity and low-severity recalls, such that the recall-quickening effect is stronger for high-severity recalls relative to low-severity recalls.

## methods
<!-- para 3 -->
Data from six sources were used to test the hypotheses in the medical products sector.
Data on pharmaceutical and medical device recalls were obtained via a Freedom of Information Act (FOIA) request to the FDA. [^4] This recall data was then matched with firm-level data from the COMPUSTAT, Institutional Shareholder Services (ISS), Wharton Research Data Services (WRDS) Thomson Reuters Institutional (13f) Holdings, and Audit Analytics Shareholder Activism databases.
The intersection of these datasets resulted in a sample of 5427 recalls initiated by 138 publicly traded firms across 19 years (2002–2020).
<!-- para 5 -->
Activist investor stock ownership was measured following Shi et al. (2020)'s three-step process.
The first step was to identify institutional investors who targeted a firm (or firms) using Schedule 13D and 13D/A filings available from the Audit Analytics Shareholder Activism database.
Investors are required to file a Schedule 13D (or 13D/A) with the Securities and Exchange Commission (SEC) within 10 days of acquiring more than 5% of shares in a publicly traded firm if they have an interest in influencing management.
In the filing, investors must also report the purpose of the transaction, which may or may not be to intervene in the firm's operations.
Following Shi et al. (2020, p. 1873), activist investors are defined as institutional investors "who have, within the past year, filed a Schedule 13D (or 13D/A) related to concerns, dispute, or control at any firm" in the sample.
Limiting the identification of activist investor attacks to firms in the sample helps to ensure "operational similarity" (Hora and Klassen 2013, p. 53) between the targeted firm and the focal firm on a critical dimension: product recalls.
Activist investors who filed a Schedule 13D (or 13D/A) related to agreements, discussion, support, or other were excluded (Brav et al. 2008); the focus was concerns, dispute, and control [^5] because they reflect an activist investor's intentions to intervene in firm operations, which constitutes a threat and thus may result in spillover effects.
<!-- para 6 -->
The sample of activist investors was then matched with their stock ownership using data from WRDS Thomson Reuters Institutional (13f) Holdings.
Specifically, a fuzzy matching algorithm connected the activist investor's name and central index key reported in the 13D filings with the fund manager's name and manager number reported in the 13f database.
Matches with scores of less than 0.95 were manually checked for matching quality and hand-matched as needed.
Following a conservative approach, activist investors that could not be identified with certainty were excluded.
Lastly, for each firm in each year, the number of shares held by activist investors was summed and divided by the total number of outstanding shares to create activist investor stock ownership.
The measure reflects the percentage of stock ownership held by activist investors who have targeted other firms—but not the recalling firm—in the recall year.
For example, if a firm initiated a recall in 2011, then the measure reflects the stock ownership held by activist investors who targeted other sample firms in 2011.
A robustness check with an alternative measure identifying activist investors who targeted any firm in the sample in the year prior to the recall year yielded consistent results.
<!-- para 7 -->
Online Supplement Appendix B provides details about the construction of activist investor stock ownership and its alignment with this study's focus on spillover effects.
As it illustrates, this measure captures stock ownership by activist investors in a non-targeted firm, examining how ownership by activist investors who have targeted other firms influences how a non-targeted firm handles product recalls.
Activist investor stock ownership ranged from 0% to nearly 8%. [^6] The measure was centered and standardized.
<!-- para 9 -->
Following past recall research using FDA data (e.g., Darby et al. 2023; Wowak et al. 2021), time-to-recall was measured as the number of days between the defect awareness date (i.e., when a firm became aware of a product quality issue) and the recall initiation date.
Both dates were provided in the FDA data.
Although time-to-recall is not normally distributed, it was not transformed a priori because it is logged as part of the estimation approach described below.
<!-- para 11 -->
Defect type was constructed based on the reason for recall, which is a description provided by the recalling firm in the FDA dataset.
The reasons were manually classified as manufacturing-related or design-related (Ball et al. 2018; Ni and Huang 2018).
To illustrate, a guide catheter that was recalled “due to a manufacturing non-conformance that could lead to hub leaks” was classified as a manufacturing-related defect.
A warming blanket, which is used to prevent hypothermia during orthopedic surgery, that was recalled because “a design change can make these blankets more susceptible to unintended occlusion of airflow resulting in limited or partial inflation during use,” was classified as a design-related defect.
Defect type is a dichotomous variable equal to zero for manufacturing-related defects and one for design-related defects.
<!-- para 12 -->
Defect severity was measured using the recall classifications provided in the FDA data.
In line with research precedent (Ball et al. 2018; Darby et al. 2023), Class I and II recalls were categorized as high-severity defects because they can cause injury, illness, or death, and Class III recalls were categorized as low-severity defects because they do not pose health risks.
<!-- para 14 -->
Several recall characteristics and supply chain factors shown to influence recalls were included as controls.
Product lines was included to control for the number of product lines impacted by the recall, as detailed in the FDA dataset (e.g., Ni and Huang 2018).
Distribution pattern, which was also detailed in the FDA dataset, was included to control for the scope of distribution because the broader the scope of distribution, the more logistically challenging the recall (Wowak et al. 2016).
It is a dichotomous variable equal to zero for localized distribution and one for nationwide or international distribution.
A firm's recall history was controlled for by including recent recalls—i.e., the number of recalls initiated by the firm in the previous year (Ni and Huang 2018). [^7] R&D intensity—the ratio of R&D expenses to sales—was also included because it has been shown to influence recalls (Wowak et al. 2021).
The natural logarithm of total sales (sales) was included because firm size can influence recalls (Hall and Johnson-Hall 2017) and the likelihood of being targeted (e.g., Brav et al. 2008).
<!-- para 15 -->
Because previous research suggests that the CEO may also influence recalls (Darby et al. 2023; Liu et al. 2016; Mayo et al. 2022; Wowak et al. 2015), three CEO characteristics were included as controls.
CEO stock ownership was measured as the percentage of shares outstanding held by the firm's CEO, excluding options, as reported in COMPUSTAT Execucomp Annual Compensation (Darby et al. 2023; Mayo et al. 2022).
CEO cash-based compensation was measured following Liu et al. (2016) as the logarithm of one plus the CEO's cash compensation.
CEO tenure was measured as the number of years elapsed between the recall year and the year the individual became CEO based on the start date provided in COMPUSTAT Execucomp Annual Compensation (Mayo et al. 2022; Wowak et al. 2015).
<!-- para 16 -->
Several firm-level variables were included to control for factors shown to influence stock ownership.
Two measures of supply chain performance were included: return on assets, measured as the ratio of net income to total assets (Falcone et al. 2024), and inventory turnover, measured as the natural logarithm of the ratio of cost of goods sold to inventory (Darby et al. 2020).
Because firms with significant debt face different pressures from stockholders (Shi et al. 2020), debt holding ratio, measured as the ratio of total debt to total assets, was included as a control.
The concentration of stock ownership by institutional investors (institutional ownership concentration) was also included because activist investors often target firms with concentrated institutional stock ownership to efficiently garner support for their campaigns (Gantchev et al. 2019).
This measure used the Herfindahl–Hirschman Index (HHI) from the WRDS Thomson-Reuters Institutional (13f) Holdings database, which captures the extent to which stock ownership is clustered among a small number of institutional investors (Shi et al. 2020).
<!-- para 17 -->
A firm's board of directors can influence both activist investor stock ownership (Gantchev et al. 2019) and recalls (Wowak et al. 2021), so three board characteristics were controlled for using ISS data.
Board independence was measured as the percentage of directors not employed by the firm (Wowak et al. 2021), and CEO-chair separation was measured as a dichotomous variable equal to one if the CEO was not the chair of the board and zero if the CEO chaired the board (Wowak et al. 2015).
Consistent with Wowak et al. (2021), Female board representation (FBR), measured as the ratio of female directors to the total number of directors, was also included.
<!-- para 18 -->
Lastly, firm and year fixed effects were included to account for time-invariant omitted variable bias and changes in the stock market and regulatory environment over time (e.g., Ni and Huang 2018).
A dummy variable was used to reflect whether the recall involves a pharmaceutical or medical device because different medical products may have different norms (Ball et al. 2018).

<!-- para 29 -->
The hypotheses were tested at the recall level rather than at the firm level because each recall has its own time-to-recall (e.g., Wowak et al. 2021).
Following research precedent (Darby et al. 2023; Ni and Huang 2018), the study relied on accelerated failure time (AFT) models, which are used to examine how time-varying variables influence the time to an event of interest (Wei 1992)—here, time-to-recall.
Because some firms in the sample experienced multiple recalls, recurrent-event AFT models with robust standard errors and a Weibull distribution were used (Box-Steffensmeier et al. 2004).
Following Shi et al. (2020), the hypotheses were tested using firms not targeted by activist investors to ensure that the models captured variation in time-to-recall due to the threat—not realization—of activism.
Thus, the analysis excludes recall observations in which the recalling firm was targeted in the recall year. [^9] As such, the coefficient for activist investor stock ownership reflects spillover effects (i.e., how the level of stock ownership by activist investors in a non-targeted firm influences the time-to-recall).
The model estimation follows the generalized equation:
<!-- para 30 -->
$$
\log(t_{ijt})=\beta_{0}+\beta X_{it}+\sum\beta_{k}\mathrm{Firm}_{i}+\sum\beta_{m}\mathrm{Year}_{t}+u_{ijt} \tag{1}
$$
<!-- para 31 -->
where $t_{ijt}$ is the time-to-recall (in days) for recall j for firm i in year t, $\beta_0$ is an intercept term, $\beta$ is a vector of coefficients, $X_{it}$ is a vector of covariates, and $u_{ijt}$ is an error term with a Weibull distribution.

To test H3, a split-sample analysis was used to examine the moderating effect of defect severity (Ball et al. 2018; Darby et al. 2023; Wowak et al. 2021).
The underlying rationale is that defect severity is best characterized as distinct categories (i.e., those that can cause illness, injury, or death versus those that pose no health risks) rather than as a continuum.
Model 4 presents the results for high-severity defects, and Model 5 presents the results for low-severity defects.
Activist investor stock ownership has a negative, significant effect on time-to-recall for high severity defects ( $\beta = -0.06$, p = 0.028) but not for low severity defects ( $\beta = 0.09$, p = 0.280).
These results suggest that the spillover effect of activist investor stock ownership is stronger for high-severity defects, which aligns with theorizing in H3.
In sum, the findings provide support for all three hypotheses. [^12]

While recurrent-event AFT models account for changes in the likelihood of an event due to underlying factors that change over time (Ni and Huang 2018), they may not fully account for unobserved heterogeneity.
To address this, frailty models were used to account for unobserved heterogeneity across recalls by including a random parameter (Ni and Huang 2018).
The underlying idea is that some recalls may occur sooner (or later)—in other words, some recalls are more “frail” than others (Ni and Huang 2018).
Following previous research (e.g., Box-Steffensmeier and De Boef 2006; Ni and Huang 2018), a Gamma distribution was used to model the recall-level frailty.
The results presented in Online Supplement Table A2 are consistent with the primary results.
Activist investor stock ownership has a negative, significant association with time-to-recall ( $\beta = -0.06$ , p = 0.032), even after accounting for unobserved heterogeneity across recalls.
<!-- para 15 -->
Shared frailty models were used to account for unobserved heterogeneity across firms.
The underlying idea is that some firms may recall sooner (or later)—in other words, some firms are more “frail” than others (Ni and Huang 2018).
Shared frailty models account for such differences by introducing a parameter that models the correlation within firms.
Following previous research (e.g., Box-Steffensmeier and De Boef 2006; Ni and Huang 2018), a Gamma distribution was used to model the firm-level frailty.
The results presented in Online Supplement Table A3 are largely consistent with the primary results. [^13] Activist investor stock ownership has a negative, significant association with time-to-recall ( $\beta = -0.06$ , p = 0.017), even after accounting for unobserved heterogeneity across firms.
<!-- para 17 -->
Two analyses were conducted to address potential endogeneity concerns related to reverse causality—i.e., activist investor stock ownership may be influenced by a firm's past recalls.
For example, activist investors may buy shares of a firm with a history of recalls or slow recall timing because such characteristics may indicate poor supply chain management and thus an opportunity to unlock hidden value by targeting the firm.
To address this, panelized fixed effects models were used with recent recalls, the natural logarithm of the average time-to-recall, and the vector of firm-level control variables to predict activist investor stock ownership. [^14] As shown in Online Supplement Table A4, the firm's recall history and past recall timing do not significantly influence activist investor stock ownership, thereby helping to alleviate reverse causality concerns.
<!-- para 18 -->
To further address reverse causality concerns, the primary analysis was replicated using activist investor stock ownership in the year prior to the recall year.
For example, if a firm initiated a recall in 2011, then this measure reflects the ownership stakes of activist investors that targeted other sample firms in 2010.
The results presented in Online Supplement Table A5 indicate that the lag of activist investor stock ownership has a negative, significant effect on time to recall ( $\beta = -0.05$ , p = 0.034), and this effect varies with defect type and defect severity.
Overall, the analysis helps to address reverse causality concerns and provides additional support for the hypotheses.
<!-- para 20 -->
The construction of activist investor stock ownership was deliberately conservative, as activist investors that could not be identified with certainty were excluded.
Consequently, the mean and standard deviation of activist investor stock ownership are weighted down by many observations with 0% ownership—the lower quartile, median, and upper quartile of activist investor stock ownership are 0%.
To ensure that this distribution did not unduly influence the conclusions, firms with activist investor ownership and those without activist investor ownership were compared.
Propensity score matching was used to create a matched sample in which each treatment observation (i.e., a firm with activist investor ownership) was matched with a control observation (i.e., a firm without activist investor ownership) that has similar observable characteristics (Wowak et al. 2021).
Following Wowak et al. (2021), the focal variable (in this study, activist investor stock ownership) is split at the median of zero.
The propensity score matching analysis used one-to-one matching, the full vector of independent variables, and time-to-recall as the dependent variable (Wowak et al. 2021).
The results indicate a negative, significant average treatment effect of -23.10 (p = 0.001) such that activist investor stock ownership is associated with a 23-day reduction in the average time-to-recall.
Put differently, firms with activist investor ownership greater than zero recall, on average, 23 days faster than firms with no activist investor ownership.
Overall, these results provide additional support for the core hypothesis.
<!-- para 22 -->
Recurrent-event AFT models were used in the primary analysis because they specifically examine how time-varying predictors influence the time to an event (Box-Steffensmeier et al. 2004; Ni and Huang 2018).
However, one limitation is that AFT models require specification of the underlying distribution, and it is possible that using a Weibull distribution may influence the results.
As such, the analysis was replicated using Cox proportional hazard (CPH) models.
CPH models examine how time-varying predictors influence the hazard of an event (i.e., recall), which differs slightly from this study's focus.
Importantly, however, CPH models do not require specification of the underlying distribution, which makes them a suitable option to examine the robustness of the results.
The results using CPH models are presented in Online Supplement Table A6 and are consistent with the primary results. [^15]
<!-- para 23 -->
AFT models may not fully account for the recurrent nature of recall events.
The marginal risk set model developed in Wei et al. (1989) overcomes this limitation by accounting for the sequence of recurrent recalls for each firm using stratification (Ni and Huang 2018).
The results using marginal risk set models are presented in Online Supplement Table A7 and are consistent with the primary results. [^16] Overall, both analyses indicate that the results are robust to different empirical strategies.

## discussion
<!-- para 3 -->
This study leveraged agency theory to understand whether and when the spillover effects of investor activism extend to a non-targeted firm's handling of product recalls.
While agency theory has commonly been used in supply chain research to understand buyer–supplier relationships (e.g., Kauppi et al. 2024), this study returns to the theory's foundations in understanding the relationship between shareholders and executives (Eisenhardt 1989).
In doing so, it highlights how visible supply chain actions such as product recalls might be used to manage the perceptions of stockholders (i.e., principals), who are increasingly “interested in the supply chains of their portfolio companies” (Straight 2024).
<!-- para 4 -->
A natural next step would be to examine the use of other supply chain actions and the potential role of supply chain partners in managing the perceptions of shareholders.
For example, an activist investor targeted Kohl's twice, criticizing its inventory practices and demanding that it separate its e-commerce and brick-and-mortar operations (Darby and Gordon 2021).
In response, other retailers in which the activist investor owns stock may try to push inventory upstream or better integrate processes with suppliers to improve their operating performance and avoid being targeted (e.g., Kim and Schoenherr 2018).
Supply chain scholars might also consider adopting a broader conceptualization of principals when using agency theory.
Beyond activist investors, several external stakeholders, like nongovernmental organizations, do not participate directly but can nonetheless influence supply chain actions and outcomes (Shirgholami et al. 2024).
Future research could also examine how supply chain operations might change when agents (i.e., executives and supply chain managers) view society or the environment as principals warranting consideration.
Such an approach would be consistent with calls to broaden the use of agency theory to reflect “a multipolar world that prioritizes collective wellbeing” (Luzzini et al. 2024, p. 97).
<!-- para 5 -->
This study appears to be the first to examine the spillover effects of activist investors on supply chain actions.
In doing so, it extends the notion of spillover from high-level, strategic considerations to the more practical arena of supply chain management amidst increased recognition of supply chain's importance in the C-suite and beyond (Flynn et al. 2021).
Hopefully, supply chain scholars will build on this study to examine whether other supply chain phenomena are directly or indirectly influenced by investor activism.
Do firms reduce excess inventory, reconfigure their supply base, or increase productivity in the presence of activist investors who have attacked other firms?
Manufacturing and inventory management issues appear to be central concerns for activist investors.
For example, an activist investor attempted to take over medical device company Merit Medical Systems' board of directors in the hopes of “improving operations” at its production facilities (Squire 2020).
Future research could examine whether firms alter their production or inventory in the hope of avoiding activists' wrath.
While firms might feel pressure to decrease production and inventories when activist investors appear among their shareholders, such pressures have to be balanced against the need for buffers to guard against uncertainty (Darby et al. 2020; Sodhi and Tang 2021).
What enables some firms to navigate these seemingly opposing pressures better than others could be a fruitful topic for future inquiry.
<!-- para 6 -->
Building on agency theory's notion of task characteristics (Eisenhardt 1989), this study also identified two recall characteristics that influence the ease with which executives (i.e., agents) try to manage the perceptions of activist investors (i.e., principals) by recalling quickly.
First, the spillover effect of activist investor stock ownership was stronger for products with design-related defects as compared to products with manufacturing-related defects.
At first glance, this may seem at odds with previous research examining the direct effect of defect type, finding that firms are faster to recall products with manufacturing-related defects as compared to design-related defects (Hora et al. 2011; Ni and Huang 2018).
This study also found a significant direct effect wherein manufacturing-related recalls are initiated more quickly than design-related recalls.
However, the significant interaction seems to suggest that there is a greater opportunity for the spillover effect of activist investor stock ownership to matter for design-related recalls because manufacturing-related recalls are initiated more quickly regardless of ownership.
Ultimately, each recall is a balancing act wherein a firm must weigh the implications of recall delays for consumer well-being against the potential for excess costs, errors, or waste that can arise from recalling too quickly (Ketchen et al. 2014; Ni and Huang 2018).
<!-- para 7 -->
The examination of defect severity revealed that the spillover effect of activist investor stock ownership was stronger for products with dangerous defects that can cause illness, injury, or death, as opposed to those with minor defects.
One interpretation is that supply chain managers wisely recognize that a defect with the potential to seriously harm consumers will draw negative attention from activist investors among their principals.
At the same time, activist investors likely view the costs associated with quickly addressing a minor defect as a needless expense (Zhu 2022).
Defect severity thus appears to be a dimension along which the interests of executives (i.e., agents), activist investors (i.e., principals), and consumers are aligned: All want to see dangerous products swiftly removed from the market, whereas products with nondangerous defects do not need to be a priority.
That said, an investigation into how activist investors might influence other aspects of quality management could also be beneficial because, ideally, products with dangerous defects would never enter the market.
A possible topic for future research is examining the extent to which, if any, the spillover effects of investor activism might extend to the development, production, and distribution of products so that dangerous products do not reach consumers in the first place (Roth et al. 2008).
<!-- para 9 -->
The regularity of product recalls has made them a prevalent aspect of the supply chain landscape, but slow recalls are all too common (Ni and Huang 2018; Wowak and Boone 2015).
In response, the FDA (2022) has called for firms to be “recall ready” to ensure prompt recalls.
While firms and regulators tend to fear activist investors, their ownership appears to be beneficial to society: This study found that just a 0.30% increase in activist investor ownership yields a 4-day reduction in recall initiation.
Recall delays can have deadly consequences, so initiating a recall as little as 4 days sooner is meaningful.
<!-- para 10 -->
Perhaps a more revealing practical finding is that medical product firms that have any activist investors among their stockholders recall products with dangerous defects an average of 23 days faster than firms with no activist investor stock ownership.
One implication is that firms and regulators interested in protecting consumer well-being perhaps should welcome the watchdog role that activist investors appear to play.
For executives and boards of directors, the findings indicate a need to better inform activist investors about supply chain failures such as product recalls.
A structural solution would be granting board seats to activist investors or their nominees, a change that activist investors often desire (e.g., the CytoDyn attack described above).
For example, the pharmaceutical company Bayer proactively appointed a prominent activist investor who previously targeted the firm to its board of directors before revealing its new strategy (Becker 2024).
A less radical move is to clearly communicate a recall's root cause to shareholders, such as through press releases or quarterly earnings calls—doing so may show operational prowess and credibility, potentially warding off being targeted due to supply chain failures.
Such disclosures also may help reduce information asymmetry between firm leadership and activist investors, who often target opaque firms (Bourveau and Schoenfeld 2017).
<!-- para 11 -->
For supply chain managers, an obvious implication is to recall defective products quickly, regardless of who owns shares of the firm's stock, as doing so is in the interests of the firm, stockholders, and society.
However, this impetus to recall quickly must be weighed against the potential for excess costs that come with recalling too quickly (Roth et al. 2008).
In response, supply chain managers may consider improving processes to develop a better understanding of why a defect occurred and exactly which batches are impacted, particularly for design-related defects, which tend to be more complex than manufacturing-related defects (Ni and Huang 2018).
A better understanding of the root cause can help provide clarity about the size and scope of a potential recall and may even lower recall costs by avoiding unnecessary returns of unaffected lots (Ketchen et al. 2014) and reducing waste (Russo et al. 2021).
<!-- para 12 -->
This study also offers policy implications for two important government agencies.
Implications for the FDA center on firm monitoring as a tool to improve recall responsiveness in the medical products sector.
Specifically, the FDA could consider expanding its criteria to include to what extent, if any, a firm has activist investors among its stockholders.
This publicly available data could inform the FDA about firms that might need extra scrutiny when defects occur and thereby help the agency allocate its limited monitoring resources.
Turning to the SEC, this study provides timely insights as the agency weighs possibly allowing investors to accumulate bigger stakes in individual firms before being required to disclose their ownership.
Critics argue that increasing the reporting threshold could allow activist investors to “go dark, build up positions in companies in secret, and then ambush unsuspecting managers” (Platt 2020).
A related question is whether allowing activist investors to “go dark” might attenuate potentially beneficial spillover effects, such as the recall-quickening effect, because firms will not feel threatened if they are unaware that activist investors appear among their stockholders.
Most importantly, the SEC should not only consider the direct effects on targeted firms but also the indirect spillover effects on non-targeted firms when deliberating regulatory changes.
<!-- para 14 -->
Obtaining data via a FOIA request allowed for the construction of a unique dataset, but the lengthy delays involved in obtaining data in this manner limit the time horizon.
Moreover, the use of archival data cannot tap into how supply chain managers attend to ownership by activist investors.
The analyses uncovered evidence consistent with hypotheses built on agency theory by illustrating that firms take proactive steps—i.e., recalling more quickly—in the hopes of avoiding being targeted, but proactive steps also could include eliminating “bad” managers or improving quality management practices (e.g., Roth et al. 2008).
Scholars should consider leveraging qualitative methods (e.g., Wieland et al. 2024) to supplement this archival data and gain a richer understanding of the underlying mechanisms.
<!-- para 15 -->
Because the identification of activist investors was deliberately conservative following previous research (Shi et al. 2020), the measure of activist investor stock ownership may underestimate their presence.
Indeed, it is weighted down by many observations with 0% ownership, which precludes the examination of potential heterogeneity across activist investors.
Scholars should consider such heterogeneity when examining the spillover effects of investor activism on other supply chain phenomena such as inventory management and manufacturing productivity, which should have the sample size and variation to do so.
Relatedly, there was a limited number of firms that had been targeted by activist investors in the final sample.
This precluded the examination of direct effects of activist investor attacks.
Scholars might consider interviews or simulations to build understanding of how previous attacks also might influence a firm's recall strategy.
<!-- para 16 -->
The categorization of defect types was aligned with extant recall research (e.g., Ni and Huang 2018) and the reasons for recall described in the FDA dataset.
However, future research might consider other categorizations, such as defects stemming from supplier-related issues upstream or user-related issues downstream (Hora et al. 2011).
While the focus on medical product recalls was desirable to explore trade-offs within a sector where product defects have troubling societal consequences (Wieland 2021), it remains unknown whether these findings generalize to other recall-laden sectors such as food (Ketchen et al. 2014) and automotive (Ni and Huang 2018).
It seems reasonable to expect that spillover effects should occur in other sectors because investor activism is intentionally public and widely recognized by firms (Ahn and Wiersema 2019), but additional research is needed to examine whether the relationships uncovered here hold elsewhere.
