# darby_2026_toward_faster_recalls_of_dangerous_medical_device 句子库存

## introduction
<!-- para 1 -->
Given that OSCM centers on the transformation of inputs into products and services (Bendoly and Oliva 2024), a large body of research has focused on the interrelationships between product-level factors and firm-level operations.
While this literature spans a variety of topics, product-level factors related to product risk (e.g., Speier et al. 2011), risks in product design (e.g., Srai et al. 2025), and risks associated with new product introductions (e.g., Choo et al. 2021) have been a recurrent theme.
<!-- para 2 -->
In the medical device industry, product risks are reflected in the device classification determined by the FDA.
Class III devices pose high risks to patients and/or users, whereas Classes I and II devices pose low to moderate risks (FDA 2017b).
It requires “a high degree of task-specific knowledge” (Bendoly et al. 2025, 10) to fully understand the risks associated with Class III devices.
Executives are regularly informed of such risks (e.g., Wowak et al. 2021; Darby et al. 2023) and have discretion over how much information they choose to disclose to investors.
This aligns with the concept of information asymmetry (Eisenhardt 1989), which led us to examine whether device class might also play a moderating role.

## theory
<!-- para 3 -->
The recall decision is a “minefield of conflicting objectives” (Ball et al. 2022, 6), as executives try to serve the needs of the firm, their own personal interests, and the interests of consumers, regulators, and shareholders.
In general, executives have a natural tendency to avoid personal losses to their income, reputation, or career (Wowak et al. 2015).
As a result, executives may shy away from risky initiatives, pursue inefficient strategies, or delay bad news disclosures (Kothari et al. 2009).
Regarding recalls in particular, previous research and anecdotal evidence illustrate the potential for self-serving behavior by executives (Ball et al. 2022; Darby et al. 2023).
<!-- para 4 -->
Agency theory, as described by Jensen and Meckling (1976) and Eisenhardt (1989), explains this tendency.
Small firms are typically owned and managed by the same individuals, so there are no conflicts of interest between owners and managers.
Large publicly traded firms, however, have thousands of different owners ranging from large institutional investors to individual investors who may own just a handful of shares.
It is not feasible for all of the owners to be actively involved in managing the firm, so they rely on executives to manage the firm on their behalf.
While this separation of ownership and control has obvious advantages, it creates an agency problem wherein the goals of those who own the firm (e.g., profit maximization) and the goals of those who manage the firm (e.g., maximization of executive pay) can conflict (Jensen and Meckling 1976).
In theory, executives have a fiduciary responsibility to make decisions that are in shareholders' best interests (Eisenhardt 1989).
When confronted with dilemmas, however, executives sometimes prioritize their own interests over those of shareholders.
With recalls, for example, executives may downplay the severity of a product defect to protect their own interests, even if doing so puts the firm and consumers at greater risk (Ball et al. 2022; Darby et al. 2023).
<!-- para 5 -->
Agency theory suggests that shareholders have two key levers to mitigate this executive “mischief” (Dalton et al. 2007, 1): (i) executive compensation and (ii) monitoring and control.
Extant research has already examined the first lever, executive compensation, in product recalls (Wowak et al. 2015; Liu et al. 2016; Darby et al. 2023), so we take a logical next step by examining the second lever.
When engaging in monitoring and control, shareholders pay careful attention to whether executives make choices aligned with shareholders' goals (Cheung et al. 2020).
Relative to other shareholders, large institutional investors have stronger incentives to be engaged due to the size of their ownership stakes and their long-term investment horizons (McCahery et al. 2016).
Large institutional investors also have the resources necessary to bear the costs of monitoring (Dharwadkar et al. 2008).
Indeed, Lewellen and Lewellen (2022, 232) found that “the estimates suggest that large institutions should be willing to invest significant resources to improve the performance of firms they hold, consistent with recent evidence that large institutions take an active role in governance.” As the head of corporate governance for State Street Global Advisors summarized, “our size, experience and long-term outlook provide us with corporate access and allow us to establish and maintain an open and constructive dialogue with company management and boards” (Scott 2014, para. 11).
Building on this notion, we theorize that ownership by large institutional investors will shape how executives handle recalls.
<!-- para 6 -->
As boundedly rational actors (Eisenhardt 1989), executives should focus on the concerns of the most powerful shareholders.
In describing the motives of institutional investors, a vice president of a publicly traded healthcare company told us, “there are some bottom feeders who just worry about money, but most want to be associated with good quality products.
They view product recalls pretty seriously.” He explained that this is because an institutional investor’s reputation can be damaged “if consumers are harmed.” In other words, the longer the delay in recalling defective products, the greater the potential for harm to the firm and its investors.
This is particularly the case for large institutional investors like BlackRock, Vanguard, Fidelity, and State Street, who have sizable ownership stakes and are focused on long-term value (Fink 2022).
As Hora et al. (2011, 768) explain, recall delays “may cause numerous injuries and even deaths.
In those cases, questions begin to be raised by the stakeholders about the delay, and restoring the lost reputation becomes more challenging for firms.”
<!-- para 7 -->
With these ramifications in mind, we theorize that firms will feel pressure to recall defective products quickly to the extent that large institutional investors have stock ownership stakes.
This is not to say that large institutional investors will make or even participate in recall decisions, but they will use their "voice" to indirectly influence such decisions.
As described in an earlier quote, large institutional investors are not interested in managing portfolio firms, but they do want to provide oversight and input (McNabb 2015).
This is similar to the premise underlying Wowak et al. (2021): large institutional investors, like boards of directors, do not make recall decisions but exert influence indirectly by overseeing these decisions.
As principals who have delegated decision-making to their agents (i.e., executives), large institutional investors may indirectly influence recall timing by monitoring the firm's handling of decisions related to product quality failures.
Specifically, we expect that higher levels of ownership by large institutional investors will be associated with faster recalls because of the increased influence that comes with a greater proportion of ownership.
Large ownership stakes amplify the "voice" of these already influential institutional investors (Connelly et al. 2010).
We thus theorize that firms with higher levels of ownership by large institutional investors will recall products with dangerous defects more quickly.
Stated formally:
<!-- para 8 -->
> [!hypothesis]
> **Hypothesis 1.** Ownership by large institutional investors is negatively associated with time-to-recall such that the greater the percentage of stock ownership by large institutional investors, the faster the recall.
<!-- para 10 -->
Information asymmetry is central to agency theory because it underlies the agency problems that arise in principal–agent relationships (Eisenhardt 1989).
As noted above, agents often possess more and better information than principals, which makes it difficult for principals to monitor agents and assess whether agents are acting appropriately.
This opens the door for the agency problem of moral hazard, wherein agents prioritize their own self-interests over the principal's interests (Bendoly et al. 2025).
As the information asymmetry between principals and agents grows, agents may become more self-serving (Bergh et al. 2019).
<!-- para 11 -->
As principals, large institutional investors are not directly involved in a firm's operations, so they have incomplete information about firm decisions (Cheung et al. 2020).
This is particularly the case for product recalls: large institutional investors are not directly involved in recall decisions, but they may indirectly influence recall timing by monitoring how recalls are handled.
Although executives have a fiduciary duty to disclose potential risks, such as the discovery of product defects, to investors, they also have discretion over how much information they disclose and the specificity of that information (e.g., Malik et al. 2025).
This is due, in part, to “the inherent subjectivity” of the recall decision-making process (Ball et al. 2018a, 2).
Executives therefore possess more and better information about product recalls than large institutional investors do.
<!-- para 12 -->
As information asymmetry increases, it becomes increasingly difficult for large institutional investors to assess whether product recalls are being handled appropriately (Bergh et al. 2019).
This increases the potential for moral hazard which, in our study's context, could take the form of executives delaying product recalls to avoid immediate financial losses or protect their jobs and reputation (Darby et al. 2023).
We thus expect that increases in information asymmetry will weaken the negative association between large institutional investor ownership and time-to-recall described in Hypothesis 1.
<!-- para 13 -->
We examine this possibility using two moderating variables: R&D intensity and device class.
Both reflect information asymmetry, but they operate through different pathways and at different levels (Bendoly and Oliva 2025).
R&D intensity is a firm-level characteristic capturing the overall emphasis that a firm places on innovation (Mackelprang et al. 2015).
Information asymmetry arises from the ramifications of R&D activities for the firm's operations.
In contrast, device class is a product-level characteristic capturing the risks associated with particular medical devices.
At this level, information asymmetry arises from the opacity surrounding product risks, as task-specific knowledge is required to understand the root cause and potential ramifications of defects in high-risk devices (Ball et al. 2018a).
<!-- para 15 -->
A firm's R&D intensity reflects a firm's emphasis on innovation (Mackelprang et al. 2015), which may be reflected in new products and/or new processes (Zou et al. 2023).
When R&D intensity is low, the firm's operations are relatively straightforward and tend to be consistent over time (Yiu et al. 2020).
This makes it easier for large institutional investors to understand the firm's operations and monitor its handling of product recalls because the information acquisition process is relatively unfettered by R&D and accompanying activities.
In other words, when information asymmetry is low, it is easier for large institutional investors to fulfill the monitoring function described in Hypothesis 1 and assess whether executives handle a defect appropriately.
<!-- para 16 -->
As R&D intensity increases, however, so too does the unpredictability of a firm's operations (Yiu et al. 2020).
This makes it increasingly difficult for large institutional investors to monitor the firm's operations and assess the appropriateness of executives' actions.
This is due, in part, to the fact that R&D investments can be allocated in myriad ways within a firm's operations, which can be difficult for outsiders to observe (Baruffaldi et al. 2024).
Thus, as R&D intensity increases, so too does information asymmetry because investors have less insight into how R&D investments are allocated and their potential impact on the firm's operations and quality management.
While large institutional investors can obtain and evaluate high-level information on R&D investments, extant research indicates that "information acquisition by outsiders will generally fall short" (Aboody and Lev 2000, 2752) because outsiders lack necessary nuance.
This information asymmetry makes it more challenging for large institutional investors to monitor the firm's operations and assess whether recalls are handled appropriately.
<!-- para 17 -->
The increase in information asymmetry creates greater potential for moral hazard.
Executives can hide behind the complexity and unpredictability of innovative operations that are facilitated by R&D investments to pursue their own self-interests (Bergh et al. 2019) and delay product recalls.
For example, large institutional investors might question a recall's handling during a private discussion or in a quarterly earnings conference call.
In response, executives could blame recall delays on an array of operational initiatives associated with their emphasis on R&D, such as integrated quality management processes (Yiu et al. 2020) or flexible manufacturing (e.g., Mackelprang et al. 2015).
Given the information asymmetry, it would be difficult for large institutional investors to effectively assess whether the delay was warranted.
In sum, we theorize that R&D intensity will increase information asymmetry and thus weaken the negative association between large institutional investor ownership and time-to-recall.
Stated formally:
<!-- para 18 -->
> [!hypothesis]
> **Hypothesis 2.** R&D intensity moderates the negative association between ownership by large institutional investors and time-to-recall.
The recall-quickening association is weaker when R&D intensity is high than when R&D intensity is low.
<!-- para 20 -->
The FDA (2017b) classifies medical devices into one of three classes depending on the risk of the device to patients and/or users: Classes I and II devices pose low to moderate risks, whereas Class III devices pose high risks.
While Class III devices can be more complex than Class I and II devices (e.g., implantable cardioverter defibrillators versus bandages), the two are not always highly correlated.
For example, a laboratory centrifuge is a complex device that consists of many different elements, but it poses low risks to patients and thus is a Class I device (FDA 2026a).
In contrast, a coronary stent is a simple device that consists of a small number of elements (i.e., a metal scaffold), but it is a Class III device because of the high risks it poses to patients (FDA 2026b).
The FDA's classification of medical devices thus centers on device risk, not device complexity (FDA 2017a).
Accordingly, we theorize about how information asymmetry arises from the risks—rather than the complexity—associated with a medical device.
<!-- para 21 -->
When defects arise in Class I and II devices, the ramifications for patients and/or users are straightforward to understand and do not require specific knowledge or expertise.
For example, both executives and large institutional investors can readily assess the implications of a defect in a Class I or Class II device, such as when manufacturing errors result in surgical gauze contaminated with foreign matter (Class I) or a non-sterile catheter (Class II).
The low information asymmetry surrounding low-to-moderate risk devices makes it easier for large institutional investors to fulfill the monitoring function described in Hypothesis 1 and evaluate whether executives handled recalls involving Class I and II devices appropriately.
<!-- para 22 -->
However, when defects arise in Class III devices, it becomes increasingly challenging for large institutional investors to evaluate the defect's implications.
This is, in part, because Class III devices are often life-sustaining or life-supporting devices that are implanted in patients (FDA 2017b).
Consider, for example, a coronary stent that does not deploy or expand as intended.
Identifying the root cause and ramifications of a defect in such high-risk devices—and how the firm should handle the recall—can be particularly challenging because of the device's interaction with the human body (Ball et al. 2018a).
Executives are close to the recall process and possess domain-specific expertise about these devices that is difficult for outsiders—even large institutional investors with sophisticated monitoring capabilities—to obtain and understand (e.g., Ball et al. 2022).
The risks associated with Class III devices thus exacerbate the problem of information asymmetry between executives, who possess task-specific knowledge, and large institutional investors, who lack such knowledge.
<!-- para 23 -->
When information asymmetry between executives and large institutional investors is high, monitoring becomes difficult and expensive (Bergh et al. 2019; Bendoly et al. 2025), thereby increasing the potential for moral hazard.
If questioned by large institutional investors, executives could simply frame a delay as due diligence aimed at limiting unnecessary costs or errors, such as recalling the wrong product or recalling unnecessary lots of the right product (Ketchen et al. 2014; Ball et al. 2022).
This is especially true given that Class III devices are often life-sustaining, life-supporting, or implanted in users (FDA 2017b), which makes it increasingly challenging for large institutional investors to assess whether such delays are warranted.
While Class III devices may be subject to high levels of scrutiny because of their associated risks, it is also extremely difficult for large institutional investors to assess whether executives are handling recalls involving high-risk devices appropriately.
In sum, we theorize that information asymmetry will be greater for high-risk (Class III) devices than for low-to-moderate risk (Class I and II) devices.
We thus expect that device class will weaken the negative association between large institutional investor ownership and time-to-recall.
Stated formally:
<!-- para 24 -->
> [!hypothesis]
> **Hypothesis 3.** Device class moderates the negative association between ownership by large institutional investors and time-to-recall.
The negative association is weaker for Class III than for Class I and II devices.

## methods
<!-- para 3 -->
Our data collection began with a Freedom of Information Act (FOIA) request to the FDA for data on medical device recalls.
The data included information about the recalling firm, the defect awareness date, the recall initiation date, the number of units recalled, the scope of distribution, and the recall severity classification.
Regarding the latter, the FDA categorizes each recall into one of three classes, depending on the severity of the product defect.
Class I and II recalls involve major defects that can cause temporary illness, serious harm, or even death, whereas Class III recalls involve minor defects such as packaging or labeling errors that pose no harm to patients and/or users but nonetheless violate FDA regulations and warrant a recall (FDA 2020b).
Importantly, the recall severity classification is determined separately from the medical device classification.
A recall's severity is determined based on the product defect's potential to harm consumers (FDA 2020b), whereas the medical device classification is determined based on the device's potential risks to consumers (FDA 2017a).
<!-- para 4 -->
Consistent with past recall studies (Ball et al. 2018b; Darby et al. 2023), we considered Class I and II as high severity recalls and used them to test our hypotheses for two key reasons.
First, high severity recalls are prevalent in this sector and have considerable ramifications for consumer health and safety (Wowak et al. 2021), so understanding factors that influence how quickly firms initiate high severity recalls has important implications.
Second, high severity recalls have a range of adverse consequences that matter to large institutional investors, including increased costs, lost sales, reduced credibility, tarnished reputation, erosion of market value, and the potential for litigation (e.g., Hora et al. 2011; Ball et al. 2022).
<!-- para 5 -->
We matched the recall data with additional information from the FDA's Medical Device Recall database as well as firm-level data for our independent variable, moderating variables, and control variables from the Thomson Reuters Institutional (13f) Holdings, [^3] COMPUSTAT, and Institutional Shareholder Services (ISS) databases.
The intersection of these datasets resulted in a sample of 2982 high severity recalls across 69 publicly traded firms from 2002 to 2020.
As might be expected, the majority of firms in our sample operate primarily in the medical device industry (SIC 384).
However, the sample was not limited to this industry because firms may be formally classified in other industries (e.g., Proctor & Gamble in SIC 284 or General Electric in SIC 999) but still produce—and potentially recall—medical devices.
To ensure that the sample is not unduly influencing our results, we conducted an additional analysis that limited the sample to only firms classified in the medical device industry (SIC 384).
The results presented in Appendix Table A1 are consistent with our primary results.
<!-- para 8 -->
Large Institutional Ownership was measured as the total percentage of outstanding shares owned by large institutional investors (e.g., Dharwadkar et al. 2008) using data obtained from the Thomson Reuters Institutional (13f) Holdings database.
In line with our theorizing, we focus on BlackRock, Fidelity, Vanguard, and State Street because these four institutional investors collectively control the majority of shares in publicly traded firms (Lewellen and Lewellen 2022).
For example, Abbott Laboratories has nearly 4000 institutional investors with these four accounting for around 20% of its shares.
Likewise, Medtronic has nearly 3000 institutional investors with these four owning around 20%.
To match our dataset, we aggregated the quarterly data to the annual level based on the defect awareness date by averaging the measure across all four quarters for each firm each year.
We then centered and standardized the measure to enhance interpretability.
<!-- para 10 -->
Time-to-Recall was measured as the number of days between when a firm first became aware of a potential defect (i.e., the defect awareness date) and when the firm initiated a recall (i.e., the recall initiation date), as reported in the FDA data (Wowak et al. 2021; Darby et al. 2023).
Time-to-Recall was logarithmically transformed because, like many measures of time, it was not normally distributed.
It was logged as part of our estimation approach described below, so it was not transformed a priori.
<!-- para 12 -->
R&D Intensity was measured using data from COMPUSTAT's Fundamentals Annual database as the ratio of R&D expenses to total sales (e.g., Dong et al. 2020).
Following previous research (e.g., Kim and Zhu 2018), we excluded observations with missing values for R&D expenses rather than interpolating values.
The construction of our dataset yielded only 70 recall observations with missing values for R&D expenses.
We centered and standardized the measure to enhance interpretability.
<!-- para 13 -->
Device Class was measured using the FDA's medical device classification (e.g., Darby et al. 2023).
Class I devices such as bandages pose low risks to patients, Class II devices such as syringes pose moderate risks, and Class III devices such as pacemakers pose high risks (FDA 2017b).
In line with this distinction, we measured Device Class as a binary variable equal to one for Class III medical devices and zero for Class I and II medical devices.
<!-- para 15 -->
We included a broad set of control variables that influence recalls directly and those that help address alternative explanations (Shang and Rönkkö 2022); in our case, variables correlated with ownership by large institutional investors that may also influence the time-to-recall.
We first included recall-level factors that may influence how the recall is handled.
To address alternative explanations stemming from a recall's size and scope, we included Units Recalled, measured as the natural logarithm of the number of units recalled (Ni and Huang 2018), and Distribution Pattern, measured as an ordinal variable that reflects the scope of product distribution (i.e., select states, nationwide, or international—Wowak et al. 2016).
We also included Recent Recalls, measured as the number of high severity recalls (i.e., Class I and II) a firm initiated in the previous year to control for the firm's recall experience (Wowak et al. 2021).
Large institutional investors may be sensitive to a recall's reputational effects (e.g., McNabb 2015), so it is important to control for the firm's recall history as well as the scale and scope of a particular recall.
<!-- para 16 -->
We also controlled for executive characteristics that have been shown to influence recalls using data from COMPUSTAT's Execucomp Annual Compensation database.
In doing so, we aimed to address alternative explanations related to executive risk-taking and compensation, which are important agency considerations for large institutional investors (Hartzell and Starks 2003).
CEO Tenure was measured as the number of years between when the individual became CEO and when the recall was initiated (Mayo et al. 2022).
CEO Stock Ownership was measured as the percentage of shares outstanding held by the firm's CEO, excluding options (Mayo et al. 2022; Darby et al. 2023).
CEO Total Compensation was measured as the logarithm of the CEO's total compensation as reported in SEC filings, including salary, bonus, stock awards, options, non-equity incentives, pensions, and other compensation (e.g., Wowak et al. 2015).
Beyond the CEO, we also controlled for CSCO Presence using a binary variable equal to one if there is a CSCO on the TMT during the recall year and zero otherwise.
Following Roh et al. (2016), we used a two-pronged procedure to identify CSCOs based on titles provided in COMPUSTAT's Execucomp database.
Specifically, we identified CSCOs as executives with titles of (i) "Chief Supply Chain Officer" or "CSCO" and (ii) titles containing "Supply Chain" along with "Corporate," "Chief," or "Executive Vice President" (Roh et al. 2016, 53).
<!-- para 17 -->
We also controlled for firm-level characteristics that have been shown to influence recalls as well as firm stock ownership.
Using data from COMPUSTAT's Fundamentals Annual database, we controlled for Sales (the natural logarithm of total sales) and for firm performance using Return on Equity (the ratio of net income to equity).
We also included Debt Ratio (the ratio of total debt to total assets) because firms with large debt holdings may face differential pressures from large institutional investors (Shi et al. 2020).
<!-- para 18 -->
Agency theory suggests that the board of directors plays an important monitoring role as well (Eisenhardt 1989; Barker et al. 2024), and board structure is a key consideration for large institutional investors (Appel et al. 2016).
Using data from the ISS database, we controlled for two board characteristics shown to influence recalls and executive actions: CEO-Chair Separation and Female Board Representation.
CEO-Chair Separation was measured as a dichotomous variable equal to one if the CEO and chair of the board are different individuals and zero if the CEO is the chair of the board.
If a director other than the CEO serves as the chair, the board may be able to better monitor the CEO (Wowak et al. 2015).
We also included Female Board Representation, measured as the proportion of females on the board of directors, because it has been shown to influence the frequency and timing of recalls (Wowak et al. 2021).
<!-- para 19 -->
The influence of institutional investors derives from both the number of shares they own and the extent to which shares are concentrated (Hoskisson et al. 2002).
To address alternative explanations surrounding avenues of influence, we controlled for the concentration of ownership stakes by institutional investors using a measure obtained from the WRDS Thomson Reuters Institutional (13f) Stock Ownership database.
Institutional Ownership Concentration was measured using a Herfindahl–Hirschman Index (the sum of the squared percentages of shares held by each institutional investor in each firm). [^4] Specifically: $\sum_{j}^{n} \frac{\text{Shares}_{ijq}}{\text{Total Shares}_{ijq}}^{2}$, where Shares reflects the number of adjusted shares of firm i held by institutional investor j in quarter q and Total Shares reflects the total number of shares outstanding for firm i in quarter q.
To match our dataset, we aggregated the measure by averaging the index across all four quarters for each firm in each year.
<!-- para 20 -->
Lastly, we included firm and year fixed effects to account for changes in stock markets and a firm's operating environment over time as well as time-invariant heterogeneity (Ball et al. 2017).
Tables 2 and 3 provide the recall-level descriptive statistics and correlations, respectively; Appendix Table A2 provides firm-level descriptive statistics for completeness.
As illustrated in Table 3, Large Institutional Ownership is negatively and significantly correlated with Time-to-Recall (-0.16*).
Moreover, our moderating variables of R&D Intensity and Device Class are positively correlated as expected but not perfectly correlated (0.22*), which underscores the value of examining factors at both the firm and product levels (Bendoly and Oliva 2025).
<!-- para 22 -->
Our theorizing centers on factors influencing the time-to-recall, which varies because each recall is different and requires careful consideration (Ball et al. 2022).
We thus followed recent studies and conducted our analysis at the recall level (Wowak et al. 2021; Darby et al. 2023).
Specifically, we relied on AFT models, which are used to examine how time-varying variables influence the time to an event of interest—that is, the time to a recall (Wei 1992).
Some firms experienced multiple recalls over the sample period, so we used recurrent-event AFT models with robust standard errors clustered by firm and a Weibull distribution (Box-Steffensmeier et al. 2004; Darby et al. 2023).
<!-- para 24 -->
While AFT models account for the fact that the likelihood of an event (i.e., a recall) changes with the passage of time due to underlying factors (Bhattacharjee et al. 2007), they may not account for characteristics that influence a firm's ownership stakes and the time-to-recall.
To address concerns related to selection on observable characteristics, we first processed our data using CEM (Yilmaz et al. 2024).
<!-- para 25 -->
The underlying goal of CEM is to achieve better balance between treatment and control observations by generating strata based on a set of matching covariates (Iacus et al. 2012).
Following previous research (e.g., Oehmichen et al. 2021; Wowak et al. 2021), we used a median split of the focal variable (Large Institutional Ownership) for the treatment.
The treatment group consists of recall observations for firms with Large Institutional Ownership at or above the median ownership percentage of 18.29%, whereas the control group consists of recalls for firms with below-median Large Institutional Ownership.
<!-- para 26 -->
We selected Recent Recalls as the primary matching covariate to address firm characteristics that may influence ownership by large institutional investors (i.e., the treatment) as well as the time-to-recall (i.e., the outcome).

For the primary analysis, the aim was to sufficiently account for heterogeneity while also ensuring sufficient sample size—in other words, “the practice of conducting CEM on a parsimonious set of covariates to avoid large sample loss” (Yilmaz et al. 2024, 844).
As described in the robustness checks, we iteratively examined three additional vectors of matching covariates—as well as conducted two propensity score matching analyses using the full vector of firm-level covariates—and obtained consistent results.
<!-- para 35 -->
The primary matching covariate, Recent Recalls, was coarsened using the automated algorithm (Iacus et al. 2012; Yilmaz et al. 2024).
This process yielded 9 matched strata containing 1436 treated observations and 1496 control observations for a total of 2932 observations.
Weights that capture the level of similarity between the treatment and control observations within each stratum then were used to weight the AFT models (Yilmaz et al. 2024).
<!-- para 36 -->
Unlike other matching techniques (e.g., propensity score matching), there is “typically no need to check the common support or balance of coarsened values” for CEM (Yilmaz et al. 2024, 841).
However, an imbalance measure, $L_{1}$, is often used to quantify the distance between the treatment and control groups across covariates.
This measure is “not valuable on its own, but rather as a point of comparison between matching solutions” (Blackwell et al. 2010, 6).
The overall $L_{1}$ measure decreases from 0.330 before matching to 0.134 after matching, which indicates an improvement in covariate balance.
Table 4 presents the univariate $L_{1}$ measures and mean differences between treatment and control groups.
The decreases in univariate $L_{1}$ measures and mean differences suggest better balance across covariates after matching (e.g., Cornelius et al. 2021; Lee et al. 2021).

## results
<!-- para 3 -->
To test Hypothesis 2, we added the interaction of R&D Intensity $\times$ Large Institutional Ownership in Model 3 (e.g., Lee et al. 2021).
R&D Intensity significantly moderates the association between Large Institutional Ownership and Time-to-Recall ($\beta = 0.36$, $p < 0.05$).
As predicted, the positive coefficient indicates that R&D Intensity weakens the association between ownership by large institutional investors and time-to-recall.
To help illustrate this moderation effect, Figure 1 depicts the interaction using the marginal effects of large institutional ownership and the 10th and 90th percentile to depict high and low values of R&D intensity (e.g., Skowronski et al. 2022).
When R&D intensity is low (the dashed line), time-to-recall decreases substantially as large institutional ownership increases.
However, when R&D intensity is high (the solid line), the association between large institutional ownership and time-to-recall is almost flat, with only moderate reductions in time-to-recall as large institutional ownership increases.
In other words, the negative association between Large Institutional Ownership and Time-to-Recall weakens with increases in R&D intensity.
<!-- para 4 -->
To test Hypothesis 3, we added the interaction of Device Class $\times$ Large Institutional Ownership in Model 4.
Device Class significantly moderates the association between Large Institutional Ownership and Time-to-Recall ($\beta = 0.27$, $p < 0.01$).
As predicted, the positive coefficient indicates that the association between ownership by large institutional investors and time-to-recall is weaker for high-risk (Class III) medical devices relative to low-to-moderate risk (Class I and II) medical devices.
To help illustrate this moderation effect, Figure 2 depicts the marginal effects of large institutional ownership for low-to-moderate risk medical devices versus high-risk medical devices.
The negative association between large institutional ownership and time-to-recall is steeper for low-to-moderate risk medical devices (the dashed line) than for high-risk medical devices (the solid line).
<!-- para 5 -->
To provide a more conservative test of our moderating hypotheses, Model 5 includes both interactions.
R&D Intensity and Device Class continue to significantly moderate the negative association between Large Institutional Ownership and Time-to-Recall ($\beta = 0.34$, $p < 0.05$ and $\beta = 0.19$, $p < 0.05$, respectively).

There are thousands of institutional investors with different ownership stakes and investment horizons, so they vary in terms of their engagement with portfolio firms (McCahery et al. 2016).
Our study centers on large institutional investors, who we theorize should have the incentives to monitor portfolio firms due to the size of their ownership stakes and their long-term investment horizons (e.g., Dalton et al. 2007; Lewellen and Lewellen 2022).
This is consistent with previous research (e.g., McCahery et al. 2016; Lewellen and Lewellen 2022) and anecdotal evidence (e.g., McNabb 2015; Fink 2022).
To explore these assumptions more formally, we conducted additional analyses using alternative measures that reflect different magnitudes of ownership and different investment horizons.
In doing so, we shed light on the mechanisms underlying the association between institutional ownership and time-to-recall across a broader constituency of institutional investors.
<!-- para 25 -->
To examine whether monitoring varies with the magnitude of ownership stakes, we used three alternative measures: One Percenters' Ownership, Largest Five Ownership, and Blockholder Ownership.
One Percenters' Ownership is the percentage of shares outstanding held by institutional investors owning at least 1% of the firm's shares (e.g., Cheung et al. 2020).
This measure was constructed using data from the Thomson Reuters Institutional (13f) Holdings database.
Largest Five Ownership is the percentage of shares outstanding owned by the five institutional investors with the highest percentage of ownership in each firm in each year (Hartzell and Starks 2003), and Blockholder Ownership is the percentage of shares outstanding by institutional investors who own at least 5% of a firm's shares—known as blockholders.
Both measures were obtained from the WRDS Thomson Reuters Institutional (13f) Stock Ownership database.
The underlying rationale is that these alternative measures allow us to examine the largest institutional investors within each firm as opposed to the largest institutional investors across firms more broadly (e.g., our primary measure).
<!-- para 26 -->
Appendix Tables H1–H3 present the results.
One Percenters' Ownership, Largest Five Ownership, and Blockholder Ownership have negative, significant associations with time-to-recall.
When considered alongside our primary results, these results suggest that ownership by large institutional investors and institutional investors with large ownership stakes matters when it comes to product recalls.
Turning to the moderators, the results indicate that R&D Intensity and Device Class have positive but not significant associations for One Percenters' Ownership.
In contrast, the moderating association of R&D Intensity is positive and significant for both Largest Five Ownership and Blockholder Ownership.
Interestingly, however, the moderating association of Device Class is positive as expected but not significant for either measure.
One possible explanation stems from potential differences in engagement between large institutional investors (in terms of assets under management) and institutional investors with large ownership stakes.
In other words, the Big Four institutional investors we focus on are large in terms of assets under management and in terms of ownership stakes within a firm, whereas these alternative measures focus on institutional investors that are large in terms of ownership stakes within a firm.
Perhaps firm-level R&D intensity is better understood because it is on a firm's balance sheet, whereas product-level device class is less easily understood and requires more meaningful engagement with the firm to reduce information asymmetry.
<!-- para 28 -->
To probe whether monitoring varies with investment horizons, we examined ownership by different types of institutional investors (e.g., Connelly et al. 2010; Helmuth et al. 2023).
Specifically, we used the Institutional Investor Classification Data obtained from Brian Bushee's Wharton Accounting website (Bushee 2001) to classify institutional investors as dedicated, quasi-indexer, or transient and data from the Thomson Reuters Institutional (13f) Holdings database to measure the total percentage of shares outstanding owned by each type.
<!-- para 29 -->
Dedicated institutional investors are those with large average investments in portfolio firms, extremely low portfolio turnover, and long-term investment horizons.
This dedicated approach is “consistent with a ‘relationship investing’ role and a commitment to provide long-term patient capital” (Bushee 2001, 214).
Similarly, quasi-indexers are institutional investors with diversified holdings, low portfolio turnover, and medium- to long-term investment horizons (Bushee 2001).
This approach is consistent with a “passive, buy-and-hold strategy of investing portfolio funds in a broad set of firms” (Bushee 2001, 214).
In contrast, transient institutional investors are those with highly diversified holdings, high portfolio turnover, and short-term investment horizons.
This approach is driven by the desire for profits from short-term trading (Bushee 2001).
We thus examined ownership by dedicated and quasi-indexer institutional investors together versus ownership by transient institutional investors (Bushee 2001).
The underlying idea is that both dedicated institutional investors and quasi-indexers have longer-term investment horizons, whereas transient institutional investors have short-term horizons (Bushee 2001).
<!-- para 30 -->
The results presented in Appendix Table H4 indicate that Dedicated + Quasi-Indexer Ownership has a negative, significant association with time-to-recall—greater ownership by institutional investors with longer-term investment horizons is associated with faster recalls.
Turning to our moderators, the moderating association of R&D Intensity is positive but not significant, whereas the moderating association of Device Class is positive and significant.
In general, these findings complement the findings above in that ownership by institutional investors with long-term investment horizons is associated with faster recalls (e.g., Lewellen and Lewellen 2022).
These findings also shed additional light on the underlying mechanism by revealing a potential reason why.
In light of their investment horizons, dedicated institutional investors and quasi-indexers tend to focus on long-term value rather than short-term stock price changes (Bushee 2001).
As a result, they may be more sensitive to the long-term consequences of recall delays, such as consumer harm, reduced credibility, tarnished brand equity, and erosion of market value (e.g., Ball et al. 2022).
<!-- para 31 -->
In contrast, the results presented in Appendix Table H5 indicate that Transient Institutional Ownership does not have a significant association with time-to-recall, and the moderating associations of R&D Intensity and Device Class are not significant.
These results make sense given that transient institutional investors have little incentive to monitor portfolio firms because of their short-term investment horizons (Bushee 2001).
Moreover, the non-significance could also reflect the conflicting findings in the literature related to the stock market implications of recalls and associated delays (e.g., Chen et al. 2009; Darby et al. 2023).
In other words, extant evidence is unclear about whether recall delays result in stock market penalties, and transient institutional investors only care about short-term stock market returns.

Our primary analysis relies on AFT models, which are inherently nonlinear.
To explore this further, we added the squared term of Large Institutional Ownership.
The results presented in Appendix Table H6 indicate that the linear term has a negative, significant association with Time-to-Recall, whereas the squared term has a positive, significant association with Time-to-Recall.

This makes sense because firms can only recall so quickly, as executives work to identify the root cause of the defect before initiating a recall (Ball et al. 2018a).

## discussion
<!-- para 3 -->
Our first research question asked, is ownership by large institutional investors associated with a firm's time-to-recall?
We found that just a 1% increase in ownership by large institutional investors (e.g., going from 18% to 19%) was associated with a 24-day reduction in time-to-recall for medical devices with dangerous defects.
This is a notable reduction given that the well-being of dozens, hundreds, or even thousands of patients is at risk each day that a defective medical device remains on the market.
<!-- para 4 -->
Our study thus contributes to the growing body of research on recall antecedents within OSCM by illustrating the monitoring role of a prominent external actor—large institutional investors—in recall timing.
In doing so, we extend what is known about corporate governance and product recalls, which, to date, has been limited to internal actors such as the board of directors and the TMT (e.g., Wowak et al. 2021; Darby et al. 2023).
Moreover, as Bendoly and Oliva (2024, 519) explain, “Surely, we can benefit from inquiries at the intersection of OM and other disciplines, just as they can benefit from such interfacing work.” In this regard, our study builds on research in neighboring fields highlighting the monitoring effectiveness of large institutional investors (e.g., Dharwadkar et al. 2008; Lewellen and Lewellen 2022), but, more importantly, it sheds new light on why some dangerous products might be recalled faster than others.
<!-- para 5 -->
Within OSCM, Cheung et al. (2020) introduced the external governance role of institutional investors by examining the influence of common institutional ownership in buyer-supplier relationships.
We add to this knowledge base by providing evidence that ownership by large institutional investors is associated with how quickly firms recall defective medical devices.
Considering our findings alongside those of Cheung et al. (2020) should encourage OSCM scholars to examine the role of institutional investors for other OSCM activities, such as sourcing decisions (Gray et al. 2020), inventory management (Barker et al. 2022), supply chain resilience (Jiang et al. 2024), and sustainability (Song et al. 2024).
As Bendoly and Oliva (2024, 521) aptly note, the phenomena that OSCM scholars study “are full of non-OM agents and enablers (more co-producers of a sort).”
<!-- para 6 -->
Building on the concept of information asymmetry that is central to agency theory, our second research question asked, do (a) R&D intensity and (b) device class moderate the association between ownership by large institutional investors and time-to-recall?
Our findings indicate that they do.
At the firm level, we found that increases in R&D intensity weakened the negative association between large institutional ownership and time-to-recall.
We theorized that this weakening stemmed from the complexity associated with R&D intensity, which increases information asymmetry and makes it more challenging for large institutional investors to monitor the firm's operations and ensure that recalls are being handled appropriately.
An alternative explanation is that monitoring by large institutional investors may play less of a role when R&D intensity is high because an emphasis on R&D may yield improvements in quality management processes (Yiu et al. 2020), product quality (Lam et al. 2022) and learning (e.g., Ni et al. 2023), which may already reduce the time-to-recall.
In Figure 1, the solid line (high R&D intensity) lies below the dashed line (low R&D intensity).
This suggests that increases in R&D intensity may be associated with faster recalls, but the direct association of R&D intensity was not significant.
Stated differently, our findings suggest that ownership by large institutional investors may have the greatest opportunity for influence when R&D intensity is low, although this must be considered alongside the theoretically ambiguous role of R&D intensity in product quality and product recalls (e.g., Thirumalai and Sinha 2011; Mackelprang et al. 2015).
<!-- para 7 -->
Regarding device class, we found that the negative association between large institutional ownership and time-to-recall was weakened for high-risk (i.e., Class III) medical devices.
At first glance, one might expect that defects in Class III devices would be scrutinized more heavily by large institutional investors—and thus recalled more quickly—due to potential risks to consumers.
However, in Figure 2, the solid line (Class III devices) lies above the dashed line (Class I and II devices).
This suggests that high-risk devices may be recalled more slowly regardless of large institutional ownership, but the direct association of device class was not significant.
Moreover, our theorizing suggests that the information asymmetry associated with high-risk devices makes it more difficult for large institutional investors to evaluate whether defects among these devices are being handled appropriately (Ball et al. 2018a), which opens the door for recall delays.
Further work is needed to examine whether such delays reflect self-serving responses to increased information asymmetry surrounding high-risk devices and/or simply due diligence on the part of executives (Darby et al. 2023).
<!-- para 8 -->
Taken together, the moderating associations we identified underscore the importance of information asymmetry as a boundary condition (Bendoly and Oliva 2025).
Specifically, our findings point to a potential extension at the intersection of a firm's internal operational processes and external monitoring by large institutional investors.
In our study, recall delays may reflect the agency problem of moral hazard (Darby et al. 2023), so firms may need to enhance their operational processes in order to reduce the time-to-recall (e.g., Ball et al. 2018a).
At the same time, increases in R&D intensity and high-risk medical devices also require an increase in information dissemination so that large institutional investors can effectively monitor the firm's operations, understand the associated risks, and assess whether recalls are being handled appropriately.
If executives feel pressured to respond quickly to dangerous defects when large institutional investors loom among their stockholders, executives may need to invest in better systems and processes to reduce information asymmetry (e.g., Yiu et al. 2020).
Building on this idea, future research could explore the extent to which ownership by large institutional investors might encourage structural changes in how firms gather, evaluate, and disseminate quality-related information in particular and operations-related information more broadly.
<!-- para 10 -->
In describing the pharmaceutical industry, Anand et al. (2025, 302) highlighted that “the operations of this industry, which include all activities from scientific innovation to supply chain management, play an important role in the health and wellbeing of millions of people around the world.” Because operations within the medical device industry involve similarly high stakes, our study adds value to practice and public policy by shedding light on an important, and previously unidentified, path in understanding how quickly firms respond to quality failures (Bendoly and Oliva 2024).
Recalls—particularly those involving dangerous defects—put executives in an unenviable position, as they consider the firm’s interests, their own personal interests, and consumer health and well-being in their decision-making (Ball et al. 2022).
Understandably, executives may be apprehensive about disclosing negative news (i.e., by initiating a recall) when large institutional investors loom among their stockholders.
However, our findings indicate that executives perhaps need to leverage their fear of being monitored by large institutional owners because it encourages them to do the right thing and recall even the most dangerous medical devices more quickly.
Doing so is aligned with the interests of the firm, its stockholders, and, importantly, public health and safety.
<!-- para 11 -->
As described in our opening quote, the FDA has limited monitoring resources and must rely upon firms to identify and respond to dangerous defects in a timely manner.
Our findings highlight that large institutional investors may inadvertently help the FDA by monitoring how firms handle medical device recalls.
Our findings also highlight conditions that may weaken this monitoring function.
Firms with a low percentage of ownership by large institutional investors may respond more slowly to dangerous defects.
This tendency is accentuated by increases in R&D investments or recalls involving Class III devices that pose high risks.
Information on large institutional investors' ownership stakes is publicly available, as is information about a firm's R&D expenditures and medical device classifications.
The FDA could use this information to identify firms that might warrant extra scrutiny and, in doing so, better allocate its limited monitoring resources.
<!-- para 12 -->
Our study also offers implications for the SEC amidst ongoing pressures to restrict shareholder influence.
As institutional investors—particularly the Big Four—have become more involved in firm operations, trade associations have lobbied the SEC for increased restrictions as well as an expansion of the functions considered “ordinary business,” which are off-limits for investors (Connelly et al. 2019).
Our findings could be interpreted as suggesting that additional restrictions may not be warranted, as greater ownership by large institutional investors is associated with faster recalls of defective medical devices.
<!-- para 14 -->
Obtaining data via a FOIA request allowed us to construct an objective measure of time-to-recall, but it leaves unaddressed exactly how large institutional investors influence recall decision-making.
As Bendoly and Oliva (2024, 519) note, OSCM scholars “often find themselves with relatively limited visibility into the kinds of process details.” While our additional analyses shed preliminary light on the underlying mechanisms, this creates an opportunity for qualitative and/or experimental research (e.g., Ball et al. 2018a; Bendoly et al. 2006) to supplement our evidence with a richer understanding of those mechanisms.
For example, it would be fruitful for future research to examine whether large institutional investors influence other aspects of quality management beyond recalls, such as product development, production, and distribution so that defective products do not reach the market in the first place.
Moreover, our mechanism tests shed light on the effects of different types of institutional investors, but future research may also want to consider the potential influence of other types of shareholders such as activist investors (Shi et al. 2020).
<!-- para 15 -->
While each recall decision is unique, many firms have a formalized process to identify, evaluate, and respond to product defects (Wowak et al. 2021).
Some firms have also established board committees to oversee quality management and recalls (Darby et al. 2023).
Both of these possibilities may limit the extent of external monitoring.
Our inclusion of firm fixed effects and board characteristics as well as our use of frailty models helps to address this concern, but they may not fully account for variation in the recall decision-making process across firms.
<!-- para 16 -->
Given our single industry design, it remains unknown whether the associations we uncovered generalize to other industries in which serious recalls are also prevalent, such as automobiles (Ni and Huang 2018) and food (Wowak et al. 2016).
We chose to test our hypotheses using high severity medical device recalls in light of the ramifications of recall delays for firms and for consumer health and safety, but further research is needed to examine whether and how the associations we uncovered extend to other recall-laden industries.
Lastly, we used several different approaches and a broad vector of control variables at the recall-, firm-, and board-level to address endogeneity concerns, but potential endogeneity—particularly stemming from time-varying unobserved heterogeneity—is nonetheless a limitation of our research.

**B.1 Alternative Vectors of Matching Covariates**
<!-- para 28 -->
Our primary analysis uses one matching covariate: _Recent Recalls._ Ideally, we could use the full vector of control variables as matching covariates.
However, one limitation of coarsened exact matching (CEM) is that it “often results in a larger reduction of the sample size due to the nature of exact matching” (Yilmaz et al. 2024, p.848).
Indeed, Yilmaz et al. (2024, p.844) find that using “a large number of coarsening covariates may be detrimental to performance.” We thus conducted three additional matching analyses that iteratively add matching covariates to further account for heterogeneity.
The matching covariates were selected based on previous research to further address firm characteristics that might influence ownership by large institutional investors as well as how the firm handles product recalls.
<!-- para 29 -->
First, we added _CEO Stock Ownership_ and _Female Board Representation_ as matching covariates because they have been shown to directly influence recall timing (Wowak et al. 2021; Darby et al. 2023).
Second, we added _Sales, Return on Equity_, and _Debt Ratio,_ which have been shown to influence institutional investor ownership (e.g., Hartzell and Starks 2003; Aghion et al. 2013).
Third, we used the full vector of firm-level control variables as matching covariates.
<!-- para 30 -->
For the additional analyses, each of the matching covariates was coarsened using the automated algorithm.

There are decreases in overall _L1_ measures, univariate _L1_ measures, and mean differences, which suggest better balance after matching (e.g., Cornelius et al. 2021; Lee et al. 2021).

**B.2 Propensity Score Matching**
<!-- para 52 -->
In our primary analysis, we used CEM because it “requires fewer decisions from researchers,” which may influence the quality of matching (Yilmaz et al. 2024, p.848).
However, as noted above, one important limitation of CEM is that it results in a substantial reduction in sample size.
To address this, we used propensity score matching, which offers the advantage of retaining more of the sample even when a broad set of matching covariates is used (Yilmaz et al. 2024).
<!-- para 53 -->
In line with previous research (e.g., Oehmichen et al. 2021; Wowak et al. 2021), we used a median split of _Large Institutional Ownership_.
To estimate the propensity scores, we used a logistic regression model, the vector of firm-level covariates, and _Large Institutional Ownership (Median)_ as the outcome variable (Yilmaz et al. 2024).
Table B9 presents the results.
Then, we used one-to-one matching without replacement and a caliper of 0.2 times the standard deviation of the propensity score (Yilmaz et al. 2024; Son et al. 2025).
This process yielded a matched sample of 1,526 observations (763 treatment observations and 763 control observations).
Table B10 provides assessments of matching quality, including the comparison of means, two-sample t-tests, and reduction in bias before and after matching.
In general, it indicates that there is adequate balance after matching—there are no significant differences in means between the treated and control groups for every covariate except _CEO-Chair Separation_.
Moreover, Figure B1 provides a comparison of the distribution of the propensity scores before and after matching (e.g., Ba et al. 2022; Pu 2022).
As it illustrates, there is considerable improvement in the propensity score distributions of the treated and control groups, which further suggests adequate balance after matching.

**C.1 Time-Invariant Heterogeneity**
<!-- para 73 -->
While our primary analysis included a broad set of control variables (Shang and Rönkkö 2022), firm and year fixed effects, and robust standard errors clustered by firm (Abadie et al. 2023), our empirical strategy does not fully account for unobserved heterogeneity.
For example, given the recurrent nature of product recalls (i.e., firms may experience multiple recalls within the same year), it is possible that the time-to-recall may be correlated across recalls within the same firm (Ni and Huang 2018.
Frailty models address this limitation by introducing a random parameter that accounts for individual characteristics that influence the time to an event (Ni and Huang 2018).
As a first step, we used a frailty model to account for heterogeneity at the _recall_ level.
The underlying idea is that some recalls are more prone to occur sooner (or later) due to unobserved or unmeasured recall characteristics (Ni and Huang 2018).
In other words, some recalls are more “frail” than others, and such differences are modeled as a random parameter.
Following research precedent (e.g., Box-Steffensmeier and De Boef 2006; Ni and Huang 2018), we used a Gamma distribution to model the recall-level frailty.
Table C1 presents the results. _Large Institutional Ownership_ has a negative, significant association with time-to-recall, even after accounting for recall-level unobserved heterogeneity.
The moderating associations of _R&D Intensity_ and _Device Class_ are both positive and significant.
<!-- para 74 -->
As a second step, we used shared frailty models.
Such models are appropriate when the frailties are individual-specific, but there are multiple observations per individual.
The underlying idea is that some firms are more prone to recall sooner (or later) due to unobserved or unmeasured firm characteristics (Ni and Huang 2018).
In other words, some firms are more “frail” than others.
Shared frailty models address this by modeling the correlation within firms, which is included as a parameter in the estimation.
Following research precedent (e.g., Box-Steffensmeier and De Boef 2006; Ni and Huang 2018), we again used a Gamma distribution to model the firm-level frailty.
The results are presented in Table C2. _Large Institutional Ownership_ has a negative, significant association with time-to-recall, even after accounting for unobserved heterogeneity at the firm level.
The moderating associations of _R&D Intensity_ and _Device Class_ are both positive and significant.
<!-- para 75 -->
Lastly, we used the marginal risk set model developed in Wei et al. (1989), which accounts for the sequence of recalls for each firm by using stratification to allow the hazard rate to vary by the _k_th recall event (Ni and Huang 2018).
To do so, we created a sequence variable that reflects the ordering of high severity recalls for each firm in each year.
Following research precedent (Wei et al. 1989; Ni and Huang 2018), we used Cox proportional hazard models with recall sequence as the stratification and standard errors clustered by firm.
The results are presented in Table C3.
Before discussing the results, it is important to note that these models examine the _hazard_ of an event (Box-Steffensmeier et al. 2004)—in our case, a recall.
A positive coefficient indicates a higher hazard of a recall, which is analogous to a shorter time-to-recall.
Accordingly, the positive and significant coefficient for _Large Institutional Ownership_ is consistent with our primary results—i.e., greater ownership by large institutional investors is associated with a higher hazard of a recall (in other words, a shorter time-to-recall).
The moderating associations of _R&D Intensity_ and _Device Class_ are both negative and significant.
Overall, the consistent results we obtained after accounting for recall-level frailty, firm-level frailty, and recall sequence should help mitigate concerns related to time-invariant heterogeneity.

**C.2 Time-Varying Heterogeneity**
<!-- para 83 -->
To address omitted variable concerns stemming from time-varying heterogeneity, we conducted two placebo treatment tests, wherein we “replace the treatment by a future value of the treatment” (Eggers et al. 2024 p.1112).
In general, placebo tests check “for an association that should be absent if the assumptions underlying the design hold” (Eggers et al. 2024 p.1106).
Our underlying assumption centers on monitoring—large institutional investors exert influence indirectly by monitoring the firm’s handling of product defects.
If our monitoring assumption is correct, then the association between large institutional ownership and time-to-recall should be absent _after_ the recall is initiated.
<!-- para 84 -->
To examine this, we used the quarterly measure of _Large Institutional Ownership_ in (i) the quarter _after_ the recall initiation date and (ii) two quarters _after_ the recall initiation date.
The underlying idea is that future values of large institutional ownership should be affected by omitted variables in a similar way (i.e., large institutional investors tend to invest in firms with particular characteristics, which may also influence the time-to-recall).
The results reported in Table C4 indicate that the post-recall quarterly measures of _Large Institutional Ownership_ do not have a statistically significant association with _Time-to-Recall._ If our results are driven by omitted variables that are correlated with ownership by large institutional investors and influence the time-to-recall, these variables should affect time-to-recall similarly before and after the recall.
Thus, the _non-significance_ helps to alleviate omitted variable concerns (Eggers et al. 2024).
In other words, the non-significant results suggest it is unlikely that the associations we identified are driven by time-varying unobserved characteristics (e.g., Durach et al. 2024).

**Appendix D: Robustness Checks for Simultaneity and Reverse Causality**
<!-- para 88 -->
To address potential simultaneity concerns, we constructed our dataset based on the _defect awareness_ year.
Specifically, our dependent variable (_Time-To-Recall_), one moderating variable (_Device Class_), and two of our control variables (_Units Recalled_ and _Distribution Pattern_) are measured at the recall level because they vary by recall.
Our independent variable (_Large Institutional Ownership_), other moderating variable (_R&D Intensity_), and remaining control variables are measured at the firm level because they vary by firm, but not by recall.
The underlying idea is that firm-level variables tend to be relatively stable within a given year.
By constructing our dataset based on the defect awareness year, our analysis reflects the potential influence of large institutional ownership _before_ recall initiation.
<!-- para 89 -->
Ideally, however, we would be able to measure each variable at the closest point to the defect awareness date.
This is feasible for _Large Institutional Ownership_ and _Institutional Ownership Concentration_ because the data are consistently available on a quarterly basis, whereas the other firm-level variables are reported annually.
We thus conducted an additional analysis in which _Large Institutional Ownership_ was measured in the _quarter_ _before_ the defect awareness date.
The results presented in Table D1 indicate that the lagged quarterly measure of _Large Institutional Ownership_ has a negative, significant association with time-to-recall.
Likewise, _R&D Intensity_ and _Device Class_ both have positive, significant moderating associations.
In sum, the results using the lagged quarterly measure are consistent with our primary results, which helps to alleviate simultaneity concerns.3(#_ftn3)
<!-- para 90 -->
It is also possible that large institutional ownership may be influenced by a firm’s recall history.
For example, large institutional investors may invest in firms with a history of recalls because they believe they can positively influence decision-making via monitoring.
To address this possibility, we panelized the data and used fixed effects models to examine whether a firm’s recent recalls or average time-to-recall influence the level of _Large Institutional Ownership_.
The results presented in Table D2 suggest that a firm’s recall history – specifically, the number of recent high severity recalls and past timing for those recalls – are not significantly associated with ownership by large institutional investors, which helps to alleviate concerns related to reverse causality.

**Appendix E: Robustness Check for Measurement Error**
<!-- para 96 -->
To address potential endogeneity concerns related to measurement error, we used an alternative measure based on data from the WRDS Thomson Reuters Institutional (13f) Stock Ownership database.
Following previous research (e.g., Crane and Koch 2018), we used the _total_ percentage of outstanding shares owned by institutional investors.
To match our dataset, we aggregated the quarterly data to the annual level by averaging the measure across all four quarters for each firm in each year.
The underlying rationale is that all institutional investors could be considered large because, by definition, they manage more than $100 million in assets (Cheung et al. 2020), so it is important to examine the broader constituency of institutional investors.
<!-- para 97 -->
The results presented in Table E1 are largely consistent with our primary results, which helps to alleviate concerns related to measurement error.
Specifically, _Total Institutional Ownership_ has a negative, significant association with time-to-recall such that greater ownership by institutional investors more broadly is associated with faster recalls.
Moreover, _R&D Intensity_ has a positive, marginally significant moderating association, and _Device Class_ has a positive, significant moderating association.
One potential explanation for the marginally significant moderating effect of _R&D Intensity_ is that _Total Institutional_ _Ownership_, while comprehensive, does not adequately reflect heterogeneity in goals and levels of engagement across institutional investors.

**Appendix F: Robustness Checks for Multicollinearity and Outliers**
<!-- para 101 -->
To address potential multicollinearity concerns, we first examined variance inflation factors (VIF).
The largest VIF was 4.73 for _Sales_ followed by 3.34 for _CEO Total Compensation_.
This is perhaps not surprising given the high correlation between these two variables (0.78).
While the relatively low VIFs suggest that multicollinearity concerns are limited, Kalnins (2018) highlights the weaknesses of VIFs and recommends an alternative approach wherein the highly correlated variables are removed to see if the effect of the independent variable changes.
Following this approach, we conducted two additional analyses—one excluding _Sales_ and one excluding _CEO Total Compensation_.
As reported in Tables F1 and F2, we obtained consistent results.
It is thus “unlikely that multicollinearity is distorting the results” (Kalnins 2018, p.2378).
<!-- para 102 -->
To address the potential impact of outliers, we then conducted two additional analyses using winsorized values at the 1st and 99th percentiles for (i) the control variables and (ii) the control variables and focal variables, except for the categorical variables_._ As reported in Tables F3 and F4, we obtained consistent results, which helps to address concerns about the impact of potential outliers.

**Appendix G: Robustness Checks for Empirical Strategy**
<!-- para 112 -->
While CEM helps to address concerns related to selection on observable characteristics, it requires researchers to make “several algorithm and specification choices” that influence matching quality and sample size (Yilmaz et al., 2024 p.848).
To be conservative, we conducted an analysis that does not use matching to pre-process the data.
Table G1 presents the results, which are consistent with our primary analysis and help bolster confidence our specification choices do not unduly influence our conclusions.
<!-- para 113 -->
AFT models offer important advantages over linear regression (Bhattacharjee et al. 2007), but they require specification of the underlying distribution—in our case, a Weibull distribution.
This may raise concerns that our choice of distribution may be influencing our results.
To address this possibility, we replicated our analysis using Cox proportional hazard models, which do not require specification of the underlying distribution (Ball et al. 2017).
Table G2 presents the results, which are consistent with our primary analysis.
Here, it is important to reiterate that a positive coefficient indicates a higher hazard of a recall, which is analogous to a shorter time-to-recall.
Accordingly, the positive and significant effect of _Large Institutional Ownership_ is consistent with our primary results—i.e., greater ownership by large institutional investors is associated with a higher hazard of a recall (in other words, a shorter time-to-recall).
The moderating associations of _R&D Intensity_ and _Device Class_ are both negative and significant.
<!-- para 114 -->
Beyond the underlying distribution, there may be concerns about the logarithmic transformation of our dependent variable, which is an inherent feature of AFT models.
To address this, we used a linear regression model and the untransformed _Time-to-Recall_ as the dependent variable.
Table G3 presents the results, which are consistent with our primary analysis.
Overall, the unmatched sample, the Cox proportional hazard models, and the linear regression models illustrate that our results are robust to alternative empirical strategies and provide additional support for all three hypotheses.
