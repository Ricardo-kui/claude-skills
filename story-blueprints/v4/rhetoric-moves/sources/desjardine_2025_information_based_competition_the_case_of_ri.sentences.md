# desjardine_2025_information_based_competition_the_case_of_ri 句子库存

## introduction
<!-- para 2 -->
Organizational scholars have long been interested in understanding how firms outcompete their rivals using a variety of competitive actions, including short-term tactical actions like product updates and marketing campaigns, as well as large and long-term strategic moves like acquisitions and joint ventures (Barnett, 1997; Hambrick, Cho & Chen, 1996).
With information being central to competition, competing businesses have also been found to disclose adverse information about each other with the goal of elevating their own competitive positions.
Quiznos, for instance, has been sued for running advertising campaigns that purportedly defamed Subway (Story, 2008).
In this spirit, Cao, Fang, and Lei (2021: 815) recently documented the tendency for firms to publicize negative news about their industry peers on social media, in what they deemed “an emerging corporate strategy.” This stream of literature and related anecdotes reveal how firms tactically manufacture and release negative information about their rivals to improve their own competitive positions.
<!-- para 3 -->
As stakeholders’ views and attitudes about firms are also shaped by the content that information intermediaries publish about those firms and their competitors (Ravasi, Rindova, Etter & Cornelissen, 2018; Sharkey, Kovács & Hsu, 2023), it is surprising that organizational scholars have mostly overlooked how information intermediaries could be leveraged as conduits in information-based competition. [^1]
<!-- para 4 -->
Information intermediaries are “entities that specialize in the gathering and provision of data relevant to consumer choice” (Sharkey et al., 2023: 1), including rating agencies (e.g., MSCI, a leading publicly traded rating agency), news outlets (e.g., Washington Post), and review aggregators (e.g., Yelp.com).
Because they are seen as trusted authorities, information intermediaries are known to shape stakeholders’ attitudes and behaviors toward the firms they cover (Rindova, Williamson, Petkova & Sever, 2005).
At the same time, because these intermediaries have their own economic agendas and incentives (Beattie, Durante, Knight & Sen, 2021), they can be subject to outside influence, raising the possibility that some players may try to leverage these intermediaries to advance their own competitive agendas.
<!-- para 5 -->
Against this backdrop, we develop a theory of information-based competition that puts information intermediaries at the center of competitive dynamics.
The fulcrum of our theory is that actors with influence over an information intermediary may use this influence to advance their own competitive objectives by shaping how information is distributed, thereby placing certain firms ahead and others behind.
In line with our theory, recent work (DesJardine, Shi & Cheng, 2023) has shown that investors who own shares in media companies use that ownership to tilt the coverage of media outlets in favor of their portfolio firms.
However, this work provides a highly static view of investor influence over information intermediaries, assuming that influence is exerted without any regard for the ongoing dynamics of the competitive landscape.
This static view is surprising, because scholars have long acknowledged that competitive attacks are initiated by firms to take advantage of threats and opportunities in the competitive landscape (Chen & Miller, 2015).
We build on this research by providing a more dynamic theory of information-based competition.
<!-- para 6 -->
We test our information-based competition theory in the context of common ownership in rating agencies.
Common institutional owners (hereafter “common owners”) are institutional investors that simultaneously own shares in multiple firms, [^2] which in our context entails a rating agency and other non-rating firms.
Just as common owners use their ownership influence to coordinate corporate actions, including acquisitions and competitive moves (Connelly, Lee, Tihanyi, Certo & Johnson, 2019; Goranova, Dharwadkar & Brandes, 2010), we argue that they do the same to tilt ratings in favor of their other portfolio firms.
The idea that rating agencies are subject to influence by institutional investors aligns with research on the conflicts of interest that permeate information intermediaries (Sharkey et al., 2023), the sway of exchange partners’ economic interests over ratings (Cornaggia & Cornaggia, 2013; Jiang, Stanford & Xie, 2012), and rating agencies’ discretion in rating firms (Berg, Koelbel & Rigobon, 2022; Chatterji, Durand, Levine & Touboul, 2016; Delmas, Etzion & Nairn-Birch, 2013).
<!-- para 7 -->
In our main analyses, we use a sample of firms whose environmental, social, and governance (ESG) [^3] performance is rated by MSCI.
We find that a target firm's ESG rating by MSCI is negatively associated with the level of ownership its rivals' institutional investors have in MSCI.
In competitive dynamics parlance, there are targets and attackers.
We use the term "target" to refer to firms that have their ratings altered by investors in "rival" firms in common owners' portfolios.
Our results suggest that institutional investors with ownership stakes in both a rating agency and a target firm's rivals can themselves become rivals of the target firm by tainting the owned rating agency's coverage of the target firm.
<!-- para 8 -->
To develop a dynamic theory of information-based competition, we examine how competitive threats and opportunities impact institutional investors' motives to use information intermediaries to shape the competitive landscape.
Noting that reputation is an important dimension on which firms compete, and that changes to reputation can drastically alter a firm's economic prospects (Bundy, Iqbal & Pfarrer, 2021), we first look at the reputational threats target firms and their rivals experience, followed by reputational opportunities, and how each impacts the willingness and ability of common owners to undertake information-based competition.
We argue that the bias these investors bring to ESG rating agencies hinges on other information-based threats and opportunities firms experience.
<!-- para 9 -->
In terms of theoretical contributions, our study provides new insights into the outside forces that shape competitive dynamics.
Most competitive dynamics research has focused on actions launched by firms to outpace their competitors, and ignored the role played by outsiders with incentives to see some firms prevail and others fail.
Building on recent research (DesJardine, Shi & Cheng, 2023), we introduce the concept of information-based competition and add a critical dimension by theorizing the conditions under which outside actors leverage their influence over information intermediaries to shape competitive dynamics.
Furthermore, by accounting for how a firm's reputation is subject to multiple information sources, we offer a more complete theory of how outside actors use their influence over information intermediaries to combat and counter competing information on firms.
While we examine information-based competition in one context, our theory has widespread theoretical generalizability, paving the way for future studies to further illuminate this more covert form of competition.
<!-- para 10 -->
By testing our theory in the context of common ownership in rating agencies, we also contribute to several other streams of research.
In the literature on information intermediaries, most prior studies limit the origin of coverage bias to the firms and investors being covered by, for example, rating agencies (Cornaggia & Cornaggia, 2013; Tang, Peytcheva & Li, 2020) and news outlets (Beattie et al., 2021; Gurun & Butler, 2012).
However, we show that a firm's coverage by rating agencies is also influenced by its rivals' investors.
Our findings on ESG intermediaries contribute to the emerging debate on the drivers of ESG ratings (Chatterji et al., 2016; Kölbel & Busch, 2021; Slager, Gond & Moon, 2012) and answer Berg, Koelbel, and Rigobon's (2022: 1315) call to direct "greater attention to how the data underlying ESG ratings is generated." We also contribute to scholarship on investor influence by going beyond the ways in which common owners can directly shape their portfolio firms' strategic and competitive actions (Connelly et al., 2019; DesJardine, Grewal & Viswanathan, 2023; Goranova et al., 2010) to further document the indirect channels common owners use to advance their investment objectives beyond the media (DesJardine, Shi & Cheng, 2023).

## theory
<!-- para 3 -->
Over several decades, scholars have documented the actions firms undertake to elevate their competitive positions (Barnett, 1997; Hambrick et al., 1996), including short-term tactical actions (e.g., product updates) as well as large and long-term strategic moves (e.g., acquisitions).
In addition, firms may try to enhance their relative competitive positions by disclosing negative information about their competitors (Cao et al., 2021) in a form of information-based competition, which we define as the means through which organizations manufacture, assemble, and disseminate information that seeks to shape stakeholders' perceptions of and interactions with other organizations.
Yet, defaming competitors can result in lawsuits and costly regulatory sanctions.
As a workaround, some firms might try to gain advantages over their competitors by leveraging their ties with third-party information intermediaries to secure better coverage (e.g., Beattie et al., 2021).
The broader literature on information intermediaries suggests that these intermediaries could serve as a valuable competitive channel, for two reasons: (1) information intermediaries play a pivotal role in shaping a firm's legitimacy and reputation with its stakeholders; and (2) information intermediaries, like other organizations, can be subject to outside influence, including from the firms they cover.
<!-- para 4 -->
In contexts involving information asymmetry, information intermediaries provide stakeholders with important insights into an organization's practices and performance (Rindova et al., 2005).
Information intermediaries, such as expert critics, news outlets, certification providers, and rating agencies, are generally viewed as having superior access to information and expertise in evaluating organizations (Rao, 1994).
By virtue of their perceived expertise and position, they can function as valuable sources of information by assessing an organization's practices and translating those assessments into articles, certifications, ratings, or other outputs that signal the quality of the organization's practices to its stakeholders (Casile & Davis-Blake, 2002).
Just as ratings can provide information about the quality of restaurants (Durand, Rao & Monin, 2007), business schools (Durand & McGuire, 2005), and law programs (Sauder & Espeland, 2009), sell-side analysts can communicate the investment prospects of stocks (Brauer & Wiersema, 2018).
<!-- para 5 -->
Enabling their potential to serve as a useful competitive tool, information intermediaries' assessments can have important implications for a firm's legitimacy and reputation.
From an institutional perspective, positive coverage from these intermediaries grants organizations legitimacy, which is an important condition for firm survival.
As noted by Sharkey and colleagues (2023: 1), information intermediaries “create clear demarcations in the market’s social hierarchy by deeming certain producers as legitimate or worthy of respect, while dismissing others.” By granting firms legitimacy, coverage produced by information intermediaries can shape firms’ reputations, changing audiences’ social and psychological perceptions such that stakeholders behave more favorably toward positively covered firms and more unfavorably toward negatively covered firms (Ravasi et al., 2018).
<!-- para 6 -->
Also important for enabling their potential as a competitive tool is that information intermediaries' coverage can be subject to conflicts of interest that create room for bias and outside influence.
For example, studies show that companies receive more favorable coverage from news outlets where they spend more advertising dollars (Beattie et al., 2021; Gurun & Butler, 2012), such that these outlets "underreport or bias news that sufficiently reduces advertiser profits" (Ellman & Germano, 2009: 680).
Likewise, studies find that companies being rated by credit agencies can receive better credit ratings by paying those agencies for their coverage (Cornaggia & Cornaggia, 2013; Jiang et al., 2012; Kempf & Tsoutsoura, 2021).
(2020) also presented experimental evidence that agencies inflate the credit ratings of investors that pay for rating services.
Collectively, this research shows that firms and investors receive better coverage from information intermediaries to which they are economically tied.
<!-- para 7 -->
If an information intermediary is willing to shape its coverage of a rated firm to advance both that firm's and the intermediary's economic agenda, other actors with power over an information intermediary could also use their influence to advance a more significant economic agenda—for instance, by swaying the coverage of firms in their favor.
Providing some evidence of this possibility, DesJardine, Shi, and Cheng (2023) found that the outlets of media companies owned by institutional investors cover firms differently based on investors' economic interests.
Yet, their study offers a notable limitation, which we use as a departure point to enrich a theory of information-based competition.
<!-- para 8 -->
By not examining the changes in competitive dynamics that firms are so frequently subjected to, DesJardine, Shi, and Cheng (2023) offered a static view of information-based competition.
However, this static view does not fully align with research showing that competitive threats and opportunities regularly evolve in ways that shape an actor's motivation and capability to initiate competitive moves (Chen & Miller, 2015).
Thus, from a competitive dynamics perspective, outside actors may only have or utilize influence over information intermediaries when they observe threats and opportunities that create "windows of opportunity" to engage in information-based competition.
For models of information-based competition to be accurate, it is imperative to account for the realities of competition—in this case, its temporal nature.
Thus, our theory of information-based competition accounts for how shifts in the competitive landscape (via threats or opportunities to a firm's reputation) trigger outside actors to exert their influence over information intermediaries.
<!-- para 10 -->
The rise of common ownership has granted institutional investors a new and unique way to profit by altering firms' competitive landscapes.
Common ownership occurs when an investor simultaneously owns shares in multiple publicly traded firms (Connelly et al., 2019).
Extending research that explores investors' influence on firms' competitive actions (Connelly, Tihanyi, Certo & Hitt, 2010; Connelly, Tihanyi, Ketchen, Carnes & Ferrier, 2017), scholars have raised the possibility that common owners use their influence in companies to encourage certain competitive arrangements that serve to increase common owners' portfolio returns.
(2019) found that common owners profit by orchestrating competitive actions such that direct competition between their portfolio firms is mitigated.
Though subject to debate (Dennis, Gerardi & Schenone, 2022; Lewellen & Lowry, 2021), others have suggested that common ownership can limit intra-industry competition by coordinating price-fixing among competitors (Azar, Schmalz & Tecu, 2018).
<!-- para 11 -->
Noting the investment returns common owners stand to realize by altering firms' competitive activities, we theorize that these investors might also seek to use their stakes in information intermediaries to advance their investment objectives elsewhere.
Some intermediaries, including rating agencies and review aggregators (e.g., Yelp.com), operate as publicly traded entities in which investors can hold significant ownership; therefore, when investors in those intermediaries also hold shares in other companies, they become common owners, bridging the information intermediaries and the firms they cover.
Combined with the insight that information intermediaries can influenced by their investors (DesJardine, Shi & Cheng, 2023; Tang et al., 2020), this unique boundary-spanning position grants common owners both the incentives and power to use information intermediaries to enhance their own investment returns through the form of information-based competition we envision.
<!-- para 13 -->
The theory we lay out in this section expands the tactics of competitive dynamics to third-party information-based attacks, beyond direct firm-to-firm competitive attacks.
It also delineates the conditions necessary for these types of attacks to occur.
As we argue, firms receive less favorable coverage from rating agencies in which their rivals' institutional investors have greater ownership stakes, making the investor the source of rating bias and the firm that competes with that investor's portfolio firms (i.e., its rivals) the target of rating bias.
In other words, by competing with an investor's portfolio firms, a target firm effectively finds itself with a new rival in that investor, making it the target of rating bias.

<!-- para 15 -->
Although the coverage of various types of information intermediaries could impact firms in ways that benefit common owners, ESG ratings issued by rating agencies seem especially advantageous and timely.
Indeed, in today's environment, ESG ratings can profoundly impact the reputations of firms as well as their interactions with key stakeholders.

<!-- para 18 -->
ESG ratings can impact firms' reputations because firms' environmental and social activities are inherently difficult for outsiders to assess otherwise.
Many firms are known to overclaim their environmental and social commitments, such that stakeholders and regulators often lament the rampant practice of "greenwashing" (Parguel, Benoît-Moreau & Larce-neux, 2011).
Because rating agencies seek to evaluate and validate a firm's internal activities, many stakeholders rely on ESG ratings to assess the trustworthiness and legitimacy of firms' environmental and social activities.
As Rao (1994: 39) remarked, "rating agencies legitimate organizations and make them trustworthy." As shown in a meta-analysis by Vishwanathan, van Oosterhout, Heugens, Duran, and Van Essen (2020), this allows ESG ratings to shape firms' reputations in ways that materially shift stakeholders' behaviors.
<!-- para 19 -->
In turn, ESG ratings could also affect investor demand for a company's stock.
Kölbel, Heeb, Paetzold, and Busch (2020) explained that the popularity of ESG screening has resulted in more investors overweighting firms with better ESG ratings and underweighting those with worse ratings in their portfolios.
As such, the potential impacts of ESG ratings on stock prices have risen over time (Kölbel & Busch, 2021), with some studies—subject to debate about different contingencies (Berchicci & King, 2022)—suggesting that better ESG ratings can increase a firm's stock price (Khan, Serafeim & Yoon, 2016; Serafeim & Yoon, 2022).
Moreover, positive ESG ratings can help firms secure more favorable analyst recommendations (Ioannou & Serafeim, 2014) and higher earnings forecasts (Chava, 2014), lower bank loan costs (Goss & Roberts, 2011) and capital constraints (Cheng, Ioannou & Serafeim, 2014), and tighter credit default swap spreads (Drago, Carnevale & Gallo, 2019).
<!-- para 20 -->
Despite the reputational and financial significance of ESG ratings, the multistep process by which these ratings are assembled gives rating agencies extensive latitude, such that ESG ratings exhibit widespread divergence between different agencies (Berg, Koelbel & Rigobon, 2022; Chatterji et al., 2016).
Rating agencies must first define ESG, an inherently broad construct that grants agencies leeway in their own interpretations (Delmas & Blass, 2010; Delmas et al., 2013).
Next, based on their chosen definition, they must determine which ESG practices to include in their eventual assessment.
What is seen as prosocial by some might be viewed as mere regulatory compliance by others, leading different agencies to include different inputs in their evaluations of ESG (Berg, Fabisik & Sautner, 2021).
Rating agencies must then decide how to evaluate each practice, including a scoring mechanism, as well as how each practice is weighted, creating additional room for discretion and divergence (Capelle-Blancard & Petit, 2017).
Finally, based on all prior inputs, the rating agency must decide how to aggregate all scores across the multiple dimensions into a final ESG rating to be sent and sold to markets. [^4]
<!-- para 21 -->
However, because this multistep process is not standardized, formalized, or regulated, it limits rating transparency.
Describing the leading ESG rater MSCI, for instance, Sharkey et al.
(2023: 12) remarked that “the specific determinants of a firm’s score are vague, described as emerging from analysts’ interpretations of data from a variety of both objective and subjective sources.” Practitioners, likewise, warn that “despite growing influence, ESG raters and data providers are largely unregulated, lack transparency in their methods, offer uneven coverage and harbor potential conflicts of interest” (Jones, 2021).
<!-- para 22 -->
The latitude that different agencies have in assembling their ESG ratings could afford opportunities for powerful common owners to influence rating agencies to undertake information-based competition.
By “powerful,” we mean common owners that control notable ownership stakes in both an ESG rating agency and a separate firm outside the rating industry.
Holding sizable stakes in both entities, common owners should have sufficient incentives and power to influence the owned rating agency to issue ratings that advance the common owner’s interests.
Yet, given their unique motivation to leverage ESG ratings to their advantage, how is this influence applied, and why would rating agencies cater to the influence of these investors?
<!-- para 23 -->
Influence begins with the discussions that occur between common owners and rating agency executives, and, sometimes, analysts.
Rating agency analysts and executives struggle to “get inside” the firms they rate to accurately understand their efforts and activities, and are at times limited by the information they can collect from the questionnaires they distribute to company managers, who may either inaccurately or haphazardly complete such reports (Sharkey et al., 2023).
As a result, practitioners suggest that ESG raters try to locate and “include material information not otherwise available to the public” in their ratings (Halper, Grieve & Shriver, 2022).
One information source raters might value is investors, who may be perceived as competent and informed (Foss, Klein, Lien, Zellweger & Zenger, 2020).
Illustrating the value of investors’ opinions and ideas, an ESG analyst we interviewed noted, “We frequently interact with our investors to better understand their values and ideas, including what ESG dimensions they care about and what dimensions should be included in our rating.” Although many of these meetings happen behind closed doors (e.g., in private meetings at a company’s headquarters), they can also occur publicly, such as when Henry Fernandez, CEO of MSCI, was interviewed by Nicola Tangen, CEO of Norges Bank, which held about 1% of MSCI’s shares in 2023. [^5]
<!-- para 24 -->
In these discussions, institutional investors may naturally speak more positively about the ESG efforts of their portfolio firms in which they have greater ownership, and more negatively about those firms' competitors—the "target" firms.
Rating agency executives and analysts could consciously or subconsciously use this feedback to inform their ESG ratings of firms, and their relative standing.
This idea is supported by research showing that a rating analyst's personal views about the companies being rated can explain one-fifth of the variance in ESG ratings (Berg, Koelbel & Rigobon, 2022) and by calls from the U.S.
Securities and Exchange Commission (SEC) for ESG rating agencies to establish better internal controls when interpreting "ESG-related data from affiliates or unaffiliated third parties" (SEC, 2022: 8).
In cases where only rating agency executives meet with investors, which is typical outside the rating industry (Solomon & Soltes, 2015), executives may pass information they glean from investors to their analysts or influence the ratings of analysts indirectly.
As one ESG rating analyst explained to us, "Rating agency executives play a critical role in the rating process.
Some of them serve as members of our corporate sustainability committee, which determines the rating rubric we use."
<!-- para 25 -->
On the other side of the equation, executives of rating agencies may cater to the interests of their major institutional investors.
Prior studies show that executives sometimes go to great lengths to appease their investors (Westphal & Bednar, 2008), partially because losing or upsetting investors can cause financial and reputational harm to executives.
To reduce that risk, standard investor relations protocols advise executives to pay close attention to major investors' holdings in other companies and try to account for those investors' economic interests (Oram, 2016).
Exemplifying the potential for this type of investor pacification, Sanderson Farms shifted its ESG reporting standards in response to BlackRock's "request," even though the company's smaller investors disagreed (Kerber, 2020).
As rating agencies seek to "form close or mutually beneficial relationships" with their exchange partners (Sharkey et al., 2023: 20), executives of rating agencies may consider their major institutional investors' interests in the rating process.
<!-- para 26 -->
Importantly, the opacity of the ESG rating process facilitates these opportunities for rating influence while decreasing the risk of detection for both institutional investors and rating agency insiders.
The theorized channel through which common owners influence rating agencies and their ESG ratings is legal.
The subjectivity of ESG ratings not only gives rating agencies latitude in their evaluations, but also gives rating agencies' executives and analysts flexibility to adjust their ratings based on an investor's advice.
A common owner who offers "advice" to inform an ESG rating could legally be considered an advisor.
Thus, if common owners go to great lengths to directly coordinate activities among multiple portfolio firms (e.g., Connelly et al., 2019), it seems plausible that they use their ownership influence in rating agencies to shape ratings for their own benefit, and that some rating agency executives are open to that influence.
<!-- para 27 -->
We expect target firms to receive less favorable ratings from rating agencies in which their rivals' institutional investors have greater ownership.
That is, the level of common ownership that rivals' institutional investors have in a rating agency is negatively correlated with the rating the target firm receives from that agency.
<!-- para 28 -->
> [!hypothesis]
> **Hypothesis 1.** The level of common ownership that rivals' institutional investors have in a rating agency is negatively associated with the target firm's ESG rating issued by the rating agency.
<!-- para 29 -->
There are two related reasons why we do not hypothesize that institutional investors seek to influence the ratings of their own portfolio firms.
First, as warnings about the anticompetitive effects of common owners have been raised among regulators, common owners can decrease their risk of detection by procuring worse ESG ratings for firms not in their portfolios.
Second, by handicapping a target firm that competes with their portfolio firms, common owners can realize improved investment prospects among several of their portfolio firms.
This relative effect occurs because ESG standards differ widely between industries, forcing stakeholders and investors to make ESG comparisons on a within-industry basis.
Taken together, by negatively influencing their portfolio firms' rivals, common owners can realize the rewards of their information-based competitive influence while decreasing the risk of their influence being detected.
<!-- para 31 -->
A social constructionist view of reputation posits that a company's reputation is based on an affective understanding of the organization as it exists in the minds of beholders (Graf-Vlachy, Oliver, Banfield, König & Bundy, 2020).
From this view, the media can serve as a general intermediary that shapes audiences' perceptions through its coverage of organizations' behaviors (Bundy et al., 2021; Rindova et al., 2005).
For example, a firm's reputation can be subject to the media's coverage of its product recalls (Rhee & Haunschild, 2006) or earnings announcements (Oliver, Campbell, Graffin & Bundy, 2022).
With regard to ESG, the extent of damage firms incur to their reputations is a function of heterogeneous media coverage of their controversial ESG issues.
When the media publicizes a firm's ESG controversies, such as excessive environmental pollution or poor treatment of its employees, that coverage can lead the firm's stakeholders to question the sincerity of its ESG efforts in ways that damage its reputation (Vergne, Wernicke & Brenner, 2018).
In contrast, when a firm's ESG controversies are minimally covered, stakeholders become less aware and less critical of those issues, which shelters the firm's reputation from damage.
<!-- para 32 -->
In our context, when a common owner's portfolio firm incurs reputational damage from media coverage of its ESG controversies, subsequent increases in the firm's ESG ratings become more useful in mitigating potentially unfavorable stakeholder evaluations and reputational harm experienced by the firm and the common owner.
As information spreads, initial stakeholder reservations can lead others to question the portfolio firm's ESG, allowing reputational damage to persist and percolate, and potentially creating ripple effects that undermine the portfolio firm's financial performance and shareholder returns (Zavyalova, Bundy & Humphrey, 2021).
Yet, if new information causes stakeholders to reassess their initial reservations about the portfolio firm's ESG activities, the reputational harm could be constrained and possibly even repaired.
<!-- para 33 -->
According to Rhee and Valdez (2009), rating agencies should play a critical role in offsetting the damage to a portfolio firm's reputation by providing "independent" and "expert" assessments of a firm's performance relative to its peers.
For a portfolio firm whose reputation is called into question by media coverage of its ESG controversies, subsequent improvements to the firm's ESG ratings could eliminate stakeholders' reservations about the firm.
Because stakeholders trust ESG ratings, those rating improvements may make the firm appear to have better ESG performance than its peers.
Because ESG ratings effectively counteract media-induced reputational damage, and because stakeholders' assessments are based on relative comparisons between similar firms, common owners may be especially motivated to attempt to enhance the relative reputations of portfolio firms that have recently sustained damage to their reputations due to ESG media controversies.
Following our prior logic, we expect common owners to achieve this goal by tarnishing the reputations of (target) firms that compete with their portfolio firms.
That is, a target firm is likely to receive worse coverage from a rating agency in which rivals' institutional investors have greater ownership when those rivals face more ESG media controversies.
<!-- para 34 -->
> [!hypothesis]
> **Hypothesis 2a.** The negative relationship between rivals' common ownership with a rating agency and the target firm's ESG rating from that agency is stronger when rivals face more ESG media controversies.
<!-- para 35 -->
Likewise, when ESG media controversy plagues a target firm that competes with the portfolio firms of common owners, common owners' motivations to intervene in ESG ratings are likely to wane.
In such cases, the target firm is already subject to intense ESG scrutiny and may be penalized by raters and other stakeholders for their perceived misdeeds, meaning rivals in the common owners' portfolio compare favorably against these firms.
Although rivals may become subject to negative spillover effects of the target firm's ESG media controversy (DesJardine, Grewal & Viswanathan, 2023; Shi, Connelly, Hoskisson
<!-- para 36 -->
& Ketchen, 2020), they should still fare better than the target firm that directly incurs the controversy.
This follows from the idea that spillover effects dissipate, such that firms involved in controversies are almost always more heavily penalized than any “bystander” peers subject to negative spillovers (Shi, Wajda & Aguilera, 2022).
<!-- para 37 -->
> [!hypothesis]
> **Hypothesis 2b.** The negative relationship between rivals' common ownership with a rating agency and the target firm's ESG rating from that agency is weaker when the target firm faces more ESG media controversies.
<!-- para 39 -->
Beyond the motivation to influence information intermediaries, common owners need the ability to do so, as reflected in the notion of being willing and able (Durand, Hawn & Ioannou, 2019).
In line with competitive dynamics research (Chen & Miller, 2015), where ESG reputational threats affect a common owner's motivations to engage in information-based competition, ESG reputational opportunities affect their ability to make these attacks.
By ESG reputational opportunities, we mean situations or circumstances that impact a firm's public image for ESG and the malleability of that image to outside influence.
ESG reputational opportunities can arise from a variety of factors, particularly the announcement of major ESG awards (e.g., the Financial Times Responsible Business Award).
Indeed, ESG awards can alter a firm's ESG reputation by increasing its social standing relative to non-winners (Li, Yin, Shi & Yi, 2022; Shi, Zhang & Hoskisson, 2017).
<!-- para 40 -->
When a firm has recently won a major award for its ESG initiatives, common owners should find it easier to sway an ESG rating agency in favor of that firm, and against its less-decorated peers.
Major awards can send messages to stakeholders about the authenticity and impact of a company's practices and policies (Kovács & Sharkey, 2014).
When based on a firm's record of ESG initiatives, these awards can signal that a firm has made laudable efforts to embrace sustainable and responsible practices that promote the interests of different stakeholders.
By doing so, ESG raters should become more receptive not only to positive information about firms that have won ESG awards, but also to negative information about their less-decorated peers whose efforts have not attracted such attention.
At the same time, since most ESG rating agencies do not directly adjust their ratings based on a firm's ESG awards (an achievement not included in most ratings methodologies), ESG ratings are not directly affected by the awards.
Rather, given their publicity, such awards independently shape stakeholders' perceptions about the firm's ESG performance.
As a result, ESG awards can affect the receptivity of executives and analysts to the information common owners share about these firms.
<!-- para 41 -->
Common owners may thus utilize the shift in raters' information receptivity triggered by ESG awards to advance their information-based competition objectives.
When a common owner's portfolio firm wins an ESG award, the common owner may leverage news about the award to further sway an ESG rating agency against the firm's less-decorated peers.
Bestowed by the award provider and not by the ESG rating agency itself, the ESG award provides yet another talking point that a common owner could leverage to make its portfolio firm appear better than its peers in the minds of ESG rating executives and analysts.
It could also make those rating executives and analysts feel more justified in assigning higher ratings to the award-winning firm and lower ratings to the firm's less-decorated peers, ultimately making them more responsive to the biased information provided by a common owner.
<!-- para 42 -->
> [!hypothesis]
> **Hypothesis 3a.** The negative relationship between rivals' common ownership with a rating agency and the target firm's ESG rating from that agency is stronger when rivals have won more ESG awards.
<!-- para 43 -->
Similarly, when a target firm wins a major ESG award, common owners of rivals may find it difficult to negatively influence the ESG ratings of the target firm.
Following the logic above, ESG rating executives and analysts likely are not as receptive to negative news about firms that have won major ESG awards.
Therefore, even if a common owner communicates negative information about target firm's ESG performance and prospects, the information should be less openly received by rating executives and analysts in light of competing information about the firm's positive ESG record.
Again, the ESG award diminishes the receptivity of rating executives and analysts to negative information shared about the firm by a common owner.
In this sense, winning major ESG awards could protect target firms from having their ESG ratings undermined by their rivals' institutional investors.
<!-- para 44 -->
> [!hypothesis]
> **Hypothesis 3b.** The negative relationship between rivals' common ownership with a rating agency and the target firm's ESG rating from that agency is weaker when the target firm has won an ESG award.

## methods
<!-- para 3 -->
Our empirical setting is based on ESG ratings of U.S. firms provided by MSCI ESG Research LLC (“MSCI ESG Research”), a subsidiary of MSCI Inc.
MSCI ESG Research provides ESG ratings for approximately 8,500 firms globally through its MSCI ESG Ratings Time Series database (formerly Intangible Value Assessment [IVA] ratings).
As elaborated in Appendix A, MSCI ESG Research uses an internal process to separately score a firm’s performance along the respective dimensions of environment, social, and governance based on various factors and weights, and then determines an aggregate ESG rating based on its weights for each dimension.
<!-- para 4 -->
We used ratings by MSCI ESG Research as our empirical context because MSCI: (a) is a publicly traded firm, which allowed us to obtain information on its institutional investors' holdings; (b) is the largest and arguably most influential ESG rating provider in the world (Berg, Heeb & Kölbel, 2022; Simpson, Rathi & Kishan, 2021); and (c) provides the most extensive coverage.
Using a single rating agency also mitigates issues arising from divergence in the methodologies across different rating agencies (Berg, Koelbel & Rigobon, 2022; Chatterji et al., 2016).
<!-- para 5 -->
We collected remaining data from multiple sources, including institutional ownership data from Thomson Reuters Institutional (13F) Holdings; geographic operations data from 10-K filings (via EDGAR); firm financial data from Compustat; top management team (TMT) ownership data from Thomson Reuters Insider Filings; product market fluidity scores from Hoberg, Phillips, and Prabhala (2014); executive compensation data from Institutional Shareholder Services (ISS) Executive Compensation Analytics; board committee data from BoardEx; regulatory enforcement data from Violation Tracker; ESG controversies data from RepRisk; and ESG awards data from ASSET4.
Since coverage of the MSCI ESG Ratings Time Series database is not comprehensive before 2013, our sample period ranges from 2013 to 2019, where 2019 is the final year for which all data sources were available.
The sample includes 2,787 unique firms with 12,634 firm-year observations.
<!-- para 7 -->
Our independent variable, rival–MSCI CIO, captures common institutional ownership among a target firm’s industry rivals and MSCI.
After obtaining institutional ownership data from Thomson Reuters Institutional (13F) Holdings, we retained investors that owned more than 1% of outstanding shares in each firm (i.e., at least 1% in MSCI and 1% in another firm) because such investors have been shown to have sufficient incentives and power to actively intervene in corporate decision-making (Connelly et al., 2019; Gilje, Gormley & Levit, 2020).
The average number of investors with at least 1% ownership in MSCI each year is 20, making it feasible for MSCI executives to be aware of each large investor. [^6] Appendix B lists the names of common institutional owners that held more than 1% of the outstanding shares of MSCI as well as another firm during our sample period.
Among these 40 investors, three once held more than 10% of the outstanding shares of MSCI and nine once held more than 5%.
We used the following formula to calculate rival–MSCI CIO:
<!-- para 8 -->
$$ Rival-MSCI\ CIO_{j}=\frac{1}{n_{-j}}\sum_{-j}\left(\sum_{i}s_{i,-j}\times\sum_{i}s_{i,k}\right) \tag{1} $$
<!-- para 9 -->
where $j$ and $k$ denote the target firm and MSCI, respectively; $-j$ denotes target firm $j$'s rivals; $i$ indexes institutional investors with common ownership in a firm and MSCI; $s$ represents the percentage of ownership held by investor $i$ in either $-j$ or $k$; and $n_{-j}$ refers to the total number of rivals for the target firm $j$.
For each firm $j$'s rival–MSCI pairs, we first calculated the total ownership held by their common owners $\left(\sum_{i}s_{i,-j}\right)$ and $\sum_{i}s_{i,k}$ and then computed the product of the two ownership measures to capture each rival's common ownership with MSCI $\left(\sum_{i}s_{i,-j}\times\sum_{i}s_{i,k}\right)$.
We then calculated the average value of the product measure among all rivals to measure rival–MSCI CIO $\left(\frac{1}{n_{-j}}\sum_{-j}\left(\sum_{i}s_{i,-j}\times\sum_{i}s_{i,k}\right)\right)$.
<!-- para 10 -->
We identified rival firms using the fixed industry classification (FIC) (Hoberg & Phillips, 2010, 2016).
In developing the FIC, Hoberg and Phillips identified pairs of competitors using a firm-by-firm product similarity score derived from analyzing the text of firms' product descriptions in 10-K filings, where firms are legally required to report accurate descriptions of their key products. [^7] Based on the product similarity score in 1997, they used a clustering algorithm to generate a certain number (25, 50, 100, 200, 300, 400, and 500) of industry classifications that maximizes the overall product similarity score within each industry.
Holding the set of industries fixed over time, the authors have updated the constituents of each industry annually based on firms' most recent product descriptions.
<!-- para 12 -->
database provides ratings for firms' ESG on a 7-point scale.
Specifically, MSCI ESG Research divides industry-adjusted scores (0–10) into seven equal parts, each corresponding to a letter "grade" rating: AAA (8.571–10), AA (7.143–8.571), A (5.714–7.143), BBB (4.286–5.714), BB (2.857–4.286), B (1.429–2.857), and CCC (0–1.429) (MSCI, 2022a).
Following prior studies (e.g., Liang, Marquis, Renneboog & Sun, 2018), we converted each letter rating to a numeric value.
We assigned integer scores of 6 to 0 to the seven rating values from AAA to CCC, respectively.
Using this coding, we measured MSCI ESG rating as the average integer score in a year.

For Hypothesis 2a and Hypothesis 2b, our moderators are rival ESG media controversies and target firm ESG media controversies, respectively, which capture the reputational threats faced by portfolio firms and target firms based on their ESG activities.
Following existing studies (e.g., Kölbel, Busch & Jancso, 2017), we used the RepRisk database to construct these variables.
RepRisk screens more than 100,000 public sources in 23 languages daily to detect 28 types of ESG issues that can negatively impact firms' reputations.
For each firm-date observation, RepRisk reports the raw incident counts for each issue as well as a score for each incident's severity (harshness of the incident) and reach (potential influence of the focal media source).
RepRisk classifies each incident into one of three levels of severity and reach—high, medium, and low; which it scores, respectively, as 3, 2, and 1.
We used this coding to measure target firm ESG media controversies as the natural logarithm of 1 plus the number of ESG risk incidents weighted by the severity and reach in each firm-year.
We measured rival ESG media controversies as the average value of ESG media controversies among rivals with common institutional ownership with MSCI.
<!-- para 34 -->
For Hypothesis 3a and Hypothesis 3b, our moderators are rival ESG awards and target firm ESG awards, respectively, which capture the reputational opportunities encountered by portfolio firms and target firms that win ESG awards.
Following recent studies (e.g., Benjamin & Biswas, 2022; E-Vahdati, Wan-Hussin & Mohd Ariffin, 2023), we used the ASSET4 database to construct these variables.
ASSET4 provides information on more than 400 metrics related to ESG for more than 7,000 firms around the world as of 2020, and has been widely used in ESG research (e.g., Kölbel et al., 2017).
In terms of ESG awards, ASSET4 reports whether a firm received an external award for its social, ethical, community, or environmental activities in a year.
Rival ESG awards equals the percentage of rivals with common ownership with MSCI that received at least one ESG award in a year and target firm ESG awards equals 1 if a target firm received at least one ESG award in a year, and 0 otherwise.
<!-- para 36 -->
To capture common ownership in MSCI and the target firm, we first controlled for target, CIO, which is calculated using the following equation: Target firm–MSCI $CIO_j = \sum_i s_{i,j} \times \sum_i s_{i,k}$, where the denotations are equivalent to those in Equation (1).
As the relation between rival– (target firm–) MSCI CIO and MSCI ESG rating might be driven by investors' beliefs in the value of ESG, we controlled for rival–other agency CIO and target firm–other agency CIO to capture common ownership in other publicly traded ESG rating agencies. $^8$ We controlled for variables that capture the intensity of competition between the target firm and its rivals, including: (a) target firm–rival CIO, calculated as Target firm–rival $CIO_j = \frac{1}{n-j} \sum_{-j} \left( \sum_i s_{i,-j} \times \sum_i s_{i,j} \right)$, where the denotations are equivalent to those in Equation (1); (b) market overlap between the target firm and its rivals; $^9$ and (c) size similarity between the target firm and all of its rivals, calculated as Size similarity $p, q = 1 - \frac{|Total assets_p - Total assets_q|}{max_{m,n}|Total assets_m - Total assets_n|}$, where $p$ and $q$ denote the target firm and a rival, respectively, and $m$ and $n$ refer to the firms with the largest and the smallest total assets in each industry.
<!-- para 37 -->
We also controlled for target firm characteristics to account for firm heterogeneity in ESG ratings, including: (a) target firm size, measured as the natural logarithm of total assets; (b) target firm slack, which is captured by a composite slack index outlined by Tyler and Caner (2016); (c) target firm return on equity, measured as the ratio of operating income to total shareholder equity; (d) target firm market-to-book ratio, measured as the ratio of the market value of total assets to the book value of total assets; (e) target firm TMT ownership, measured as the percentage of shares held by top executives; (f) target firm institutional ownership (IO) concentration, measured as the Herfindahl index of a target firm's shareholdings by institutional investors; (g) target firm competitive threat, which is captured by the product market fluidity score (Hoberg et al., 2014); (h) target firm ESG compensation metric, which equals 1 if the target firm has at least one ESG-related executive compensation metric in a year, and 0 otherwise; (i) target firm ESG board committee, which equals 1 if the target firm has at least one ESG-related board committee in a year, and 0 otherwise; and (j) target firm ESG regulatory enforcements, measured as the natural logarithm of 1 plus the number of ESG-related regulatory enforcements as reported by Violation Tracker. [^10]
<!-- para 38 -->
To address cross-rival effects on ESG ratings, we followed Guo, Sengul, and Yu (2020) to control for rival characteristics, denoted by the word “rival” (e.g., rival firm slack).
Each variable is measured as the average value among all rivals identified by the FIC-100. [^11]
<!-- para 40 -->
Since our theory focuses on between-firm effects, we used pooled OLS models with industry-year fixed effects, where industries are defined by the FIC-100 (Hoberg & Phillips, 2010, 2016).
In each industry-year, the median (mean) value of the number of rivals with MSCI ESG ratings is 14 (24), and the minimum (maximum) value is 2 (189), making it feasible to make cross-sectional comparisons.
We clustered standard errors at the industry-year level to address potential correlations of residuals within each industry-year (Petersen, 2008).
To alleviate potential influence of outliers, we winsorized all continuous control variables at the 1st and 99th percentiles, but the results are similar without winsorizing.
Since investors most likely exert their influence on firms and ESG rating agencies during their current holding periods, we did not lag the explanatory variables, as doing so could cause incorrect inferences (Bellemare, Masaki & Pepinsky, 2017). [^12] However, such an empirical strategy may be subject to endogeneity concerns, which we addressed using a series of robustness checks.

In our main analyses, our dependent variable is available only for firms covered by MSCI ESG Research.
MSCI's coverage is based on stock indexes; thus, since 2013 (the start of our sample) all firms in the MSCI U.S.
Investable Market Index (IMI) are covered.
As unobservable heterogeneity could simultaneously drive whether a firm is included in the MSCI U.S.
IMI (and therefore covered by MSCI ESG Research) and the MSCI ESG rating for the firm (as managers' connections to MSCI may drive both decisions), there is potential for selection bias in our sample, which we addressed with Heckman models.

In the first-stage probit regression, we estimated the likelihood of a firm being included in the MSCI U.S.
IMI and thus covered by MSCI ESG Research.

Following Certo, Busenbark, Woo, and Semadeni (2016), we included two exclusion restrictions: metropolitan statistical area (MSA) coverage ratio and length of trading. [^17]

In constructing this variable, we generated scaled MSCI ESG rating and scaled ESG risk incident by scaling both MSCI ESG rating and ESG risk incident to an interval between 0 and 1 to make them comparable, measuring ESG risk incident in the same way as ESG media controversies described earlier.
We then measured MSCI-incident difference using the formula: MSCI-incident difference = scaled MSCI ESG rating - (1 - scaled ESG risk incident). [^20] Higher values indicate that MSCI ESG ratings deviated more from ESG incidents covered in the media.

Our main effect may be subject to omitted variable bias and reverse causality.
To mitigate these concerns, we conducted “quasi-natural” experiments on ownership changes associated with acquisitions of different ESG rating agencies.
Such acquisitions would have shifted the common ownership in an ESG rating agency and rated firms, but this may not have been driven by common owners.
Therefore, a pre- and post-acquisition analysis of ESG ratings around these events could illustrate to what extent the change in ESG ratings stemmed from the new common owners after these acquisitions.
We analyzed two acquisitions: MSCI’s acquisition of KLD and Thomson Reuters’s acquisition of ASSET4.
<!-- para 82 -->
In 2009, the ESG rating agency Kinder, Lydenberg, and Domini (KLD) was acquired by RiskMetrics, which was then acquired by MSCI in March 2010, making KLD wholly owned by MSCI.
Prior to the acquisition by MSCI, KLD was privately owned, meaning the common owners of a target firm’s rivals and MSCI would have been unable to influence the firm's ESG assessment by KLD; however, according to our theory, a negative relationship should exist after MSCI became the owner of KLD because common owners (of MSCI and other firms) could then influence KLD's ESG ratings.

As outlined in our study, the influence we theorize is likely to hinge on an investor's motivation and ability to influence rating agencies.
Following Bushee (1998), numerous studies have distinguished dedicated institutional investors, which have long investment horizons and concentrated portfolio holdings, from transient institutional investors, which have short investment horizons and diversified portfolio holdings.
Compared to their transient counterparts, and because of their more concentrated investments, dedicated institutional investors are assumed to have more power to influence their portfolio firms (Connelly et al., 2010; Oehmichen, Firk, Wolff & Maybuechen, 2021).
Moreover, given their long investment horizons, they are more motivated than transient institutional investors to exert their influence.
As such, if our theory is accurate, we should observe that our main effect is stronger for dedicated institutional investors than for transient institutional investors.

## discussion
<!-- para 2 -->
Viewing the conflicts of interest that plague information intermediaries through the lens of competitive dynamics, our study advances competitive dynamics research and expands the origins and reach of information intermediary bias.
Set in the context of common ownership in rating agencies, we have theorized and documented that firms receive worse ESG ratings from rating agencies when institutional investors' common ownership stakes in rival firms are higher.
In essence, these firms become competitive targets of their rivals' institutional investors.
We also posited and found that the ESG rating bias orchestrated by common owners is influenced by reputational threats and opportunities, which alter common owners' motivation and ability to use information intermediaries to advance their competitive agendas.
We now discuss some tenets of information-based competition and theoretical implications of our research.
