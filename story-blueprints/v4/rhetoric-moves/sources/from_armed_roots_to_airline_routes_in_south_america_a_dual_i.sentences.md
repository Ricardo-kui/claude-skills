# from_armed_roots_to_airline_routes_in_south_america_a_dual_i 句子库存

## introduction
<!-- para 2 -->
Imprinting theory has emerged as an important lens for understanding the enduring impact of historical context on contemporary organizations (Kipping & Üsdiken, [2014](#smj70084-bib-0055); Marquis & Qiao, [2025](#smj70084-bib-0071)), a phenomenon of growing interest in entrepreneurship (Suddaby et al., [2023](#smj70084-bib-0099)), strategy (Argyres et al., [2020](#smj70084-bib-0006); Cattani, [2005](#smj70084-bib-0016)), organization theory (Godfrey et al., [2016](#smj70084-bib-0036)), and international business (Qiao & Xia, [2026](#smj70084-bib-0086); Zhang, [2022](#smj70084-bib-0107)).
It contends that during a sensitive period, typically founding, organizations need to secure legitimacy and resources by establishing structures, routines, and other components to align with environmental requirements and/or stakeholder preferences for survival (Stinchcombe, [1965](#smj70084-bib-0097)).
This process yields imprints that persist and shape organizational outcomes enduringly (Marquis & Tilcsik, [2013](#smj70084-bib-0072); Simsek et al., [2015](#smj70084-bib-0096)).
<!-- para 3 -->
Despite the significant advances in organizational imprinting research, two critical gaps remain, limiting our understanding of historically oriented organizational phenomena.
First, existing studies have focused mainly on internal organizational imprints, such as structures, routines, and cultures (Marquis & Qiao, [2025](#smj70084-bib-0071)).
Yet, external imprints, such as the organizational identity as perceived by audiences outside of the organization (Korschun, [2015](#smj70084-bib-0056); Kovács & Horwitz, [2021](#smj70084-bib-0057)), have received less attention.
Importantly, internal and external imprints might compete for influence and exhibit varying levels of durability in their influence on organizational outcomes (i.e., effect persistence).
Second, previous research has typically assumed imprinting effects reflect characteristics, preferences, and actions of imprinters—entities responsible for creating the imprint (Simsek et al., [2015](#smj70084-bib-0096))—solely in that very sensitive period in the past.
In contrast, we posit that post-founding transformations of these imprinters can continuously shape the imprinting effect, even after their direct organizational involvement has ceased.
<!-- para 4 -->
We examine how founding relationships with a relatively understudied type of partner—the military (Hiatt et al., [2018](#smj70084-bib-0042))—shape firm expansion into international markets through two imprints: capability internal to the organization and identity external to it.
The internal capability imprint is manifested as partner-derived operational procedures, safety protocols, and organizational routines associated with military institutions that enhance performance, while the external identity imprint is manifested through enduring perceptions by external audiences that shape regulatory approval and market acceptance.
We specifically focus on international expansion because both imprints are particularly pronounced in foreign markets, where host-country stakeholders and regulators rely heavily on observable performance metrics and general impressions to evaluate unfamiliar firms under conditions of heightened uncertainty (Li et al., [2017](#smj70084-bib-0065), [2019](#smj70084-bib-0064)).
By tracing both imprints to the same founding condition, this framework demonstrates that internal and external imprints differ in the persistence of their effects and respond asymmetrically to post-founding changes involving the original imprinting partner.
<!-- para 5 -->
Our hypotheses are developed within the context of the South American airline industry where founding military partners simultaneously leave an internal capability imprint through disciplined operational procedures that facilitate internationalization and create an external identity imprint that may trigger national security concerns that hinder internationalization.
We further propose that internal capabilities demonstrate stronger durability over time.
Moreover, the influence of these imprints is moderated by post-foundation transformations of the original imprinter.
Specifically, the transition from military to civilian air traffic control diminishes internal capability advantages by democratizing safety capabilities previously exclusive to military-trained organizations, whereas military coups amplify negative external identity perceptions by heightening national security concerns among foreign regulators.
Contrary to General Douglas MacArthur's famous quote that “Old soldiers never die; they just fade away,” we demonstrate that both military imprints (capability and identity) persist rather than diminish quickly over time, though the durability of effects varies and their relative influence differs with the present-day situations of the imprinting military partner.
Our empirical study encompasses all airlines founded in 10 South American countries (Argentina, Bolivia, Brazil, Chile, Colombia, Ecuador, Paraguay, Peru, Uruguay, and Venezuela) from the industry's inception in 1919 through 1984.
Results support our hypotheses.
<!-- para 6 -->
This paper advances imprinting theory and strategy research in three ways.
First, we extend imprinting theory by identifying dual imprints—internal capabilities and external identity—that operate through fundamentally different mechanisms.
Internal imprints are embedded within organizational boundaries while external imprints reside in stakeholder cognitions beyond organizational control (Qiao & Xia, [2026](#smj70084-bib-0086)).
These dual imprints exhibit differential effect persistence, with internal imprints producing more consistent effects over time due to their effect on capabilities while external imprints produce more variable effects due to changing stakeholder perceptions.
Second, we show that imprinters continue shaping their organizational imprints through post-founding transformations, contrary to assumptions that imprinter influence diminishes after direct involvement ceases (Marquis & Qiao, [2020](#smj70084-bib-0070)).
Third, our study contributes to strategy research by answering one fundamental question—why firms persistently differ (Rumelt et al., [1994](#smj70084-bib-0091))—through demonstrating that lasting organizational heterogeneity reflects both internal capability differences and external identity variations that emerge from historical partner relationships.
This framework heeds calls for comprehensive theories better integrating history into strategy research (Argyres et al., [2020](#smj70084-bib-0006); Qiao, [2025](#smj70084-bib-0085)).

## theory
<!-- para 3 -->
Imprinting theory suggests that organizations are particularly susceptible to external influences during certain sensitive periods, especially at their founding (Marquis & Tilcsik, [2013](#smj70084-bib-0072); Stinchcombe, [1965](#smj70084-bib-0097)).
During these sensitive periods, prominent features, such as the founders' social connections and preferences (Qiao & Xia, [2026](#smj70084-bib-0086)), become imprints embedded within organizational structures, routines, cultures, and other components (Fisher et al., [2016](#smj70084-bib-0028); Marquis & Qiao, [2025](#smj70084-bib-0071)).
Once established, these imprints have lasting impacts on organizational behavior and strategy (Simsek et al., [2015](#smj70084-bib-0096)).
The existing imprinting literature predominantly emphasizes internal organizational elements, such as structures, routines, and cultures, that embody prominent characteristics from sensitive periods as carriers of imprints (Marquis & Qiao, [2025](#smj70084-bib-0071)).
For example, to obtain legitimacy and secure resources, new ventures must establish structures, such as a centralized reporting system to comply with the interstate banking law (Marquis & Huang, [2010](#smj70084-bib-0069)), and routines, such as acquiring resources in the peripheral markets to evade competition (Swaminathan, [1996](#smj70084-bib-0101)).
Once established, these internal organizational imprints tend to remain stable over time, thereby exerting a consistent and enduring influence on organizations.
<!-- para 4 -->
Yet we argue that founding conditions can also produce external organizational imprints as well, which exist outside of the organization's boundary but may significantly and enduringly shape its outcomes.
Unlike internal structural, routinized, or cultural elements, external organizational imprints may include collective memories, perceptions, and narratives maintained by external audiences, such as residential communities, consumer groups, media outlets, and external shareholders (Qiao & Xia, [2026](#smj70084-bib-0086)).
For example, external audiences developed lasting impressions of Hewlett-Packard based on the company's close association with some key partners during its founding stage, such as its mentor Stanford Professor Frederick Terman and the technology community.
This perception has endured long after the direct involvement of the founding partners had ceased (Malone, [2007](#smj70084-bib-0068)).
<!-- para 5 -->
Acknowledging external organizational imprints advances imprinting theory.
This broader perspective reveals that dual mechanisms exhibit different persistence patterns and respond distinctly to post-founding transformations of the imprinter (Boxenbaum & Jonsson, [2017](#smj70084-bib-0012)).
Unlike the persistence of internal organizational imprints within an organization's boundaries (Marquis & Qiao, [2025](#smj70084-bib-0071)), that of external organizational imprints is shaped by the audience outside of the organization and is less directly dependent on organizational efforts (Anteby & Molnár, [2012](#smj70084-bib-0005); Griffin & Bollen, [2009](#smj70084-bib-0037); Lange et al., [2011](#smj70084-bib-0059)).
For example, Jewish communities developed collective memories about firms that supported Adolf Hitler and the Nazi Party (Qiao & Xia, [2026](#smj70084-bib-0086)), despite that many paid reparations for those who suffered (Podoshen & Hunt, [2009](#smj70084-bib-0082)).
Further, our dynamic perspective advances imprinting theory beyond frameworks treating historical influence as static post-formation, demonstrating that imprinting effects may depend on ongoing relationships between organizational attributes established at founding and evolving characteristics of the original imprinter.
The total effect reflects not only the initial imprint, but also how subsequent imprinter transformations reshape their influence.
<!-- para 7 -->
We focus on the military as a relatively understudied but critical partner in the organizational imprinting and stakeholder literatures (Hiatt et al., [2018](#smj70084-bib-0042)).
While previous research has extensively examined traditional partners like investors, customers, and regulators, the military's role in shaping organizational practices, especially in industries like aviation, has received less attention.
This oversight is significant because most countries have a military and the military provides unique resources, technical expertise, and logistical capabilities that can transfer highly structured practices and disciplined routines to civilian ventures (Hiatt & Sine, [2014](#smj70084-bib-0043); Huntington, [1957](#smj70084-bib-0046)), and importantly, for most countries, air traffic control systems were historically and even some still are governed by the military (Gilbert, [1973](#smj70084-bib-0032); Kahne & Frolow, [2002](#smj70084-bib-0050)).
The airline industry, often regarded as a “national security industry” in which “the military maintained a consistent interest” (Schneider, [1991](#smj70084-bib-0093), 225, 228), exemplifies this dynamic.
Many South American airlines, such as Aerovías del Continente Americano S.A.
(AVIANCA) and Aerolíneas Argentinas, have leveraged military support to become key players internationally (Davies, [1984](#smj70084-bib-0020)).
Below, we explore why international expansion—successful establishment of new international routes (Sarathy & Ramamurti, [1997](#smj70084-bib-0092))—constituted a commonly shared strategic imperative for South American airlines and how founding military partners shaped this expansion through competing mechanisms.
<!-- para 8 -->
International expansion of airlines has historically been determined by a complex interplay of safety perceptions, national security considerations, and regulatory frameworks that emerged during the formative decades of commercial aviation.
Safety and operational reliability and the resulting passenger confidence have constituted a key determinant of successful international expansion, as foreign passengers, regulators, and governments relied heavily on perceived airline competence to evaluate the possibly unfamiliar carriers operating in their territories (Ho, [1996](#smj70084-bib-0045); Sultan & Simpson, [2000](#smj70084-bib-0100)).
Airlines needed to establish credible reputations for safety and operational discipline to overcome the inherent skepticism, particularly given the widely publicized mechanical failures and pilot errors that characterized early commercial aviation (Davies, [1984](#smj70084-bib-0020)).
Meanwhile, national security concerns emerged as a parallel constraint, with foreign governments viewing airlines as potential extensions of home country political and military apparatus, leading to restrictive ownership provisions, route limitations, and heightened scrutiny of carriers with perceived military or state connections (Chesterman & Lehnardt, [2007](#smj70084-bib-0017); Lehner, [1995](#smj70084-bib-0062)).
The tension between demonstrating operational competence to gain market access while avoiding triggering national security apprehensions created a delicate balance that airlines navigated through careful stakeholder management, strategic partnerships, and gradual market entry approaches that emphasized commercial rather than political objectives (Sarathy & Ramamurti, [1997](#smj70084-bib-0092)).
<!-- para 9 -->
For South American airlines, international expansion represented not merely a growth opportunity but a fundamental strategic necessity for reaching the necessary economies of scale that drove long-term viability.
Geographic and demographic constraints rendered domestic markets insufficient for sustainable operations.
Countries like Ecuador, Paraguay, and Uruguay possessed small populations that severely limited domestic passenger volume, while larger markets like Argentina and Brazil exhibited uneven population distributions concentrated in coastal urban centers, leaving vast interior regions economically unviable for aviation service (Davies, [1984](#smj70084-bib-0020); Doganis, [1985](#smj70084-bib-0021)).
International routes offered access to larger customer bases beyond these limited domestic markets, enabling airlines to achieve minimum efficient scale, diversify revenue streams, and mitigate risks associated with economic volatility and political instability concentrated in single national markets.
International expansion also enhanced brand recognition and operational legitimacy: establishing airlines as credible global carriers helps attract strategic partners with established international hubs (Doganis, [1985](#smj70084-bib-0021)).
The economic imperative proved so compelling that virtually all airlines attempted international expansion (Davies, [1984](#smj70084-bib-0020)).
Hence, the South American context offers limited variation in expansion willingness but substantial variation in expansion success, allowing us to isolate airlines' ability to establish international routes successfully.
We leverage this critical context to understand how both internal capabilities and external stakeholder perceptions shape organizational outcomes through their differential influence on market access as well as effect persistence.
<!-- para 10 -->
We propose that military–partner relationships established through initial investments and board memberships during an airline's founding period generate enduring effects through two distinct imprints: internal capabilities embedded within operational procedures and external identity manifested in stakeholder perceptions.
For instance, Aerolíneas Argentinas and VARIG, both established with significant military influence, carried both superior military-grade safety procedures and a longstanding perception of their close association with Argentine and Brazilian militaries respectively (Emboaba-Moreira & Guimaraes-Ferrer, [2012](#smj70084-bib-0025); Lewis, [2000](#smj70084-bib-0063)).
We then argue that these imprints can produce divergent effects on international expansion: military-derived capabilities may facilitate expansion through demonstrable operational excellence, whereas military-associated identity may, on average, trigger national security concerns that complicate market entry efforts.
<!-- para 12 -->
We argue that founding partners can imprint the focal organization with internal capabilities that demonstrate remarkable persistence.
Because militaries globally were early adopters of aviation technology, they developed superior procedures and technology very early in the industry's emergence.
For example, Argentina and Chile's militaries adopted aviation as early as 1912 and 1913 (Davies, [1984](#smj70084-bib-0020)), just 9–10 years after the Wright brothers' first flight.
Military collaborations during the industry's emergence and particularly during a firm's founding period influenced a firm's internal capabilities through the systematic transfer of advanced operational procedures, standardized maintenance protocols, and rigorous safety cultures from military aviation contexts to civilian airline operations (Amador, [2012](#smj70084-bib-0004)).
<!-- para 13 -->
Military organizations possessed highly structured operational frameworks that were developed through years of experience managing complex aviation systems under demanding conditions, including comprehensive maintenance schedules, detailed procedural checklists, and systematic crew training programs that prioritize operational excellence and risk mitigation.
When military personnel assume founding roles in civilian airlines, they embed these proven operational frameworks within organizational structures, routines, and cultures, creating internal organizational imprints that can persist after direct military involvement has ceased.
This occurs through multiple mechanisms.
First, the codified military-derived procedures create structural inertia, making modifications too costly due to cascading changes across interconnected systems (Hannan & Freeman, [1984](#smj70084-bib-0038)).
Second, military-trained human capital, and their systematic implementation of military training created organizational memory stored in coordination patterns, becoming institutionalized and automatic through repeated enactment (Cohen & Bacdayan, [1994](#smj70084-bib-0018)).
These embedded capabilities manifest in observable outcomes including superior safety performance (e.g., reflected in lower accident rates) and/or technical expertise, maintenance support, and training resources inherited from the association with military institutions.
Third, tacit knowledge reinforces persistence, as military-derived expertise in managing complex aviation systems under demanding conditions involves significant tacit knowledge that resists articulation and transfer, making capabilities difficult to replicate or modify even when strategically desired (Szulanski, [1996](#smj70084-bib-0102)).
<!-- para 14 -->
The transfer of military aviation protocols produces measurable improvements in operational performance, as evidenced by airlines with military founding partners demonstrating superior maintenance reliability, crew proficiency, and systematic approaches to safety management that distinguish them from purely civilian-founded competitors.
For example, Uruguay's TAMU (Transportes Aéreos Militares Uruguayos) inherited military infrastructure and processes to achieve exceptional operational stability and reliability (Amador, [2012](#smj70084-bib-0004)), and LLOYD Aéreo Boliviano harnessed its military partner ties for reputable safety performance (Olivares, [2019](#smj70084-bib-0080)).

The internal capability imprint is likely to affect international expansion prospects through its influence on operational performance credibility.
The superior capabilities inherited from the military can provide competitive advantages in operational performance, safety records, and regulatory compliance that foreign stakeholders value when evaluating unfamiliar carriers.
Airlines with embedded military-derived capabilities demonstrate measurable operational excellence through lower accident rates, superior on-time performance, and enhanced maintenance reliability that create competitive differentiation in international markets where performance credibility is essential (Kaleka & Morgan, [2017](#smj70084-bib-0051)).
Foreign regulators conducting safety assessments for market entry approval evaluate operational capabilities through systematic auditing processes that favor airlines with demonstrated safety cultures and disciplined operational procedures, making military-derived internal capabilities particularly valuable for securing regulatory approval in stringent international markets.
The systematic nature of military-derived procedures creates scalable operational frameworks that facilitate rapid international expansion by providing proven templates for establishing consistent service quality across diverse geographic markets, securing regulatory approval and passenger trust for their international expansion.
For example, Brazil's VARIG, benefiting from military-derived practices, established a robust record for safety that enabled successful entry into highly regulated international markets like the U.S. and Europe (Emboaba-Moreira & Guimaraes-Ferrer, [2012](#smj70084-bib-0025)).
Similarly, Ecuador's Transportes Aéreos Militares Ecuatorianos leveraged its superior military-influenced pilot training programs in challenging operational environments like the Andean region (Davies, [1984](#smj70084-bib-0020)), reassuring passengers and regulators alike of their capability for international expansion.

We then suggest that founding military partners can imprint the focal organization with an external identity through lasting associations with military institutions, which can become embedded in stakeholder categorizations, collective memories, and media narratives (Anteby & Molnár, [2012](#smj70084-bib-0005); Fisher et al., [2016](#smj70084-bib-0028); Kovács & Horwitz, [2021](#smj70084-bib-0057)).
Airlines founded with military partners often adopted names that explicitly signaled these associations, such as Ecuador's Transportes Aéreos Militares Ecuatorianos (TAME) and Uruguay's Transportes Aéreos Militares Uruguayos (TAMU), or maintained organizational symbols and branding that communicated military heritage to external audiences (Davies, [1984](#smj70084-bib-0020)).
These naming choices reflected the profound impact that military partners had on venture founding, and some founders prominently featured these connections in organizational nomenclature.
<!-- para 22 -->
Organizational names serve as intentional identity signals that shape external audience perceptions and evaluations (Glynn & Abzug, [2002](#smj70084-bib-0035); Lee, [2001](#smj70084-bib-0061)).
These names influence stakeholder categorizations and resource access by signaling category membership and organizational characteristics (Khessina & Carroll, [2008](#smj70084-bib-0053); Navis & Glynn, [2010](#smj70084-bib-0076)).
The external identity imprint persists through the stakeholder-held categorizations and collective understandings rather than only the names themselves.
Once established, names become embedded in stakeholder memories, regulatory classifications, media narratives, and collective schemas, shaping how external audiences interpret organizational actions (Anteby & Molnár, [2012](#smj70084-bib-0005); Griffin & Bollen, [2009](#smj70084-bib-0037)).
In the context of market entry evaluations, foreign regulators may encounter airline names as identification markers that trigger associations with military institutions regardless of current operational reality.
These stakeholder-held perceptions constitute the external organizational imprint (Qiao & Xia, [2026](#smj70084-bib-0086)), encompassing both the collective understanding and categorization maintained by external audiences based on historical military associations.
<!-- para 23 -->
The persistence of external military identity operates through mechanisms fundamentally different from internal capabilities.
Collective memories among stakeholders create enduring perceptions as audiences accumulate associations between the airline and military institutions over time.
These memories prove stable, with airlines like Ecuador's TAME and Uruguay's TAMU maintaining military nomenclature for decades despite potential international stigmatization, demonstrating that external stakeholder recognition constrains organizational identity choices (Glynn & Abzug, [2002](#smj70084-bib-0035); Keller, [1993](#smj70084-bib-0052)).
Accumulated reputation generates self-reinforcing loops, guiding subsequent evaluations through established categorizations (Rindova et al., [2006](#smj70084-bib-0089)).
Foreign stakeholders particularly depend on symbolic cues and historical associations when evaluating unfamiliar carriers, making military heritage a salient categorization criterion (Kovács & Horwitz, [2021](#smj70084-bib-0057); Palmer, [2025](#smj70084-bib-0081)).
Thus, we hypothesize that:

<!-- para 25 -->
We contend that external identity imprint might shape internationalization of the organization.
The external military identity is likely to create significant barriers to international expansion by triggering national security concerns among foreign regulators and stakeholders who perceive military-affiliated airlines as potential extensions of home country political and security apparatus.
Airlines bearing military nomenclature or historical military associations face heightened scrutiny from foreign aviation authorities who evaluate carrier applications through national security lenses, particularly in strategically sensitive markets where concerns about espionage, intelligence gathering, or geopolitical influence are paramount (Price & Forrest, [2024](#smj70084-bib-0084); Teece, [2022](#smj70084-bib-0103)).
The accumulated reputation for military affiliation, initially valuable domestically, generates fear and suspicion internationally as stakeholders interpret military partner ties as indicating political rather than purely commercial motivations.
Regulatory agencies in North America and Europe have consistently emphasized national security considerations when evaluating foreign firms with military or government backgrounds (Li et al., [2017](#smj70084-bib-0065), [2019](#smj70084-bib-0064)), implementing more stringent approval processes, enhanced operational restrictions, and ongoing monitoring requirements that significantly complicate market entry efforts (Lehner, [1995](#smj70084-bib-0062)).
<!-- para 26 -->
A historical case that exemplifies this dynamic is SCADTA (Sociedad Colombo-Alemana de Transportes Aéreos), founded in Colombia in 1919.
The airline carried a strong military identity imprint through its name (“Alemana” referencing German heritage with military connotations), ownership, and early management, which raised geopolitical concerns in the interwar period.
U.S. regulators, citing national security risks and fears of espionage, barred SCADTA from operating routes north of the Panama Canal and imposed restrictions that effectively excluded the carrier from key North American markets.
The airline's inability to overcome these perceptions, despite operational competence, contributed to sustained barriers to international expansion (Gill, [1990](#smj70084-bib-0033)).
These regulatory barriers operate similarly to restrictions facing state-owned enterprises in international mergers and acquisitions, where concerns about political influence create systematic disadvantages for expansion into foreign markets (Li et al., [2017](#smj70084-bib-0065), [2019](#smj70084-bib-0064)).
Public perception compounds these regulatory challenges, as passengers and consumer groups in target markets may view military-imprinted airlines as instruments of political influence rather than purely commercial entities, diminishing consumer trust and reducing commercial viability (Chesterman & Lehnardt, [2007](#smj70084-bib-0017)).
The external locus of control over military-associated identity means that airlines cannot easily overcome these perceptions through internal operational improvements.
Companies with stigmatized histories sometimes undergo costly rebranding efforts, including name changes, to distance themselves from negative legacies (Miller et al., [2014](#smj70084-bib-0075); Zhao et al., [2018](#smj70084-bib-0108)).
A notable example is the United Fruit Company, which was rebranded as United Brands and later Chiquita to obscure its controversial history in Latin America (Bucheli et al., [2023](#smj70084-bib-0013); Bucheli & Kim, [2015](#smj70084-bib-0014)).
Despite rebranding efforts, historical associations may remain accessible through regulatory classifications, media narratives, and distributed stakeholder memories that operate beyond organizational control (e.g., Anteby & Molnár, [2012](#smj70084-bib-0005); Dowling, [2000](#smj70084-bib-0022); Ocasio et al., [2016](#smj70084-bib-0078); Qiao & Xia, [2026](#smj70084-bib-0086); Ravasi et al., [2019](#smj70084-bib-0087)).

<!-- para 29 -->
We suggest that internal capability imprints exhibit more persistent effects than external identity imprints due to fundamental differences in their embedding mechanisms.
Although both characteristics endure beyond initial formation periods, internal capabilities are embedded in organizational structures, routines, and tacit knowledge systems, enabling consistent operation despite environmental changes.
In contrast, external identity imprints, while stable across extended periods in collective memories and narratives, can produce more variable effects on organizational outcomes as stakeholder interpretations shift with changing contexts.
The differential effect persistence stems from distinct storage locations and change mechanisms operating on these organizational attributes.
<!-- para 30 -->
First, internal capabilities reside in organizational processes, such as knowledge embedded in routines, procedures, and coordination patterns maintained through daily enactment (Nelson & Winter, [1982](#smj70084-bib-0077)).
These processes create procedural knowledge that becomes automatic and produces consistent performance effects, as they are resistant to conscious modification (Cohen & Bacdayan, [1994](#smj70084-bib-0018); Feldman & Pentland, [2003](#smj70084-bib-0027)).
Their effects on operational performance remain stable over time, decaying mainly through sustained disuse rather than deliberate change.
In contrast, external identity resides in stakeholder memories, perceptions, and narratives, requiring continued audience attention for maintenance (Gioia et al., [2000](#smj70084-bib-0034); Qiao & Xia, [2026](#smj70084-bib-0086)).
While identity markers endure through accumulated reputation and collective memories (Fombrun, [1996](#smj70084-bib-0029); Glynn & Abzug, [2002](#smj70084-bib-0035)), their effects on stakeholder evaluations are more variable.
Audience-held interpretations shift with media narratives, political events, and expectation violations, even when organizational characteristics remain constant (Rindova et al., [2006](#smj70084-bib-0089)).
The nature of organizational processes creates more stable performance effects than collective memories, which are updated by autonomous stakeholders based on salient events, leading to varying stakeholder responses over time.
<!-- para 31 -->
The change mechanisms underlying internal capabilities and external identity imprints also contribute to their differential effect persistence.
Internal capabilities produce more persistent effects due to structural inertia and tacit knowledge, which make modifications costly and difficult (e.g., Hannan et al., [2003](#smj70084-bib-0039); Hannan & Freeman, [1984](#smj70084-bib-0038)).
These factors ensure capabilities continue influencing performance consistently.
In contrast, external identity, while persisting through collective memories and accumulated reputation (Fombrun, [1996](#smj70084-bib-0029); Khessina & Carroll, [2008](#smj70084-bib-0053); Navis & Glynn, [2010](#smj70084-bib-0076)), produces more variable effects on outcomes.
Audience-held perceptions can be articulated and communicated, allowing diffusion through media and information cascades that shift stakeholder evaluations more rapidly (Gioia et al., [2000](#smj70084-bib-0034); Qiao & Xia, [2026](#smj70084-bib-0086)).
Identity-image reciprocity creates ongoing reinterpretation, making external identity effects more responsive to environmental changes than capability effects governed by structural interconnections and tacit knowledge.
Therefore, we propose:
<!-- para 32 -->
Hypothesis 3.*The internal capability imprint exhibits more enduring effects on international route establishment than the external identity imprint*.
<!-- para 34 -->
We further examine how post-founding transformations of the imprinting partner reshape the effects of internal capability and external identity imprints.
Founding military partners establish enduring imprints, but subsequent changes to the military institution can modify how these established imprints influence organizational outcomes.
This dynamic perspective recognizes that imprinting effects depend on concurrent characteristics of the original imprinter (Marquis & Qiao, [2020](#smj70084-bib-0070)), creating relationships where the same foundational imprint produces varying effects depending on whether the imprinting partner maintains or loses institutional authority and legitimacy (Simsek et al., [2015](#smj70084-bib-0096)).
We examine two mechanisms through which post-founding imprinter transformations alter imprint expression: transition from military to civilian air traffic control diminishes the military's authority and capability advantages, and military coup, which amplifies identity stigmatization.
<!-- para 36 -->
We argue that civilian systems that displace military authority over aviation operations diminish the competitive advantage of internal capability imprints.
As civilian systems implement standardized International Civil Aviation Organization (ICAO) protocols, military-specific expertise is transformed into broadly accessible civilian standards, reducing the distinctiveness of military-connected airlines' capabilities.
Initially, the internal capabilities acquired by military-connected airlines, such as advanced operational procedures and rigorous safety cultures, provided a competitive advantage due to the military's early monopoly on aviation expertise.
However, institutional transitions such as establishing civilian air traffic control systems eventually enabled all carriers to achieve similar performance, regardless of military heritage, thereby diminishing the advantages conferred by military partners and specific capabilities.
<!-- para 37 -->
Military and civilian air traffic control systems operate through fundamentally different procedural frameworks that reflect their distinct operational priorities and safety philosophies (Kritzinger, [2006](#smj70084-bib-0058)).
Military air traffic control employs specialized procedures, such as Military Assumes Responsibility for Separation of Aircraft operations that permit formation flying with aircraft separated by as little as one mile horizontally, tactical penetration descents utilizing steep approach angles to minimize exposure time, and Precision Approach Radar systems requiring continuous controller guidance through voice commands that demand intensive pilot-controller coordination (Price & Forrest, [2024](#smj70084-bib-0084)).
Conversely, civilian air traffic control implements standardized ICAO procedures that have made airspace and aviation safer and independent of the military processes and protocols (Isaac & Ruitenberg, [2017](#smj70084-bib-0047)).
Specifically, standardized ICAO procedures emphasize larger separation minimums of three to five nautical miles, consistent three-degree glideslope approaches optimized for passenger comfort and fuel efficiency, and instrument landing system approaches that transfer navigation responsibility to aircraft equipment rather than controller expertise.
These civilian transitions are typically accompanied by comprehensive technological modernization that further democratized aviation capabilities.
For example, Bolivia's establishment of its civil administrative body for the management of air traffic control and airport services—Administración de Aeropuertos y Servicios Auxiliares a la Navegación Aérea (AASANA)—in 1967 included radar system installation and full ICAO Annex 11 compliance that eliminated reliance on military navigation expertise.
Argentina's civilian authority upgrade incorporated Mode S radar technology and systematic ICAO safety standards that provided all carriers with equivalent technical capabilities regardless of personnel training background.
<!-- para 38 -->
The implementation of civilian air traffic control thus subordinates military institutional authority while simultaneously diminishing the competitive advantage of military-imprinted capabilities, as standardized civilian protocols combined with advanced radar systems enable most carriers to achieve relatively equivalent operational performance independent of their military heritage.
AVIANCA's experience during Colombia's protracted transition from military oversight under Decree 1682 in 1934 to civilian authority through Decree 1956 in 1951 illustrates this mechanism.
The airline's initial advantages from military-derived operational discipline became less distinctive once Colombia implemented ICAO-compliant procedures that democratized advanced operational capabilities across all domestic carriers, reducing AVIANCA's unique competitive positioning in international markets where standardized procedures had become the norm (Billings, [2018](#smj70084-bib-0011)).
The internal capability imprint remains embedded in organizational routines, but its influence on facilitating international expansion diminishes as the transition from military to civilian air traffic control universalizes previously distinctive operational knowledge and procedures.
Consequently, we hypothesize:
<!-- para 39 -->
Hypothesis 4.*The transition from military to civilian air traffic control negatively moderates the relationship between founding military partner ties and international route establishment*.
<!-- para 41 -->
Finally, we propose that a military coup exacerbates the negative effects of external identity imprints by heightening stakeholder threat perceptions associated with military affiliations.
As external identity imprints are shaped by audience perceptions, they are particularly sensitive to changes in imprinter legitimacy and reputation.
Military coups, for instance, can transform military institutions from symbols of competence to agents of instability, amplifying the negative effects of military-associated identity on international expansion.
Foreign regulators and stakeholders may interpret historical military partners as indicators of security risks, triggering regulatory barriers and diplomatic restrictions.
<!-- para 42 -->
Specifically, military coups signal fundamental breakdowns in democratic governance and institutional stability that intensify the negative valence of collective memories surrounding military-affiliated organizations.
Foreign governments interpreting coup events through national security lenses may perceive airlines with military associations as potential instruments of authoritarian regimes rather than independent commercial entities, transforming what might have been neutral or positive military heritage into active political liabilities (Bell, [2016](#smj70084-bib-0009); Chesterman & Lehnardt, [2007](#smj70084-bib-0017)).
The mechanism operates through both immediate diplomatic responses and lasting stigmatization that constrains market access long after initial political upheaval subsides, as stakeholder categorizations shift from “military-trained means competent” to “military-connected means threatening.” The reputation from its historical military legacy, initially signaling operational discipline, becomes reinterpreted through the lens of the coup, with information cascades among foreign regulators and media rapidly diffusing threat narratives that override previous positive associations.
<!-- para 43 -->
Aerolíneas Argentinas during Argentina's 1976–1983 National Reorganization Process provides an example of this dynamic.
The March 24, 1976 military coup led by Lieutenant General Jorge Rafael Videla transformed the airline's military founding heritage from operational credential into an active liability that foreign governments associated with state terrorism and international aggression.
The 1982 Falklands War crystallized these concerns, resulting in the United Kingdom's complete closure of airspace to Aerolíneas Argentinas that forced circuitous routing through Madrid for London services and significantly weakened the airline's competitiveness on lucrative North Atlantic routes for 8 years until the democratic restoration in 1990 enabled gradual normalization.
This pattern of coup-triggered aviation restrictions extended beyond bilateral conflicts, as the European Economic Community's April 1982 total import ban from Argentina demonstrated how military government actions could isolate national carriers from entire regional markets through coordinated diplomatic responses that treated airlines as extensions of their home country's political apparatus rather than independent commercial entities.
The persistent external identity imprint linking Aerolíneas Argentinas to military institutions remained embedded in stakeholder categorizations, but the coup amplified its negative expression by elevating threat perceptions that transformed historical military partners into disqualifying political liabilities.
Stated formally, we propose:
<!-- para 44 -->
Hypothesis 5.*Military coup negatively moderates the relationship between founding military partner ties and international route establishment*.

## methods
<!-- para 3 -->
Our primary data sources included R.E.G.
Davies ([1984](#smj70084-bib-0020)) *Airlines of Latin America Since 1919*, his global airline statistics archived at the Smithsonian National Air and Space Museum, and historical issues of *Flight International* magazine.
The dataset covers 282 airlines founded in 10 South American countries (Argentina, Bolivia, Brazil, Chile, Colombia, Ecuador, Paraguay, Peru, Uruguay, and Venezuela) from the industry's inception in 1919 to 1984, the latest year for which data were available.
Of these, 98 airlines expanded internationally, operating routes to 51 foreign countries.
While most routes connected South American nations, destinations like the U.S., Spain, France, and Germany featured prominently outside the continent.
Following prior literature (Berry et al., [2010](#smj70084-bib-0010)), we treated all 51 countries as potential destinations.
This firm-country-year dataset tracks the international expansion potential of each airline from its founding to 1984, offering a detailed view of how South American airlines navigated geographic markets over time.
<!-- para 6 -->
Our dependent variable, geographic expansion, was measured by the establishment of new international routes by airlines.
We measured this variable based on Davies ([1984](#smj70084-bib-0020)), excluding non-operational ones such as those that were experimental or proposed but not implemented.
We coded the variable as 1 if the focal airline established an international route in a country in a given year, and 0 otherwise.
To ensure reliability, two research assistants and one co-author independently coded the variable, achieving an agreement rate of above 95%.
The discrepancies were resolved through collaborative review, returning to source materials and cross-referencing with supplementary aviation histories and archival records.
<!-- para 8 -->
Founding military ties (1 = yes, 0 = no) was defined as a high-ranking military officer (colonel or above) or their close relative (sibling or spouse) being part of the airline's founding senior management team, advisory board, or a major investor, as per Hiatt et al. ([2018](#smj70084-bib-0042)).
We coded this variable with archival information from the Smithsonian National Air and Space Museum.
<!-- para 10 -->
Our first mediator is an airline's internal capability imprint manifested through operational partnerships with military institutions, capturing behavioral manifestations of embedded military-derived procedures and expertise.
We measured military operational partnership as a binary variable indicating whether an airline has logistics support, shared use of military airports/airfields/bases, operational partnerships such as aircraft leasing arrangements, route transactions, military personnel employment, or collaborative training programs (1 = partnership present, 0 = absent).
These can shape specific safety behaviors (Miller et al., [2018](#smj70084-bib-0074)).
Two research assistants and one coauthor independently coded this variable from Davies ([1984](#smj70084-bib-0020)), achieving 97% initial agreement.
Again, the discrepancies were resolved through collaborative review returning to source materials and cross-referencing with supplementary archival records.
Table [^1] exhibits some examples.
Our alternative measure of internal capability imprint concerns operational safety, measured as airline-specific accident propensity.
We first manually coded the accident occurrence and then decomposed it into systematic risk from environmental factors and idiosyncratic risk reflecting airline-specific capabilities, with residuals indicating capability-based safety performance.
Details of this measure are in Robustness Checks.
<!-- para 11 -->
The second mediator captures an airline's external identity imprint, measured by whether its name carries explicit military connotations.
We classified this using a keyword-based approach, looking for terms historically associated with military aviation.
These included words such as *militar*, *naval*, and *aeronáutica*, as well as naming structures that resemble formal military units, such as numbered groups or squadrons (e.g., “Grupo de Transportes #8 (SATCO)”, “LAN/TAM/Grupo de Transportes Numero 41”, and “Alemana”).
Such patterns are common in the official designations of military organizations across Spanish, Portuguese, and English contexts.
To ensure robustness, airline names were checked for partial and case-insensitive matches, allowing us to capture explicit military references even when they appeared in compound names or abbreviations (e.g., acronyms).
<!-- para 13 -->
We included two moderators in our analysis.
The first one concerns the transition from military to civilian air traffic control.
We searched for legal documents (e.g., Supreme Decree No. 08019 for Bolivia, Decree 1956 of 1951 for Colombia, and Law 73/90 for Paraguay), government websites of these countries (e.g., Chile and Uruguay), and Civil Air Navigation Services Organization (e.g., Brazil and Venezuela) to determine when the transition happened (1 = happened already, 0 = no).
Second, we accounted for military coups, coded as 1 if the observation is after a domestic military coup occurred and 0 otherwise from the Coups Agency and Mechanisms database and the Colpus dataset.
<!-- para 15 -->
We controlled for various environmental- and firm-level variables used in prior work.
Specifically, we first controlled for host-country conditions from the Cross-National Time-Series Data Archive (Banks & Wilson, [2023](#smj70084-bib-0008)): GDP per capita, fiscal budget balance, total trade per capita, ratio of defense expenditure over government expenditures, and military size in manpower.
These variables capture the macroeconomic environment, international operations, and military influence and are from Hiatt et al. ([2018](#smj70084-bib-0042)).
Additionally, we controlled for institutional quality using the Polity database's institutionalized autocracy variable (Marshall et al., [2009](#smj70084-bib-0073)), which comprises political executive recruitment competitiveness, executive recruitment openness, chief executive constraints, election participation regulation, and election participation competitiveness.
We also included host-country conditions like GDP per capita and population size, as well as institutional distance between home and host countries based on the Polity database scores to account for potential barriers facing emerging market airlines entering foreign locations, many of which were developed Western nations with substantially different institutional environments.
The results are similar if we use the absolute value of institutional distance.
<!-- para 16 -->
We then controlled for competition and firm-level characteristics to account for potential influences on new geographic market entry.
Specifically, we measured competition using airline-sector density (number of operating airlines by country-year) and airline-sector age (years since a country's first airline was founded).
At the firm level, we controlled for whether the focal airline acquired another airline that had founding military ties and whether the airline has active military ties to better attribute our findings to the imprints built by the airline's own founding military ties.
In addition, we included airline size (logarithm of passenger seats in active aircraft), airline age, foreign ownership, state ownership, and political ties.
Political ties were defined as having large shareholders, board members, or top leaders with close political connections (Hiatt et al., [2018](#smj70084-bib-0042)).
We also included a binary variable indicating whether the airline was regional for mainly flying routes within the country's borders defined by Davies ([1984](#smj70084-bib-0020)) to account for potential experience effects on international expansion (Kim & Aguilera, [2016](#smj70084-bib-0054)).
Finally, we controlled for geopolitical factors between home and host countries in shaping airline expansion, and importantly these variables could alleviate the concerns that the baseline rate of entry from certain home-host country pairs might differ.
First, warfare between the two countries might be related to national security concerns of airlines and military coup and affect airlines' international expansion.
This variable, measured as whether there were any wars between the focal dyad of home and host countries till the observational year, was from the Correlates of War database.
In addition, to account for the costs of setting up international routes (e.g., Sarathy & Ramamurti, [1997](#smj70084-bib-0092)), we controlled for whether the host country and home country are geographically contiguous (1 = yes, 0 = no), especially considering that many South American airlines were focused on setting up international routes within the same continent.
<!-- para 18 -->
The data were organized into a firm-country-year panel.
To estimate our model, we transformed the dependent variable into a “hazard rate” and estimated it under the single-event survival analysis framework.
Hazard rate is the instantaneous probability of the first occurrence of new geographic market entry conditional on no prior occurrence up to time *t* within a given time spell.
Namely, .
Although Cox proportional hazard model might be more robust to different assumptions of distributions, our data analysis with the partial residual approach showed that the proportionality assumption did not hold (Schoenfeld, [1982](#smj70084-bib-0094)).
<!-- para 19 -->
Following established recommendations for modeling social event data (Carroll & Hannan, [2000](#smj70084-bib-0015); Hiatt et al., [2009](#smj70084-bib-0044)), we employed exponential piecewise survival models that provide semi-parametric flexibility without imposing restrictive parametric assumptions about monotonic hazard patterns.
This approach allows hazard rates to vary across temporal segments, accommodating non-monotonic patterns characteristic of organizational events while maintaining analytical tractability.
We divided our observation period into meaningful segments based on major aviation industry transitions: the early growth period (1919 through 1950) characterized by military aviation dominance, limited commercial infrastructure, and reciprocal engine technology, and the jet age period (1950 to the early 1980s) marked by the institutionalization of civilian air traffic control, the adoption of ICAO standards, and mass travel (Rhoades & Hinebaugh, [2025](#smj70084-bib-0088)).
This theoretically driven temporal division captures the institutional shifts central to our hypotheses regarding imprinter transformations and capability democratization, providing interpretable baseline hazard estimates.
We estimated separate baseline hazards for each period by including a dummy variable reflecting whether it is after 1950, enabling flexible accommodation of shifting expansion dynamics across these distinct eras.
We also used decadal dummies as a robustness check.
<!-- para 21 -->
Our framework incorporates mediation effects.
We followed the established approaches and recent recommendations in performing the mediation analysis (Aguinis et al., [2017](#smj70084-bib-0001); MacKinnon et al., [2002](#smj70084-bib-0067); Shaver, [2005](#smj70084-bib-0095)) with the following models:

$$
\text{dependent variable} = \alpha \text{ independent variable} + \text{controls} (1)
$$
$$
\text{mediator} = \beta \text{ independent variable} + \text{controls} (2)
$$
$$
\text{dependent variable} = \gamma \text{ independent variable} + \delta \text{ mediator} + \text{controls} (3)
$$

Given we have multiple mediators, the percent-of-total-effect-mediated statistic and the related coefficient differential approach (compare the magnitude of and ) might be hard to interpret, and thus we did not use the related approach (Hayes, [2018](#smj70084-bib-0041)).
<!-- para 25 -->
An important consideration is that founders of airlines might self-select whether they would connect to the military, creating an endogeneity issue.
Furthermore, comparing Models (1) and (3), Shaver ([2005](#smj70084-bib-0095)) suggested that Model (1) might be mis-specified due to an omitted variable, which is mediator in this case, and recommended an instrumental variable analysis.
We focused on natural disasters in the airline's home country as an instrumental variable.
First, natural disasters are exogenous, reflecting “nature's fury” (Ballesteros et al., [2017](#smj70084-bib-0007); Dutta, [2017](#smj70084-bib-0023): 443), and are not affected by airlines' international expansion.
Second, natural disasters might expose limitations of formal institutions, such as written rules and regulations, for acquiring strategic resources from the state (Ghimire, [2021](#smj70084-bib-0031); Lin, [2014](#smj70084-bib-0066)), requiring firms to seek informal means, such as forming military ties.
Third, the existing outward foreign direct investment literature has suggested that airlines typically consider distance, host market institutions, demand, and their capabilities when adding international routes (e.g., Sarathy & Ramamurti, [1997](#smj70084-bib-0092)).
Natural disasters, hence, may predict founding military partner ties but have a limited effect on global expansion directly (Oh & Oetzel, [2022](#smj70084-bib-0079)).
So, the instrument may satisfy exclusion–restriction conditions.
We obtained data on the number natural disasters (with over 1000 deaths) from the EM-DAT International Disaster Database and used it as an instrument.

On a ceteris paribus basis, airlines with founding military partners are 61.2% more likely to have military operational partnership than those without such a connection (mean = 0.269).
Column 4 then shows that operational partnership of the focal airline facilitates its international expansion (*β*  = 2.465, *p*  <.001) or around 10.77 times quicker in establishing an international route (mean hazard rate = 10.6%).
These effects are both substantial compared to the respective mean levels.
So, the causal-step approach supports mediation.
Meanwhile, the product coefficient statistic (*z*  = 25.457, *p*  <.001) also supports mediation.
Thus, Hypotheses [1a](#smj70084-mthst-0001 "Link to mathStatement") and [1b](#smj70084-mthst-0002 "Link to mathStatement") are supported.

Column 6 illustrates that the founding military tie is positively related to airline internationalization (*β*  = 4.829, *p*  <.001).
These results indicate that the internal capability imprint is stronger than the external identity imprint in shaping airline internationalization.
However, this method is sensitive to finite sample bias, often resulting in larger coefficient estimates of the instrumented variable and thus should be interpreted with caution (Qiao, [2025](#smj70084-bib-0085)).

Hypothesis [^2] stipulates that internal capability imprints exert more persistent effects than external identity imprints in shaping airline internationalization.
To facilitate clear interpretation of interaction coefficients, we mean-centered the continuous moderator variable (airline age) before creating interaction terms with the two binary imprint measures (military operational partnership and military name).
Mean-centering improves interpretability while leaving interaction coefficients unchanged (Aguinis et al., [2017](#smj70084-bib-0001); Aiken et al., [1991](#smj70084-bib-0002); Echambadi & Hess, [2007](#smj70084-bib-0024)).
Binary variables require no centering as their values retain meaningful interpretation, and other variables in the model similarly do not need transformation for this analysis.
The results in Column 7 of Table [^5] show that the interaction between airline age and internal capability imprint (operational partnership) is positive (*β*  = 0.060, *p*  <.001), indicating that capability effects on international expansion strengthen over time as airlines accumulate operational experience.
The interaction between airline age and external identity imprint (military name) is also positive (*β*  = 0.080, *p*  <.001), indicating that identity effects erode over time (with the main effect being negative) as stakeholders gradually forget about initial associations and interpretations shift with changing political contexts and media narratives.
Because one independent variable is positively and the other is negatively related to the dependent variable, we reverse-coded military name connotations to make them more consistent and then compared these coefficients with a Wald test.
The test confirms that these interaction coefficients differ significantly (*χ* <sup>2</sup>  = 60.08, *p*  <.001), demonstrating that internal capability and external identity imprints exhibit statistically distinguishable temporal dynamics.
The results are consistent with our findings on the total effects of the two imprints reported in Columns 3 and 6 of Table [^5].
These findings support our Hypothesis [^2] on differential effect persistence.

We conducted several additional analyses to ensure the robustness of our results.
First, we alleviated potential endogeneity issues due to the self-selection of connecting to the military during the founding period by employing three approaches: (1) entropy balancing weighting, (2) a time-based discontinuity design anchored to World War II, and (3) a subsample of airlines whose founding military partners were less of their own choosing due to birth in the military.
We then reran the analyses using (1) an alternative measure of the internal capability imprint based on the data of airline accidents, (2) an alternative sample of airlines without active military ties, (3) discrete-time models—logistic models with time fixed effects—as an alternative to the continuous-time survival analysis (Allison, [1982](#smj70084-bib-0003); Jenkins, [1995](#smj70084-bib-0049)), (4) controlling for home-host country-dyad fixed effects to further account for the possibility that the baseline hazard rates might differ across these dyads, and (5) an alternative specification with decadal intervals for the exponential piecewise models.
In the following, we detail these analyses.
<!-- para 16 -->
We first employed the entropy balancing matching (EBM) approach.
Like propensity score matching (PSM) and coarsened exact matching (CEM), EBM also matches treatments with controls and other variables.
However, while PSM and CEM drop a significant portion of observations, EBM does not drop observations but tries to make the statistical moments (mean, standard deviation, skewness, and even kurtosis) similar between the treatmen and control groups.
Table [S2](#support-information-section) shows the covariate balancing results after EBM: after matching, the differences of all control variables, in terms of their means, between the treatment and control groups become negligible.
These results suggest that the treatment we have can be seen as random to the extent that we have ruled out selection on these observable variables.
The EBM results, reported in Columns 6 and 7 of Table [^6], are consistent with those in Columns 3 and 4 in Table [^5] on the differing effects of the two imprints.
<!-- para 18 -->
To further address endogeneity concerns, we employed a time discontinuity design, exploiting the systematic reduction in the likelihood of having founding military partner(s) during World War II.
Our identification strategy recognizes that the global conflict significantly altered the desirability and availability of military associations for civilian airline founders, creating exogenous variation in founding military tie likelihood.
This variation enables clearer identification of main effects, namely, the relationship between founding partners and airline internationalization.
The discontinuity operates through two concurrent mechanisms: reduced supply of military expertise as Allied forces prioritized wartime efforts over civilian training programs, and decreased demand from airline founders who increasingly viewed military associations as politically risky due to increased military polarization and emerging international stigmatization of military involvement in civilian enterprises, especially those related to the Axis powers (Potash, [1969](#smj70084-bib-0083); Rouquié, [1987](#smj70084-bib-0090)).
This timing creates an exogenous variation in whether airline founders pursued military partners, independent of their strategic preferences or operational needs, enabling identification of the net effect of founding military ties on subsequent international expansion outcomes.
<!-- para 19 -->
We created a time-discontinuity sample selecting airlines founded between 1933 and 1945 across South American countries.
Airlines founded before 1942 operated in environments where military partner ties remained readily available and socially acceptable, as many South American countries maintained military missions from France, Germany, Italy, and the U.S. that provided aviation expertise and training.
However, airlines founded after 1942 faced fundamentally altered conditions as the European military relationships became politically problematic: Germany and Italy were Axis powers at war with Allied nations, the significant U.S. involvement in the war since December 1941, while France had surrendered to Nazi Germany in 1940, tainting French military associations.
Forming new military ties after this threshold risked association with discredited Axis powers or compromised Allied states, creating stigmatization.
Our time discontinuity design thus captures this natural experiment where post-1942 founding substantially reduced both strategic desirability and opportunities of pursuing military partner relationships.
<!-- para 20 -->
We conducted balance tests comparing airlines founded before versus after 1942 on other observable characteristics (excluding age, which differs by design in temporal discontinuity approaches).
An ideal discontinuity design exhibits no systematic differences between treatment and control groups along covariates.
Results presented in Column 1 of Table [S3](#support-information-section) show that, except for airline size, all characteristics are statistically indistinguishable between the two groups of airlines.
Because size does differ, we present discontinuity results both with and without control variables.
Columns 2 and 3 of Table [S3](#support-information-section) show positive total effects of founding military ties on airline internationalization.
Our result in Column 2 of Table [S3](#support-information-section) remains similar if we focus on airlines in countries that hosted either German or Italian missions in the past.
<!-- para 22 -->
We further alleviate the endogeneity issue by using a sample with more exogenous founding military ties.
According to Davies ([1984](#smj70084-bib-0020)) and materials we gathered, some airlines were born in the military (i.e., air force) of these countries, such as Fast Air, Transporte Aéreo Militar Uruguayo, Línea Aérea Nor-Este, Líneas Aéreas del Estado, and Linea Aeropostal Santiago-Arica.
For these airlines, their founding military partner ties were not of their own choosing.
Using this subsample, we found similar results for the effects of founding military ties (i.e., Column 3 of Table [^5]) in Column 4 of Table [S3](#support-information-section).
<!-- para 24 -->
Our alternative measure of internal capability imprint concerns operational safety, capturing airline-specific accident propensity.
Following established finance methodology for isolating firm-specific risk components (Fama & French, [1993](#smj70084-bib-0026)), we decompose accident data into systematic risk attributable to factors beyond airline control and idiosyncratic risk more as a result of airline-specific capabilities.
Specifically, we regress accident occurrence on year fixed effects capturing temporal variation in aviation technology and safety standards, country-dyad fixed effects and the transition from military to civilian air traffic control (the moderator for this mediator).
The residuals from this regression capture airline-specific accident propensity—deviations from expected accident rates given environmental conditions—that more accurately reflect internal capability differences while acknowledging that crash data contain noise from exogenous environmental factors such as aircraft design flaws or weather events.
Two research assistants and one coauthor independently coded the underlying accident occurrence variable (1 = accident occurred, 0 = no accident) based on three comprehensive sources and cross-checked for accuracy: (1) Davies ([1984](#smj70084-bib-0020)), (2) the Bureau of Aircraft Accident Archives, and (3) the Aviation Safety Network, achieving 96% initial agreement with discrepancies resolved through collaborative review and source triangulation.
<!-- para 25 -->
We then computed airline-specific accident propensity residuals for each airline-year observation, with negative values indicating better-than-expected safety performance (lower accident propensity) and positive values indicating worse-than-expected performance given environmental conditions.
To maintain consistency, we reverse-coded the residual so that a more positive value reflects better internal capability, calling it airline safety.
Columns 5 and 6 of Table [S3](#support-information-section) show the results: (1) founding military ties are less (*β*  = 0.231, *p*  <.001 or 23.1%) prone to idiosyncratic accidents more attributed to the focal airline (i.e., safer from accidents) and (2) safety benefits airline internationalization (*β*  = 4.424, *p*  =.010).

We conducted final four robustness checks reported in Table [S4](#support-information-section).
First, restricting analysis to airlines without active military ties (Panel A) demonstrates that founding military ties, rather than contemporaneous relationships, drive our results, with internal capability and external identity effects persisting even after military ties cease to exist.
Yet, external identity effects become insignificant when restricting to airlines without active military ties, suggesting that severing operational connections may attenuate audience-held identity associations, or alternatively reflecting reduced empirical variation in this restricted subsample.
Second, discrete-time survival models using logit specifications with time fixed effects (Panel B) yield consistent results, confirming robustness across estimation approaches (Allison, [1982](#smj70084-bib-0003); Jenkins, [1995](#smj70084-bib-0049)).
Third, controlling for home-host country-dyad fixed effects (Panel C) addresses potential confounds from bilateral relationships beyond our existing time-varying controls, with most hypothesized effects remaining robust.
Fourth, alternative temporal segmentation using decadal intervals (Panel D) rather than our theoretically motivated two-period specification produces similar coefficient estimates, confirming results are not sensitive to temporal break choices (Hiatt et al., [2009](#smj70084-bib-0044)).
For these last two analyses, the interaction involving the transition from military to civilian air traffic control becomes insignificant as these specifications absorb country-year variation inherent in the moderator.

## discussion
<!-- para 2 -->
This study explores how founding partners create enduring effects on ventures through dual imprinting pathways: internal capabilities and external identity.
Founding military ties generate internal capabilities through embedded operational procedures that facilitate international expansion, while simultaneously creating external identity through military associations that trigger national security barriers impeding international expansion.
Second, the internal capability imprints exert more enduring influence than their external identity counterparts.
Further, concurrent changes to the imprinter significantly reshape these effects: the transition from military to civilian air traffic control subordinates imprinter authority and weakens capability advantages by standardizing procedures, while military coups tarnish imprinter reputation and thus amplify identity barriers by intensifying security concerns.
