# gulati_1999_network_location_and_learning_the_influence_of_n 句子库存

## introduction
<!-- para 2 -->
In the last few years much has been written about the rapid proliferation of strategic alliances.
A strategic alliance is commonly defined as any voluntarily initiated cooperative agreement between firms that involves exchange, sharing, or codevelopment, and it can include contributions by partners of capital, technology, or firm-specific assets (e.g., Harrigan, 1985; Gulati, 1995a, 1995b).
While there have been numerous theoretical and empirical accounts of the formation of alliances, the primary focus has been on understanding some of the resource-based considerations that promote the formation of alliances (e.g., Berg, Duncan, and Friedman, 1982; Mariti and Smiley, 1983; Hagedoorn, 1993).
The emphasis on material resources and capabilities as catalysts for alliances resonates closely with the resource-based view of the firm, which highlights the importance of material resource endowments (Wernerfelt, 1984; Dierickx and Cool, 1989; Barney, 1991; Mahoney and Pandian, 1992).
While resource-based strategy research has typically focused on explaining sustained performance differences across firms, the role of resource heterogeneity in explaining strategic change and strategic actions is becoming more salient (e.g., Kraatz and Zajac, 1999).
<!-- para 3 -->
The emphasis of prior research on the material–resource considerations that may lead to the creation of alliances has led to a neglect of important social imperatives that may also be influential.
In his seminal book, Andrews (1971) claimed that the strategic actions of firms are the outcome of a match between a firm's existing competence and the availability of new opportunities.
Scholars approaching alliance formation from a resource-based perspective have primarily focused on the existing competence (or lack thereof) that may propel firms to enter into new alliances.
They have generally paid less attention to important factors that could influence the availability of and access to alliance opportunities in the first place.
Thus, in Andrews' terms, they have focused primarily on the competence side of the conditions that propel strategic actions and not so much on the conditions that determine the opportunity set firms may perceive.
<!-- para 4 -->
Social factors resulting from the embeddedness of firms in a rich social context can be influential in altering the opportunity set perceived by firms.
By neglecting such factors, prior accounts that focus on competence-based drivers for alliance formation implicitly consider firms as atomistic actors engaging in strategic actions in an asocial context (Baum and Dutton, 1996).
The external context remains encapsulated within measures of competitiveness in product or supplier markets, and there is limited consideration of the fact that the opportunity set a firm may perceive for strategic actions can be influenced in important ways by the social structural context in which it is placed.
Economic sociologists have convincingly demonstrated how the social structure of ties within which economic actors are embedded can influence their subsequent actions (e.g., Granovetter, 1985).
They have shown that the distinct social structural patterns in exchange relations within markets shape the flow of information (Burt, 1982; Baker, 1984).
This in turn provides both opportunities and constraints for actors and can have implications for their behavior and performance.
Such an embeddedness perspective, which highlights the salience of networks, is applicable to both individual and interorganizational networks (Baker, 1990; Podolny, 1993; Gulati, 1995b; Powell, Koput, and Smith-Doerr, 1996; Gulati and Gargiulo, 1999).
Viewed from this standpoint, much of the research on strategic alliances represents an undersocialized account of firm behavior.
<!-- para 5 -->
Recently, scholars have brought a network perspective to the study of strategic alliances (Gulati, 1998).
The focus of some of these studies has been to explain the creation of interorganizational networks by examining the role of key industry events in shaping alliance networks (Madhavan, Koka, and Prescott, 1998) and the role of networks in the choice of partners in alliances (Gulati, 1995b).
Scholars have also linked networks to the extent of R&D and non-R&D alliances by firms (Powell et al., 1996), to the cumulative frequency of future alliances by firms (Kogut, Shan, and Walker, 1992; Eisenhardt and Schoonhoven, 1996), to alliances with new partners (Walker, Kogut, and Shan, 1997), to the precise nature of interfirm relationships (Gulati, 1995a; Gulati and Singh, 1998), and to their effects on the structure and performance of alliance relationships (Zaheer and Venkatraman, 1995; Dyer, 1996).
All these accounts have advanced our understanding of strategic alliances and extended the embeddedness perspective, previously applied to the study of individuals and their networks, to firms and their interorganizational networks.
They have, however, paid less attention to building a dynamic model that takes into account the influence of evolving networks on the proclivity of firms to enter into new alliances over time or to providing a detailed assessment of the mechanisms underlying the effect of networks on firms.
There have also been few accounts that have drawn on field research with managers involved in forming alliances to inform our understanding of the role of interfirm networks on new alliances.
<!-- para 6 -->
In this paper, I examine the question: What determines which firms enter into alliances and which do not?
Rather than focusing on dyads, as some previous work has done (e.g., Gulati, 1995b; Powell et al., 1996; Gulati and Gargiulo, 1999), I focus on the firm level and consider social factors that influence the extent to which firms participate in alliances over time.
I draw on extensive fieldwork that suggests that although strategic alliances are essentially dyadic exchanges, key precursors, processes, and outcomes associated with them can be defined and shaped by the networks within which most firms are embedded.
I introduce the notion of firm network resources and propose that firms accrue such resources from the interfirm networks in which they are located.
Such resources, in turn, influence the extent to which firms enter into new alliances.
<!-- para 7 -->
By introducing network resources as important for alliance formation, this study advances the perspective that strategic actions of firms are influenced by the social context in which they are embedded (Baum and Dutton, 1996; Dacin, 1997; Oliver, 1997). [^1] In this paper, the context is conceptualized as providing firms with valuable resources that enable them to identify new opportunities.
Such resources are becoming more salient as firms are increasingly situated in multiplex interfirm networks that can provide them with information about new business opportunities, which can be particularly valuable in a global setting.
<!-- para 8 -->
In this study, I examine the time-varying influence of network resources on the alliance behavior of a panel of firms over a 9-year period.
These network resources accrue from the participation of firms in the interfirm network resulting from accumulated prior alliances by industry participants.
For each time period in this study, this network includes all alliances that have been formed until the previous period.
This interfirm network of cumulated prior alliances updates each year to incorporate the new alliances formed, and this new network influences alliance formation for subsequent periods.
Thus, when observed over time, the formation of new ties in each period alters the very same network that influenced the new ties.
The temporal element is key here and results in an endogenous network dynamic between embedded firm action and the network structure that guides but is also transformed by that action (Gulati and Gargiulo, 1999).
<!-- para 9 -->
The large-sample empirical study reported here was preceded by extensive interviews with managers involved in alliance decisions at a variety of firms.
A total of 153 interviews were conducted at 11 firms over a 2-year period.
The questions asked were initially open-ended ones about the kinds of factors that led to the creation of new alliances and became increasingly focused, once the primary factors had been identified.
The importance of the interfirm network of prior alliances was uncovered during this fieldwork, prior to the large-sample study.
The fieldwork provides grounding for claims about the role of networks and the kinds of information that flow through them.
I draw on selective quotes from some of the managers I interviewed wherever relevant in the paper.

## theory
<!-- para 3 -->
Network resources inhere not so much within the firm but in the interfirm networks in which firms are located.
They are a specific form of firm resources that can be considered to be ‘strengths that firms can use to conceive of and implement their strategies’ (Barney, 1991: 101).
They are distinct from the resources that reside securely within its boundaries and are the source of valuable information for firms.
The amount of such resources available to firms can influence their strategic behavior by altering the opportunity set available to them.
The greater the information, the larger the opportunity set. [^2] While scholars developing the resource-based perspective have highlighted the importance of social factors and also the role of unique firm history, no attention has been given to network resources that emerge from firms’ participation in interfirm networks (Barney, 1991).
Network resources are distinct from ‘external capabilities’ highlighted by Langlois (1992) as capabilities produced by and residing in a specialized market network.
Instead, firm network resources result from the informational advantages they obtain from their participation in interfirm networks that channel valuable information.
A firm’s network resources result from its unique historical experience and, as I will discuss, come about in a unique path-dependent process in which both the frequency of its past ties and also the identity of its partners are critical.
To understand why network resources resulting from interfirm networks are important for firms and their alliances, we need to consider the factors usually associated with the formation of alliances.
<!-- para 4 -->
For firms to build alliances that effectively address their needs while minimizing the risks
<!-- para 5 -->
posed by moral hazard concerns, they must first be aware of the existence of potential partners and have an idea of their needs and requirements and, second, have information about the reliability of those partners (Balakrishnan and Koza, 1993).
Simultaneously, those potential partners must also have requisite information on the focal firm.
Firms entering alliances face considerable moral hazard concerns because of the unpredictability of the behavior of partners and the likely costs to a firm from opportunistic behavior by a partner, if it occurs.
Despite the rapid growth of both domestic and international alliances in many industrial sectors, such partnerships are still considered risky (Kogut, 1989; Doz, Hamel, and Prahalad, 1989; Gulati, Khanna and Nohria, 1994).
A partner may either free-ride by limiting its contributions to an alliance or simply behave opportunistically.
Such concerns are further compounded by the unpredictability of many alliances.
All these conditions create a significant informational hurdle for the creation of alliances.
<!-- para 6 -->
Given the ambiguities and uncertainty associated with alliances, access to valuable information can lower search costs and alleviate some of the risks of opportunism, making firms more likely to enter such partnerships.
An important source of such information is the networks in which firms are located.
The importance of social networks in shaping the flow of information and opportunities has been discussed by economic sociologists (Granovetter, 1985; Baker, 1990; Mizruchi, 1992).
Recent evidence suggests that firms, as well as individuals, enter into embedded ties with each other that are characterized by trust and rich information exchange across organizational boundaries (Eccles, 1981; Useem, 1982; Dore, 1983; Powell, 1990).
While the focus has been on comparing such ties with market transactions, there has been limited emphasis on the implications of the cumulation of such ties into interfirm networks.
A network of embedded ties accumulated over time can become the basis of a rich information exchange network that enables firms to learn about new alliance opportunities with reliable partners (Kogut, Shan, and Walker, 1992; Gulati, 1995b; Powell et al., 1996).
<!-- para 7 -->
The informational advantages bestowed by networks of interfirm ties can be conceptualized as a network resource.
The embeddedness of firms in networks that serve as a basis of their network resources can both restrict and enlarge the opportunity set of alliances available to them.
By influencing the extent to which firms have access to information about potential partners, interfirm networks can help a firm identify partners that it perceives as viable for alliances.
Similarly, networks channel information to potential partners and thus alter the extent to which partners consider a focal firm to be viable.
But networks may also constrain a firm's set of choices for alliances by limiting the circle of potential partners about whom it has access to information, and for non-participants it provides no information at all, which may lock them out of new opportunities.
<!-- para 8 -->
The network resources firms can receive from their participation in interfirm networks is akin to the social capital of individuals (Walker et al., 1997).
In his seminal essay on social capital, Coleman (1988: S98) suggested:
<!-- para 9 -->
social capital inheres in the structure of relations between actors and among actors.
It is not lodged either in the actors themselves or in physical implements of production.
Because purposive organizations can be actors ('corporate actors') just as persons can, relations among corporate actors can constitute social capital for them as well.
<!-- para 10 -->
Earlier scholars have described social capital as a resource akin to financial and technological capital that exists within the social relationships in which an actor is embedded (Loury, 1977, 1987; Bourdieu, 1986).
It has been studied at multiple levels of analysis, ranging from societies, nations, and communities (Fukuyama, 1995; Putnam, 1993, 1995) to individuals (Useem and Karabel, 1986; Coleman, 1988; Belliveau, O'Reilly, and Wade, 1996).
At higher levels of aggregation, social capital has generally been used to describe the features of social organization that facilitate coordination and cooperation for mutual benefit (Putnam, 1993: 35–36), while at the individual level social capital accrues from the pattern of contact networks in which individuals are placed.
Scholars within the resource-based perspective have also highlighted the role of organizational capital resources that has among its elements the network of contacts a firm may have with its external environment (Tomer, 1987).
In this context, I consider the implications of network resources not so much for the performance of firms but, rather, as an important enabling condition for future cooperation.
<!-- para 11 -->
Most firms are embedded in a variety of interfirm networks, such as board interlocks, trade associations, and R&D cooperatives.
To conceive of any of these networks as a basis for network resources it is important to consider the extent to which the network serves as a channel of information and the kind of information it transmits (Gulati and Westphal, 1999).
One increasingly important network that has become an influential channel of information is the network of prior alliances (Kogut et al., 1992; Gulati, 1995b).
This network encompasses the historical context of accumulated alliances that industry participants have entered until the previous year.
With the rapid proliferation of alliances in the last several decades, most firms are now embedded in such a network of prior alliances.
Most alliances involve prolonged contact between partners, and firms actively rely on such networks as conduits of valuable information that can be a catalyst for new alliances.
Such networks are dynamic however, and change over time.
At any given time, this network encompasses all past alliances.
With the formation of new alliances this network updates, and it is the new network that becomes influential for subsequent firm behavior.
<!-- para 12 -->
The informational advantages from the network of prior alliances can enable the creation of new alliances by three distinct means: access, timing, and referrals (Burt, 1992).
Access refers to information about current or potential partners as to their capabilities and trustworthiness—an existing network can influence a firm's available set of choices of feasible partners and its attractiveness to other firms as a partner.
This is vividly illustrated by the comments of an alliance manager I interviewed:
<!-- para 13 -->
Our network of [prior alliance] partners is an active source of information for us about new deals [alliances].
We are in constant dialogue with many of our partners, and this allows us to find many new opportunities with them and also with other firms out there.
<!-- para 14 -->
Thus, it seems that firm managers actively seek information about new alliances from their prior alliance partners, both with the previous partner and with other firms.
<!-- para 15 -->
Timing entails having informational benefits about potential partners at the right time.
A firm seeking attractive alliance partners must approach them at the right time and preempt their seeking alliances elsewhere.
Another sample quote from a manager shows that this firm relied extensively on its partners from its interfirm network of past alliances for information:
<!-- para 16 -->
They [our partners] are familiar with many of our projects from their very inception, and if there is potential for an alliance we discuss it.
Likewise, we learn about many of their product goals very early on, and we actively explore alliance opportunities with them.
<!-- para 17 -->
Another manager highlighted the salience of timing of information as a critical facet of the information provided by his firm's social networks:
<!-- para 18 -->
And so, even for alliances to happen the confluence of circumstances have to be at the right time.
We and our prospective partner must know about each other's needs and identify an opportunity for an alliance together in a timely manner...
Our partners from past alliances are one of our most important sources of timely information about alliance opportunities out there, both with them and with other firms with whom they are acquainted.
<!-- para 19 -->
Thus, in addition to having access to information about each other, the close contact resulting from prior alliances ensures that partners learn about joint opportunities for alliances in a timely manner.
<!-- para 20 -->
A third benefit of the information firms receive through the network of prior alliances results from the indirect referrals firms provide to each other about their previous partners.
Thus, information exchange is not limited to a firm's immediate network ties but also depends on how a firm is situated in the network.
One mechanism by which the location in a network operates is through third-party referrals, in which prior partners may refer their own other partners to a focal firm for partnership.
As a result, two firms may learn about each other by virtue of sharing one or more alliance partners in common, who may have introduced them and perhaps vouched for their reliability.
As one manager I interviewed pointed out,
<!-- para 21 -->
In some cases we realize that perhaps our skills don't really match for a project, and our partner may refer us to another firm about whom we were unaware ...
An important aspect of this referral business is, of course, about vouching for the reliability of that firm.
Thus, if one of our long-standing partners suggests one of their own partners as a good fit for our needs, we usually consider it very seriously.
<!-- para 22 -->
Such comments suggest that firms' ability to enter new partnerships is influenced by indirect ties in the network of their past alliances.
A firm may enter an alliance with a particular firm based on the fact that it already shares a number of partners in common.
As networks channel information through both direct and indirect contacts, such resources accrue not only from the extent of participation in networks but also from a firm's location within them.
As a result, the specific patterning of a firm's ties allows it access to different degrees of information.
<!-- para 23 -->
The information exchange in alliance networks can go beyond indirect referrals and encompass the whole network.
A manager I interviewed at a large computer software firm put it this way:
<!-- para 24 -->
No matter how much effort you expend in market intelligence efforts, you can never know about all the firms there are out there ...
If you step back and look at the entire industry, it's becoming like a spider's web, where we all learn something about each other through the network.
<!-- para 25 -->
The network of prior alliances is a rich source of information from which firms can also learn about new firms of which they were previously unaware.
These factors can be powerful enabling conditions that enhance the likelihood that a firm will engage in additional alliances in the future.
<!-- para 26 -->
> [!hypothesis]
> Hypothesis 1: The greater the extent of a firm's network resources from the network of prior alliances, the greater the likelihood that it will enter a new alliance in the subsequent year.
<!-- para 28 -->
Membership in a network of prior alliances requires firms to have entered such ties in the past.
By participating in alliances, firms can develop capabilities with forming alliances that accrue as a result of historical processes of learning (Barney, 1991; Dierickx and Cool, 1989).
Thus, another outcome for firms resulting from their membership in alliance networks is that they can develop managerial capabilities associated with forming new alliances.
<!-- para 29 -->
While strategy scholars have primarily applied various capabilities-based arguments to explain sustained performance differences across firms, variation in capabilities can also be the basis for strategic behavior (e.g., Kraatz and Zajac, 1998).
In this instance, my concern is not with technological or material resource-based capabilities but with organizational capabilities that enable firms to form alliances with greater ease.
Alliances are complex organizational arrangements that can require multiple levels of internal approval, significant search in identifying partners, detailed assessments for ratifying contracts, and considerable management attention to sustain the partnership (Ring and Van de Ven, 1994; Gulati et al. 1994; Doz, 1996).
<!-- para 30 -->
Due to the considerable managerial challenges associated with forming alliances, let alone managing them, the possession of alliance formation capabilities can be a significant catalyst for firms considering new alliances.
This is vividly exemplified in the comments of one of the managers I interviewed:
<!-- para 31 -->
Forming a new alliance is not as easy as you might think.
There are considerable political, legal, and organizational hoops to be jumped ...
In my experience, some firms are wonderfully adept with forming these things [alliances].
They have systems, procedures, and personnel, all of which click together to make new alliances happen.
<!-- para 32 -->
An important basis for alliance formation capabilities is learning from prior experience.
Early evidence of organizational learning from prior experience was provided by the extensive empirical research on learning curves that suggests a lowering of production costs with experience (e.g., Fudenberg and Tirole, 1984; Ghemawat and Spence, 1985).
There is also evidence from research on organizational learning that firms may build organizational capabilities from experience and then engage in repeat experiences that further refine their capabilities.
Levinthal and March (1993) talked about how firms engage in 'exploitation' of existing capabilities, as opposed to 'exploration' of new prospects, due to their propensity for continued use and development of existing skills.
Along similar lines, evolutionary economists and organizational theorists have suggested that firms are driven by routines and that, once routines come into place, firms may engage in similar sets of activities repeatedly and improve upon those routines (Nelson and Winter, 1982; Amburgey, Kelly, and Barnett, 1993).
Some of the bases for such learning include building appropriate routines that result from a process of 'error detection and correction in theories in use' (Argyris and Schon, 1978).
With greater experience, firms can also enhance their absorptive capacity from alliances (Cohen and Levinthal, 1990).
Furthermore, top managers within the firm may acquire cognitive mindsets that focus their attention on forming new alliances as a primary strategic avenue for growth (Boeker, 1997).
<!-- para 33 -->
Evidence from previous research on strategic alliances suggests that the benefits of experience can translate into specific skills on the formation and management of alliances (Lyles, 1988; Amburgey, Dacin, and Singh, 1996; Dyer and Singh, 1998; Anand and Khanna, 1997; Arregle, Amburgey, and Dacin, 1997).
Once firms begin to enter alliances, they can internalize and refine specific routines associated with forming such partnerships.
A comment by a manager I interviewed exemplifies this point:
<!-- para 34 -->
One thing that also makes it easier for us to enter new alliances is our extensive experience with doing alliances.
Forming a new partnership is not a big deal any more—we have our own formula and we know it works!
<!-- para 35 -->
Another vice president I interviewed said:
<!-- para 36 -->
'Through our vastly successful technology partnerships program we have built ourselves a reputation in the industry for being an effective and reliable alliance partner.
Today, we are pursued by other firms to enter alliances much more frequently than we pursue potential partners...
This reputation is not a false myth we try and perpetuate, but rather is grounded in reality.
Our vast experience with alliances has allowed us to build important skills for the creation and management of alliances.
<!-- para 37 -->
The benefits firms experience with alliances extend beyond gaining visibility and external recognition from their participation in alliance networks, which makes them attractive partners to other firms, to building capabilities with alliances.
Some firms in which I interviewed set up separate organizational units to assist with the creation and management of their strategic alliances, and others had developed standardized procedures to facilitate the creation of new alliances.
These included clarifying decision-making authority, setting guidelines for projects considered appropriate for alliances, specifying company-wide legal framework for alliances, and creating a checklist of ex ante issues to be considered for the future management of alliances.
Separate organizational units to oversee alliances provided valuable initial input to specific divisions considering alliances in the form of legal and managerial templates that covered relevant issues.
They also helped them interface with their legal department and provided guidelines to consider in selecting a partner.
In several instances such units also disseminated information about alliances as a strategy to their managers and scanned the market for new alliance opportunities.
As a result, in firms experienced with alliances, managers were more familiar with alliances and there were systems and structures in place to simplify the tasks associated with the creation of new alliances along with an acceptance by top management that such ties could be strategically viable and valuable.
I found consistent evidence that firms built up concrete alliance formation capabilities with experience, which, in turn, enabled them to form new alliances with greater ease and greater frequency.
Applying these ideas to the formation of alliances suggests that firms with greater alliance formation capabilities are likely to enter new alliances most frequently.
This can be summarized in the following hypothesis:
<!-- para 38 -->
> [!hypothesis]
> Hypothesis 2: The greater a firm's alliance formation capabilities, the greater the likelihood that it will enter a new alliance in the subsequent year.

## methods
<!-- para 3 -->
The data used in this study were gathered from a variety of sources.
Initially, I conducted field interviews with 153 managers in 11 large multinational corporations over a two-year period.
These managers typically had authority over alliance decisions.
The interviews were open-ended and unstructured and were conducted with the intention of broadly understanding the factors associated with the decision to enter new alliances.
These interviews provided initial clues on the importance of the network of prior ties in alliance formation, as well as insights into the mechanisms underlying the network factors.
<!-- para 4 -->
I tested the impact of network resources and firm capabilities-based factors using longitudinal data on interfirm strategic alliances in a sample of American, European, and Japanese firms in three different industries from 1981 to 1989.
The choice of these three regions was based on the fact that they account for a majority of industrial activity worldwide and have been described by Kenichi Ohmae and others as the 'triad power,' where most of the world's industrial activity is concentrated.
The three industries were included to cover a disparate group, ranging from developed and mature industries, such as automotive, to an emerging industry, such as new materials.
I collected data on a sample of 166 firms in new materials, industrial automation, and automotive products.
I selected a panel of 50–60 of the largest publicly traded firms within each sector, estimating a firm's size from its sales in that sector as reported in various industry sources.
I also checked with multiple industry experts to ensure that the panels included all prominent competitors in the sectors.
This design led to the inclusion of 62 firms in new materials, 52 firms in automotive products, and 52 firms in industrial automation.
Of these firms, 54 were American, 66 were Japanese, and 46 were European.
<!-- para 5 -->
For each firm, I collected financial data for each year between 1980 and 1989 from Worldscope volumes, which list detailed information about prominent firms in a wide range of sectors.
For firms not reported in Worldscope, data were obtained from COMPUSTAT for U.S. firms, Nikkei for Japanese firms, and Disclosure for European firms.
For a number of Japanese firms, data were obtained from Daewoo Investor's Research Guide as well.
I also collected information for each firm from numerous industry-specific trade journals about the subsegment of its industry within which it had expertise.
To make sure that these classifications were correctly recorded, I cross-checked these with multiple experts from each of the industries.
<!-- para 6 -->
Information on the alliances formed by the three panels of firms was derived from a much larger and more comprehensive data set, which I compiled from various sources.
More than half the data on alliance announcements came from the Cooperative Agreements and Technology Indicators data base, collected by researchers at the University of Maastricht, who examined public announcements in specialized technical journals, books, and business periodicals for various sectors and in the popular business press.
I collected further data on alliance announcements and details on the specific attributes of all alliances from numerous sources, including industry reports, industry-specific articles reporting alliances, and additional materials made available by industry consultants.
I consulted multiple sources when they were available.
For the automotive industry, the sources consulted include Automotive News, Ward's Automotive Reports, US Auto Industry Report, Motor Industry of Japan, and Japanese Auto Manufacturers Forum; for the industrial automation sector, Managing Automation (1988, 1989); for the new materials sector, reports from the Office of Technology Assessment and the Organization for Economic Cooperation and Development; and for all sectors, Predicast's Funk and Scott Index of Corporate Change.
<!-- para 7 -->
The objective was to gather data on all the alliances formed within the selected industries.
Only alliances that had actually been formed were included—reports of probable future alliances were excluded.
The complete alliance data set includes information on 2400 alliances formed by American, European, and Japanese firms.
I took pains to ensure that the data set fully covered all alliances in each industry for each year of the study period.
It is impossible to gauge the extent or consequences of the possible sample selection bias resulting from including only publicly announced ventures.
Since my panel included only large and prominent firms in each industry, which usually get extensive press coverage, I expect this concern to be much less serious than it would have been in a sample of small firms that receive less public scrutiny.
<!-- para 8 -->
One possible concern with the study design is left censoring, because many of the sample firms existed prior to the start of the alliance observation period in 1981.
I therefore collected additional alliance data on the alliance activity of this sample of firms for 11 years, dating back to 1970, and used them to create the initial matrix of prior alliances for 1981.
These data only confirm what previous studies have reported—alliance activity was negligible until 1980, when there was an explosion of alliances (Harrigan, 1985; Hergert and Morris, 1988).
<!-- para 10 -->
This paper examines the factors that affect the likelihood that a firm will enter into an alliance in each period of observation.
The unit of analysis here is the firm-year.
A record was created for each firm in each year of the period covered in this study.
Each firm-year record was given a dichotomous dependent variable, which indicates whether the firm entered any alliances in the given year.
In those instances in which firms entered multiple alliances in a single year, I recorded that it entered into a tie.
New alliances include those formed with any partner and thus include those with prior and with new partners.
Hence, the dependent variable Alliance was coded as '1' if the firm had formed an alliance in that year and '0' otherwise.
<!-- para 11 -->
To compute the network measures, I constructed adjacency matrices representing the relationships between the firms, with separate matrices for each industry for each year.
For each matrix, I included all alliance activity among industry panel members up to the prior year.
Additional data on alliances announced by the panel members between 1970 and 1980 were entered into the initial matrix for 1981 to minimize left-censorship effects.
The network of prior alliances from which firms obtain access to valuable information is dynamic and updated every year with the creation of new alliances.
For each year, the network was cumulative and encompassed all alliances that had been formed from 1970 until the prior year.
The new network each year was lagged and was used to compute network characteristics that predict the likelihood of entry into alliances in the subsequent year.
All matrices were input into UCINET IV, a versatile software package that allows the computation of various network measures (Borgatti, Everett, and Freeman, 1992).
<!-- para 12 -->
I made a number of choices in constructing these matrices that related to the treatment of different types of alliances, the accumulation of multiple ties by the same partners, and the past alliances that should be included.
I tested all these choices against alternatives to ensure the robustness of my findings.
First, I weighted alliances by their strength, as represented by their formal governance structure, using a 7-point scale (Contractor and Lorange, 1988; Nohria and Garcia-Pont, 1991) and checked the results against a simple dichotomous measure.
Second, to take into account the cumulative history of alliances between firms, I used a Guttman scale that captures the score of the strongest alliance formed by the two firms, checked the results against simple additive scores, and normalized additive scores.
Third, I included all previous alliances since 1970 in the construction of the interfirm networks but checked the results against a separate analysis using a moving window with only 5 years of prior alliances.
In this separate formulation, the network of prior alliances included only alliances that had been formed in the previous 5 years.
The length of the window was chosen on the basis of research suggesting that the average life span for alliances is about 5 years (Kogut, 1988).
<!-- para 13 -->
According to Hypothesis 1, the greater a firm's access to network resources from the alliance network, the more likely it is to enter new alliances.
Since the key element of network resources is information, I was interested in computing network measures that captured the extent of information accessible to firms from their position in the network.
There are several approaches to examining the centrality of firms in networks that are frequently used as indicators of the extent of information available to actors (Freeman, 1979).
One approach to assessing the centrality of a firm's location is to determine the number of cliques to which the firm belongs, also called 'clique overlap centrality' (Everett and Borgatti, 1998).
This is distinct from its frequency of alliances.
A firm that belongs to many cliques is one that is located in the midst of dense clusters of firms that have ties with each other.
Given the uncertainties with alliances, access to such redundant information can be an enabling condition for a firm considering alliances.
Membership in multiple cliques suggests embeddedness in dense regions in a network which implies that a firm has many opportunities to pursue alliances.
Such firms may also achieve prominence by linking together disparate groups across the network.
My first measure of centrality, Cliques, captures the number of cliques to which a firm belongs.
This was assessed within UCINET IV using the Bron and Kerbosch (1973) algorithm, which identifies all Luce and Perry (1949) cliques.
Such cliques are formally defined as consisting of actors all of whom are in face-to-face contact.
The minimum clique size I specified was three.
I computed the clique centrality of each firm for each year and expressed the scores relative to the most central firm in the network for that particular year.
<!-- para 14 -->
Another aspect of a firm's centrality is the breadth of its relationships or how widespread its direct and indirect connections are to all possible partners in the network.
This is distinct from its frequency of alliances.
For instance, a firm may have numerous ties, but all of them with a few firms that are not very well connected to other firms, or it may have widely dispersed ties, but with isolated partners who have no other alliances.
In both instances, the firm may have many alliances, but its breadth of ties, in terms of the access they provide to all potential partners in the network, is limited.
In contrast, a firm may have fewer alliances, but those ties are with partner firms that are themselves very well connected to other firms, who in turn may be well connected themselves to others, who are well connected, and so forth.
In this scenario, a firm has few alliances, but its breadth of coverage is higher because of its connection with partners who are themselves very well connected in the network.
Thus, breadth of ties results not only from the frequency of prior alliances but also from the extent of connections of the partners to whom a firm is tied and, in turn, the extent of connection of their partners' partners, and so forth.
Firms with broader networks are not only likely to have more information about potential partners in the network but are themselves more attractive and visible partners to other firms seeking to enter an alliance.
As a result, the breadth of a firm's connections is likely to affect its level of network resources.
<!-- para 15 -->
The second measure of centrality, Closeness, is the closeness centrality score for each firm across all its partners and was constructed within UCINET IV.
This captures how closely connected a firm is to the rest of the firms in the interfirm network, both directly and indirectly.
It is computed by counting the number of firms that a focal firm must go through to reach other firms in the network (Freeman, 1979).
Firms that have a high closeness centrality are likely to have access to more information about all the possible partners in the network than firms with low centrality. [^3] The centrality scores were also normalized to the highest centrality score within each industry and for each year.
<!-- para 16 -->
Several variables that capture the alliance capabilities of firms were introduced to test Hypothesis 2, which specifies the relationship between a firm's alliance formation capabilities and its current alliance behavior.
Experience captures the number of past alliances a firm has formed.
Prior research on alliances has used the number of prior ties between specific pairs of firms as an indicator of trust (e.g., Gulati, 1995a, 1995b), but the focus there was on the history of alliances between two firms.
In this instance, I count all the prior alliances a focal firm has entered with any partners, new or old.
This variable captures the extent of the alliance formation capabilities a firm may have developed from its prior experience. [^4]
<!-- para 17 -->
I also assessed the alliance formation capabilities of focal firms by measuring the diversity of their alliance experiences, based on the assumption that greater diversity can lead to greater capabilities with alliance formation.
Diversity was considered in terms of the kinds of governance structure the firm used to formalize its prior alliances and the nationalities of its partners.
I assessed diversity with two sets of measures.
First, I computed the reciprocal of the Herfindahl index for the nationalities of each firm's prior alliance partners and for the governance structure of its prior alliances.
I also computed alternative variables for these two measures, using a specification akin to Blau's index of heterogeneity (Blau, 1977).
I computed the proportion of firm i's ties of type j until year t, out of the total number of ties the firm had entered until that year, denoted as  $P_{it,j}$.
I computed the index of diversity  $Y_{it}$ by subtracting the summation over all j of the square of  $P_{it,j}$.
<!-- para 18 -->
$$ Y_{it}=1-\sum_{j=1}^{J}\left(P_{it,j}\right)^{2} $$
<!-- para 19 -->
I introduced one additional variable to assess possible temporal dimensions associated with alliance formation capabilities within firms.
Presumably, a recent experience with entering an alliance would make alliance formation capabilities more salient than an experience in the more distant past.
This could also be interpreted as the extent to which firms are likely to engage in activities similar to those done in the recent past (Amburgey et al., 1993).
Thus, I examined if the duration of time since a firm last entered an alliance altered its current likelihood of entering a new alliance.
I introduced a variable measuring the time duration since the firm had previously entered an alliance.
Both linear and quadratic terms for this variable were introduced.
<!-- para 21 -->
While the primary focus of this study is the role of a firm's network resources in influencing strategic behavior, there may also be important material resource considerations that could affect a firm's alliance formation.
Financial economists have long argued that a firm's financial profile is a reliable indicator of its economic imperatives, and a firm's expected behavior can be deduced from such measures.
The study of the formation of strategic alliances has in fact focused on such material resource considerations and sought to identify some of the firm-specific imperatives likely to lead firms to enter alliances (for a review, see Harrigan and Newman, 1990).
This has led to a rich research stream that has examined which types of firms in which industries enter into what types of alliances for what reasons (e.g., Mariti and Smiley, 1983; Ghemawat, Porter, and Rawlinson, 1986; Hagedoorn, 1993).
<!-- para 22 -->
At the firm level, scholars have sought to show the role of resource contingencies such as strategic vulnerability and incumbency on the proclivity of firms to enter alliances (Eisenhardt and Schoonhoven, 1996; Mitchell and Singh, 1992).
Other scholars have looked at firms' attributes, such as size, age, competitive position, product diversity, and financial resources, as important predictors of their propensity to enter strategic alliances with each other (e.g., Shan, 1990; Barley, Freeman, and Hybels, 1992; Powell and Brantley, 1992; Burgers, Hill, and Kim, 1993; Shan, Walker, and Kogut, 1994).
Evidence on the effects of some of these factors on alliance formation is mixed and in many instances conflicting.
For instance, several studies have looked at the incidence of alliances and the size of firms entering them.
The heightened proclivity of larger firms to enter alliances has led some to conclude that the quest for market power may be an important consideration in such ties (Pate, 1969; Berg and Friedman, 1978).
There is, however, mixed evidence for the effect of size.
While some researchers have observed a negative relationship in certain sectors (Shan, 1987), others have found a positive relationship (Powell and Brantley, 1992).
Still other researchers suggest that the relationship may actually be nonlinear (Kogut, Shan, and Walker, 1992; Burgers et al., 1993).
<!-- para 23 -->
Given the conflicting claims about the role of material resource considerations for alliances, I did not put forth specific hypotheses for them.
Following prior research, however, I included several commonly considered material-resource-based considerations for alliances postulated in prior research and more systemic factors resulting from overall propensities in specific industries.
My first control variable accounted for the role of financial liquidity in the alliance activity of firms.
In examining a firm's financial liquidity, it is important to distinguish solvency from long-term debt.
The former reflects the firm's ability to meet its immediate financial obligations and is indicative of its access to resources in the short run.
Firms unable to meet such commitments can be vulnerable to bankruptcy proceedings.
A firm's long-term debt reflects financial obligations that come due in the long run.
Since firms have a choice of raising new capital through issuing new debt or equity, the amount of long-term debt is also indicative of the capital structure of the firm.
<!-- para 24 -->
I used ‘quick ratio,’ which is defined as current assets minus inventory, divided by current liabilities, as a measure of firm solvency, called Solvency (Dooley, 1969).
This was normalized to the median quick ratio within the firm’s industry because the sample was pooled across three industries.
I measured leverage as the total amount of long-term debt divided by the firm’s current assets.
This, in turn, was normalized to the median debt/asset ratio within the firm’s industry to compute the variable Debt.
<!-- para 25 -->
An important factor generally used to predict firm behavior is overall performance.
There is no prior evidence as to whether the firms entering alliances are high performers or low performers in the industry.
For my purposes, I relied on return on assets normalized to the industry median as a measure of performance (Performance).
<!-- para 26 -->
I controlled for the size of the firms with a Size variable, which is the total assets of the firm normalized to the industry median, to capture any effects of a firm's size on its alliance activities.
<!-- para 27 -->
I included a control variable that assesses whether the likelihood that a firm will form an alliance is related to the density of cumulative alliances in each industry up to the prior year.
I computed the network density in the industry in the prior year, which is the cumulative number of alliance combinations formed in the industry divided by the number of dyadic combinations that are possible.
In a limited way, this tests institutional claims that firms may mimic other firms to reduce uncertainty and captures the net effect of the various macroeconomic, sector-specific factors that may be influencing the formation of alliances (Amburgey and Miner, 1992).
For each firm-year record, I created a variable Density, which is the cumulative number of alliances announced in the industry until the previous year divided by the entire feasible set of partnerships.
<!-- para 28 -->
I included a series of dummy variables for each year to capture any effects of temporal trends related to current technological and environmental conditions that may have influenced the creation of alliances by firms.
For simplicity of presentation I then reestimated these effects using a single variable, Time, which ranges from 0 to 8, indicating each year.
Both linear and quadratic terms for this variable were tested.
<!-- para 29 -->
I controlled for the nationality of firms by including dummy variables.
Despite the rapid globalization of economies, most firms still operate within their national contexts, and there may be systematic differences among firms of different nationalities in the proclivity to enter alliances (Shan and Hamilton, 1991).
I included two dummy variables, DUSA and DJPN, for the country of origin of the firm.
The former indicates an American firm, the latter a Japanese firm, while the default category is European firms. [^5]
<!-- para 30 -->
I controlled for sectoral differences by including two dummy variables: New Materials and Industrial Automation.
The former indicates the New Materials sector, and the latter, Industrial Automation, while the default sector is Automotive.

<!-- para 2 -->
I modeled alliance formation using the following dynamic panel model, in which a variable's positive coefficients indicate that it promotes alliance formation:
<!-- para 3 -->
$$ p_{i}(t)=F\left(a+bx_{i}+cy_{i}\left(t-1\right)+u_{i}\right) $$
<!-- para 4 -->
where  $p_i(t) = \text{the probability at time } (t)$ of the announcement of an alliance by firm  $i$;  $x_i = \text{a time-constant vector of covariates characterizing firm } i$;  $y_i(t-1) = \text{a time-varying vector of covariates characterizing firm } i$;  $u_i = \text{unobserved time-constant effects not captured by the independent variables}$; and  $F = \text{the normal cumulative distribution function}$.
<!-- para 5 -->
An issue that arises when analyzing data on a time series of cross-sections, or panel data, is the possibility of unobserved time-invariant effects known as ‘unobserved heterogeneity.’ This is of particular concern for this study with respect to the claim that a firm’s prior history of alliances, which is an indicator of firm capability with alliance formation, affects its future likelihood of entering an alliance.
There are two distinct explanations for this empirical regularity, if it indeed occurs (Heckman, 1981).
One explanation is that a genuine behavioral effect exists whereby, because of the prior alliances it has experienced, a firm’s preferences are altered in the future.
In econometric terms, such a behavioral effect is called ‘state dependence’—the likelihood of an event is a function of the state of the unit.
<!-- para 6 -->
Japanese firms in their propensity to form alliances.
I also conducted separate subgroup analyses for firms of each nationality separately.

<!-- para 9 -->
If state dependence alone encapsulated the empirical reality, there would be no problem, but there is another possibility that, if not accounted for, could lead to spurious results: firms may differ in their propensity to enter alliances because of unobserved factors.
In this instance, such unobservable effects could result from permanent differences between firms in their preferences for alliances that are not captured by any of the independent variables included.
If this noise were systematic for the same unit over time, it could lead to a serial correlation among the error terms for those observations, which would yield consistent but inefficient coefficients, rendering any statistical testing inaccurate.
Furthermore, prior alliance experience may appear to be a determinant of future alliance formation solely because it is a proxy for temporally persistent unobservable factors that determine alliance formation and nonformation.
Improper treatment can lead to spurious effects appearing with attempts to assess the influence of past experience on current decisions; this phenomenon is termed 'spurious state dependence' (Heckman, 1981; Heckman and Borjas, 1980; Hsiao, 1986; Black, Moffitt, and Warner, 1990).
<!-- para 10 -->
In a statistical sense, the problem of unobserved heterogeneity relates to model specification (Peterson and Koput, 1991).
If a model is completely specified, no such problem occurs, but most statistical models suffer from some degree of omitted-variable bias.
Another way to think about this problem is to consider the appropriate risk set.
In the current design, I include all firms within each industry for each year as the set of firms at risk of entering an alliance.
It is quite likely that some of these firms are in fact not at risk of entering an alliance in some or even all observation periods, while other firms have a high propensity to ally.
This suggests the possibility of misspecification of the risk set unless adequate allowances are made for such unobserved differences in propensity.
One way to deal with such a bias is to clean up the risk set by eliminating records unlikely to experience the event, a process analogous to removing men from pregnancy studies.
The difference in propensity is frequently a result of unobservable factors, however, making it impossible a priori to weed out records from the sample on reasonable grounds without biasing the sample.
<!-- para 11 -->
Two approaches frequently used to address problems of unobserved heterogeneity statistically are fixed- and random-effects models.
Fixed-effects models treat the unobserved individual effect as a constant over time and compute it for each unit (firm).
In other words, the method entails estimating a constant term for each distinct unit and including dummy variables for each and is similar to least-squares-with-dummy-variables (LSDV) regression models (Hannan and Young, 1977).
Random-effects models treat the heterogeneity that varies across units as randomly drawn from some underlying probability distribution.
<!-- para 12 -->
To address concerns of heterogeneity, I employed a random-effects panel probit model, developed by Butler and Moffitt (1982), for the statistical analysis. [^6] I later tested the robustness of my findings with a fixed-effects model and found consistent results.
My decision to employ a random-effects model was based on the following: First, estimates computed using fixed-effects models can be biased for panels over short periods (Chintagunta, Jain, and Vilcassim, 1991; Heckman, 1981; Hsiao, 1986).
This is not a problem with random-effects models.
Because all the firm-year records in my sample were present for only 9 years, random effects was clearly the favored approach.
Second, fixed-effects models cannot include time-independent covariates, a limitation that would have meant excluding several control variables, and an analysis without some of these variables would have been severely limited.
<!-- para 13 -->
The random-effects models were computed using LIMDEP 6.0.
The random effects approach used generates a coefficient Rho, which indicates the degree of overdispersion of the variance.
Specifically, Rho is the proportion of the variance of the error term that is accounted for by the unobservable firm-specific variables.
The significance of Rho suggests that observationally identical firms display different alliance propensities because of permanent differences in their alliance preferences and other unobserved factors.
<!-- para 16 -->
As noted above, the panel probit model used for this analysis corrects for any biases resulting from intrinsic randomness or omitted variables and captures any unobserved factors that generate differences among observationally identical firms (i.e., similar on all included independent variables) in their propensity to form alliances.
This not only limits the omitted-variable bias but also limits spurious findings on the effect of firm experience on alliance formation.

## discussion
<!-- para 2 -->
The results of the longitudinal analysis confirm that, over time, the proclivity of firms to enter new alliances is influenced by the amount of network resources available to them.
Results show that network resources, as indicated by firms' location in the interfirm network of prior alliances in which they are embedded (and also the positions of their partners), were a significant predictor of the frequency with which firms entered new alliances.
There is also some evidence that the extent of capabilities firms accumulated with forming alliances also positively affected the frequency with which they entered new alliances.
While the measure for firms' capabilities, using their past experience as an indicator, was a significant predictor of new alliance activity by firms in all models, some of the other measures for such capabilities were not significant.
I found no effect for the duration since a firm had last done an alliance, suggesting that there may be limited or no depletion of alliance capabilities over time.
Neither of the two measures of the diversity of prior alliance experience of firms (in terms of governance structures used and nationalities of partners) was significant.
This suggests that perhaps capabilities in managing a diverse set of alliances and partners are not as important for firms as the experience of all such partnerships.
This could result from the fact that once firms have developed the administrative control procedures for creating new alliances, they are able to put that knowledge to use in any kind of alliance.
There may also be additional facets of diversity of experience that could be taken into account such as diversity of objectives and scope of activities in alliances that were not part of this study.
<!-- para 3 -->
To ensure the robustness of the findings, I included numerous control variables postulated in prior research as important material-resource considerations for firms entering alliances.
Surprisingly, many material-resource attributes of firms, such as liquidity, solvency, and performance, were not significant predictors of alliance activity by firms.
Given the longitudinal context of this study and the time-varying nature of most of the variables included, I also ensured that I adequately accounted for any broad temporal trends that could influence the findings.
I tested for such temporal trends both with dummy variables for each year and a single linear term for time.
The insignificance of the time variable, which usually captures residual factors, in the models in which network resource factors were included is indicative of the explanatory power of these variables.
<!-- para 4 -->
The results for the regional origin of firms reveal some interesting trends.
Contrary to my expectations, there were no significant differences in the propensity of American and Japanese firms to enter alliances in comparison with European firms.
In separate analyses, I found no significant differences across American and Japanese firms either.
Since these variables are insignificant even in the baseline model, I believe that this indicates that many of the expected national differences are explained by some of the controls I have included.
This was confirmed in separate estimation, in which I found that the dummy variables for nationality were significant when introduced on their own but failed to retain significance once the remaining controls were introduced.
I also ensured the robustness of my findings by conducting subgroup analyses in which I looked at firms from each region separately.
Results were consistent across all three subgroups, suggesting that network resources and firm capabilities are influential across national contexts.
<!-- para 5 -->
I also explored sectoral differences in greater depth to ensure that similar dynamics occurred across sectors.
I conducted separate subgroup analyses in which I ran models separately for each industry and found very similar results across the three industries.
This indicates the robustness of findings on the role of network resources in alliance formation.
An assessment of the dummy variables for sector with the pooled sample shows some interesting trends.
The negative and significant effect in the baseline model suggests that firms in both new materials and industrial automation sectors were less likely to form alliances than those in the default sector of automotive.
These effects were no longer significant once I included measures for network resources.
This indicates that important differences in inherent propensities for alliances across sectors are explained by systematic differences in network resources available to firms across those sectors.
There may be important institutional considerations underlying both sectoral and national differences in alliance propensities that have not been fully explored here but could be examined in future research.
<!-- para 6 -->
This study addresses several concerns with prior research on strategic alliances.
First, the primary focus of research on alliances has been to ask the 'why' question, which focuses on understanding some of the reasons that firms enter alliances.
Part of the problem with an orientation toward 'why' questions is not only that they are syntactically inclined to teleological or functional answers (Granovetter, 1994), but, more important, they lead to a neglect of some of the conditions under which certain behaviors are likely (Oliver, 1990).
Thus, in recent years, more attention has been paid to elaborating some of the firm- and industry-level imperatives that have led to the proliferation of alliances and less attention to some of the conditions under which the creation of alliances by specific firms becomes more or less likely.
A related concern is that many prior studies have considered the unit of analysis to be alliances, as opposed to firm participation in alliances.
They have typically sampled on alliances themselves, rendering it difficult to undertake a firm-level analysis.
Also, much of the empirical research on alliances has been cross-sectional or pooled cross-sectional in scope.
Even recent efforts to introduce a network perspective to the formation of alliances have taken a pooled cross-sectional approach and thus have not been able to examine systematically how the evolving financial, economic, and social circumstances of a firm may moderate its alliance behavior over time (e.g., Kogut, Shan, and Walker, 1992; Eisenhardt and Schoonhoven, 1996).
<!-- para 7 -->
Finally, the focus on firm and industry imperatives and the description of external context in competitive terms has typically assumed an atomistic notion of firms evaluating alternative courses of action and does not take into account the actions of other firms or the relationships in which they themselves are already embedded.
Moreover, it ignores the interactive elements of the market, whereby participants discover market information through their interactions in the market (Hayek, 1949; White, 1981).
This study is distinctive in that it tackles some of the above concerns by assessing the alliance behavior of firms over a period of time.
The research design allowed me to examine the conditions under which the formation of alliances by specific firms becomes more or less likely.
<!-- para 8 -->
Using a network perspective to study strategic alliances can also provide valuable insights for the broader domain of interfirm networks.
The creation of an alliance is an important strategic action, yet the cumulation of such alliances also constitutes a network.
Thus, alliances are distinct in that they can be studied as both endogenous and exogenous factors.
The former can be examined by looking at the influence of interfirm networks on the formation of alliances, while the latter can be assessed by considering the effects of the interfirm network of cumulated alliances.
In this study, both were examined simultaneously by assessing the influence of the interfirm network of prior alliances on future alliances in a longitudinal study.
Studying the development of an alliance network over time provides unique insights into the evolution of networks, where strategic action and social structure are closely intertwined (Gulati and Gargiulo, 1999).
It also allows us to examine the extent to which firms' alliances may lock them into path-dependent courses of action in the future.
Given our limited understanding of the dynamics of networks, alliances provide a unique arena in which action and structure are closely interconnected and the dynamic coevolution of networks can be examined.
This setting also allowed us to assess whether there may be diminishing returns from network resources.
While scholars have suggested that the benefits of embeddedness may also turn into disadvantages when the actor is 'overembedded' in a network of stable, thick relations (e.g., Portes and Sensenbrenner, 1993), I found no such effects for alliance formation.
Furthermore, the study of interfirm networks is now a burgeoning field of inquiry in and of itself, and strategic alliances have become an important set of ties in which firms have become engaged.
They thus merit further examination (Gulati, 1998).
<!-- para 9 -->
The network resources firms receive from their interfirm networks can also be linked with their performance.
One approach to assessing the influence on performance of network resources has been to examine the relationship between the extent to which firms are embedded in alliances and the likelihood of their survival (e.g., Singh, Tucker, and House, 1986; Baum and Oliver, 1991, 1992; Mitchell and Singh, 1996).
Scholars have also linked network characteristics with the growth of firms (Powell et al., 1996) and their innovative output (Shan et al., 1994; Hagedoorn and Schakenraad, 1994; Ahuja, 1996), and have considered some of the specific strategic factors that may mediate the relationship between network factors and firm performance (Madhavan, 1996).
Prior studies have also examined each individual alliance and explored the extent to which social structural factors influence the outcomes of individual relationships (Dyer, 1996; Gulati and Lawrence, 1998).
Recent evidence even suggests that firms with greater experience with alliances may have enhanced capabilities in generating value from such partnerships (Anand and Khanna, 1997).
<!-- para 10 -->
Thus far, studies of the implications of network resources for firm behavior and firm performance have proceeded separately, yet they are interconnected and merit consideration together.
For instance, the network resources resulting from the network of prior alliances not only provides powerful enabling conditions for firms to enter new alliances, it can also have consequences for the relative success of individual alliances the firm enters.
This, in turn, can have long-term performance consequences for the firm itself.
Considering behavior and performance simultaneously is an important and fruitful direction for future research.
<!-- para 11 -->
This study expands the realm of resource considerations under the resource-based perspective from resources residing securely within a firm's boundaries to network resources that are available in its external context and result from network membership and location.
The emergence of network resources as a result of the historical industry alliance network that in turn influences future alliances suggests a path-dependent process by which firms accumulate network resources that are sticky and can become the basis of sustainable competitive advantage (Barney, 1991).
Many prior scholars have suggested the importance of unique historical conditions and path-dependent processes as a significant basis for firm resources (David, 1985; Arthur, 1989).
The notion of network resources adds specificity to this understanding and suggests an important means by which history can matter.
<!-- para 12 -->
The notion of network resources can be further extended to incorporate the multiplicity of networks beyond the network of prior alliances in which firms are placed, such as those resulting from ties with banks, universities, and board interlocks.
Such an expansion of the domain of interfirm networks that provide network resources can enable a broader understanding of the multiple avenues by which firms can obtain such resources from their social context.
Furthermore, since firms are embedded in many interfirm networks, including some that can provide access to financial and technological capital, a useful extension of this study could also consider the implications of participation in multiple networks on firms' subsequent behavior and performance.
Some possible networks include board interlocks, investment bank ties, professional association memberships, and participation in government-sponsored R&D consortia.
It would also be fruitful to consider the contents of those ties and make qualitative distinctions between ties based upon the kinds of information flowing through them (Westphal, Gulati, and Shortell, 1997; Gulati and Westphal, 1999).
Finally, the impact of network resources on a firm's activities can usefully be extended to examine their influence for broader sets of strategic activities beyond entry into alliances.
<!-- para 13 -->
Incorporating network factors into our account of the alliance behavior of firms not only provides us with a more accurate representation of the key influences on the strategic actions of firms but has important implications for managerial practice as well, many of which have yet to be explored.
The focus on the implications of network resources for enabling a specific strategic action by firms could easily be extended to consider the role of network resources for firm performance.
Network resources are usually heterogeneously disbursed within an industry and due to their unique historical basis can be difficult to imitate, making them a viable avenue for sustainable competitive advantage.
This study also suggests that the organizational search for alliances may result in a path-dependent process in which the gradual production of a network structure increases the information available to select firms, albeit also limiting the effective range of potential partners a firm is likely to consider (Arthur, 1989).
Firms may thus become victims of their own history.
Only firms with a rich prior history that endows them with network resources and rich capabilities for forming alliances are likely to consider entering new alliances, and some firms may get stuck in the vicious cycle of never being able to get themselves to enter an alliance.
<!-- para 14 -->
Managers could choose to anticipate such concerns about their participation in networks and proactively initiate selective network contacts that enhance their informational capabilities.
They can be proactive in designing their networks and in considering the ramifications on their future choices of each new tie they form, since network resources result from the location of firms in the network.
The informational advantages resulting from ties to central firms must be factored into the calculus for partner choice.
This choice must be influenced not only by the appeal of a potential partner for the project at hand but also by potential partners' location in the network.
Especially if a firm is considering embarking on a major strategic initiative driven by alliances, it should consider building up network resources by seeking out strategic alliances with central firms that enable the further development of new alliances.
Thus, once managers understand the dynamics of alliance networks, they may choose path-creation strategies rather than becoming path-dependent.
As a result, they can take a more forward-looking stance and visualize the desired network structure of alliances in the future and work backward to define their current alliance strategy.
