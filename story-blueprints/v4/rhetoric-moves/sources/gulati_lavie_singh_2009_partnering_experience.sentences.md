# gulati_lavie_singh_2009_partnering_experience 句子库存

## introduction
<!-- para 2 -->
In the past two decades there has been tremendous growth in the formation of interfirm alliances (Gulati, 2007).
Despite this growth, studies report persistently low success rates for alliances and suggest that many firms fail to realize the potential gains from partnering activity (Gulati, Sytch, and Mehrotra, 2008; Harrigan, 1985; Kale, Dyer, and Singh, 2002; Kalmbach and Roussel, 1999; Kogut, 1989).
In particular, studies that measure value creation in alliances by investigating stock market responses to alliance announcements suggest that only half of the firms participating in alliances obtain positive market returns from their partnering activity (Chan et al., 1997; Crutchley, Enyang, and Hensen, 1991).
Moreover, such gains are only marginally significant and quite low on average (Chang and Chen, 2002; Das, Sen, and Sengupta, 1998; Koh and Venkatraman, 1991; Lee and Wyatt, 1990; Reuer and Koza, 2000; Woolridge and Snow, 1990).
In view of these findings, some scholars have begun to explore the conditions under which firms can create value in alliances (Merchant and Schendel, 2000). [^1]
<!-- para 3 -->
Prior research has identified the accumulated partnering experience of a firm as an important predictor of value creation in its new alliances (Anand and Khanna, 2000; Kale et al., 2002; Kale and Singh, 2007), arguing that such experience contributes to a firm's relational capability and eventually to its gains from partnering activities (Dyer and Singh, 1998).
While this argument has been widely accepted in strategic alliances research, studies that empirically tested this proposition have revealed mixed or limited support (e.g., Anand and Khanna, 2000; Merchant and Schendel, 2000; Park and Kim, 1997).
These mixed findings raise several questions: Are there distinctive types of experience that differentially impact the gains from alliances?
Are there certain conditions under which a firm can gain from its partnering experience and exploit it in its new alliances?
Which firms extract the outmost benefits from partnering experience?
What types of partners are most desirable for increasing the gains from partnering experience?
<!-- para 4 -->
In addressing these questions concerning the potentially contingent value of partnering experience, we extend prior research on the contribution of partnering experience to value creation in firms' newly formed alliances, thus advancing alliance research and organizational learning perspectives.
Specifically, our framework extends recent research that has distinguished between a firm's general partnering experience and its partner-specific experience by theorizing on the relative contributions of these types of experience.
We question the premise that partner-specific experience offers limited value (Goerzen, 2007; Hoang and Rothaermel, 2005) by furnishing evidence on some of its positive implications.
We further advance alliance research by uncovering boundary conditions under which a firm can gain from its partner-specific experience, suggesting that firm-, partner-, and relation-specific factors affect the gains that a firm can extract from its partnering experience in subsequent alliances.
Hence, our study elaborates on the different facets of partnering experience and reconciles some of the apparent contradictions in earlier empirical studies.
It also contributes to the organizational learning literature (Argote and Ophir, 2002; Cohen and Levinthal, 1990; Levitt and March, 1988) by identifying knowledge application as a key element of the learning chain, and elucidating the effects of partnering experience on firms' learning processes.
<!-- para 5 -->
We proceed by analyzing the distinctive facets of general partnering experience and partner-specific experience.
The former refers to the accumulated experience a firm has gained from any prior interfirm alliance, which, in turn, may enhance its relational capability.
The latter encompasses the specific experience a firm has accumulated through recurrent alliances with the same partner, which may also facilitate interfirm trust and collaboration.
We suggest that a firm's gains from general partnering experience are limited because some experience benefits are not completely fungible across all alliances and entail relation-specific factors that emerge only in recurrent alliances with specific partners.
We further claim that a firm's partnering experience sets an insufficient condition for value creation in the firm's new alliances, and thus consider how the contribution of partner-specific experience is contingent on the accessibility of learning opportunities in new alliances, the munificence of operational resources, and the level of uncertainty surrounding firm performance.
To demonstrate partnering experience effects, we adopt the alliance formation event as the unit of analysis, and following a widely used approach, we measure abnormal stock market returns around the time of alliance announcement as an indication of the value created in the alliance (e.g., Koh and Venkatraman, 1991; Anand and Khanna, 2000; Kale et al., 2002; Gulati and Wang, 2003).
We test our hypotheses with a comprehensive sample of joint ventures formed among Fortune 300 firms between 1987 and 1996.

## theory
<!-- para 3 -->
Research on value creation in alliances suggests that firms' experience with alliances can greatly influence the value created in their subsequent alliances (e.g., Anand and Khanna, 2000).
In particular, the relational view (Dyer and Singh, 1998) relates value creation to the presence of relational mechanisms such as relation-specific investments, knowledge sharing, complementary partner resources, and informal safeguards.
The abilities governance studies (McConnell and Nantell, 1985), and has been employed extensively in the study of alliance benefits.

The availability of such gains underscores the importance of partnering experience because developing relational capabilities entails learning from prior alliances (Anand and Khanna, 2000).
<!-- para 5 -->
In this study, we focus on partnering experience as a factor that may influence a firm's gains from alliances.
Without clearly distinguishing between different facets of experience, prior research has generally noted that firms with greater partnering experience develop relational capabilities that enhance their abilities to extract value from subsequent alliances.
This value-creation effect results in differential reaction of the stock market to announcements of future alliances by those firms (Anand and Khanna, 2000).
Some of these experience benefits accrue jointly to dyads of partners due to their shared history of prior alliances, whereas other benefits separately accrue to firms due to their individually accumulated experience.
Nevertheless, empirical studies have found limited support for the proposed contributions of partnering experience to value creation in alliances.
<!-- para 6 -->
At the dyadic level of the alliance, prior research suggested that the joint partnering experience of two firms may attenuate opportunistic behavior in subsequent alliances between them by guiding partner selection decisions and by fostering mutual trust, reciprocity, informal obligation, and loyalty (Gulati, 1995; Gulati and Wang, 2003; Kogut, 1989; Park and Kim, 1997).
Consequently, partners may be able to commit critical resources (Dyer and Singh, 1998; Zajac and Olsen, 1993) and economize on the governance costs in such recurrent alliances (Gulati and Singh, 1998; Williamson, 1985).
Experience accumulated during recurrent alliances with the same partners also contributes to the capacity of partners to resolve arising conflicts (Kale et al., 2000; Simonin, 1997), reduces uncertainty, and enhances the predictability of alliance outcomes.
Hence, firms are expected to benefit from their mutual partnering experience when establishing recurrent alliances.
Unfortunately, these assertions have not received much support in prior empirical research.
For instance, Park and Kim (1997) reported marginally negative abnormal returns to firms that participated in joint ventures with partners with whom they had prior relationships.
More recently, Hoang and Rothaermel (2005) revealed that recurrent alliances between pharmaceutical and biotechnology firms have not increased the likelihood of success in their joint drug development projects.
Similarly, Goerzen (2007) found a negative association between the economic performance of Japanese multinationals and their repeated equity-based partnerships.
<!-- para 7 -->
At the individual firm level, some scholars suggested that a firm's experience with various prior partners contributes to its relational capabilities (Dyer and Singh, 1998; Gulati, 1999, 2007; Kale et al., 2002; Lorenzoni and Lipparini, 1999).
In particular, such experience contributes to the development of alliance management routines that assist in partner selection, the choice of appropriate governance mechanisms, and the effective management of alliances.
These relational capabilities, in turn, enhance value creation in subsequent alliances (Anand and Khanna, 2000).
Nevertheless, these ideas have received limited support in prior empirical research.
For example, in a comprehensive study of the sources of value creation in joint ventures, Merchant and Schendel (2000) found that partnering experience, indicated by the overall number of prior joint ventures formed by a focal firm, produced a negative but insignificant effect on a firm's abnormal market returns.
Anand and Khanna (2000) reported similar results, but demonstrated that this effect becomes positive and significant when controlling for firm-specific factors.
This positive effect persisted, however, only in research and development (R&D) joint ventures, but not in production or marketing joint ventures.
Finally, Hoang and Rothaermel (2005) found that in drug development projects, this experience effect is marginally diminishing and benefits biotechnology firms, but not their pharmaceutical partners.
<!-- para 8 -->
Prior research has offered several explanations for the limited support for the impact of partnering experience on positive market gains.
Some studies postulate that proxies for partnering experience typically suffer from measurement limitations (e.g., Merchant and Schendel, 2000).
In particular, the number of prior alliances does not necessarily capture the actual amount of experience accumulated by the partners, which could depend on the duration of the relationship, the organizational and cultural fit between the partners, and their respective learning capacities.
Furthermore, repeated alliances between the same partners may increase the risk of opportunistic behavior and asymmetric appropriation of gains, as the partners become familiar with the firm's proprietary assets (Balakrishnan and Koza, 1993; Park and Kim, 1997).
Moreover, gains from partnering experience may depend on the type of alliance, with positive gains available in R&D joint ventures that generate greater interaction and learning opportunities than marketing joint ventures (Anand and Khanna, 2000; Merchant and Schendel, 2000).
Finally, experience benefits may be mitigated by unproductive learning that leads to organizational inertia and limits the firm's capacity to explore new opportunities (Goerzen, 2007, Lavie and Rosenkopf, 2006).
These incongruent findings and varying explanations merit further investigation.
We seek to enhance understanding of the differential market gains ascribed to general partnering experience and partner-specific experience as well as identify factors that set boundary conditions to their effects.
<!-- para 10 -->
We unpack the benefits of partnering experience by distinguishing between general partnering experience (GPE) and partner-specific experience (PSE) (Hoang and Rothaermel, 2005).
GPE refers to the accumulated experience that a firm gains from any interfirm alliance that it has formed in the past. [^2] In contrast, PSE refers to the specific experience that a firm has accumulated through recurrent alliances with the same partner.
Such experience is dyadic rather than firm-specific.
Whereas GPE serves as the foundation for a firm's relational capability, PSE assists in building trust and facilitating coordination with certain partners.
<!-- para 11 -->
Most prior studies that have examined the effects of partnering experience on a firm's gains from alliances have focused on the firm's experience with all alliances formed in the past, that is, GPE (e.g., Anand and Khanna, 2000; Merchant and Schendel, 2000).
In the few studies that considered the experience accumulated through recurrent alliances with the same partners (e.g., Goerzen, 2007; Park and Kim, 1997), the distinction between these different types of experience has not been the focus of attention.
Still other studies that have acknowledged this distinction have focused on explaining changes in alliance governance or project-level outcomes rather than on variance in market returns (Reuer, Zollo, and Singh, 2002; Hoang and Rothaermel, 2005).
<!-- para 12 -->
We relate the distinctive benefits of these two types of partnering experience to some of the ways in which firms learn from alliances.
Learning generally involves creation, retention, and transfer of knowledge.
These processes are essential for value creation, but since they often entail interactions of people, tasks, and tools, they pose difficulties when occurring in an interfirm context (Argote and Ingram, 2000). [^3] Partnering experience enables firms to overcome some of these impediments by improving their alliance management skills, nurturing routines that guide the choice of an appropriate type of alliance agreement, establishing knowledge-sharing practices that enhance the efficiency of collaboration, and making firms aware of potential partnering hazards and their associated safeguards.
We next propose that while all these benefits accrue for both PSE and GPE, the benefits of PSE are greater because: (a) the process of learning from PSE is more efficient than the learning from GPE, that is, it entails lower transaction costs in the course of knowledge accumulation, and (b) PSE offers richer benefits than those available through GPE.
<!-- para 13 -->
First, learning from PSE is more efficient than learning from GPE because of the specificity of the learning context in the case of PSE.
Prior research has suggested that some of the benefits arising from reproduction of past experience depend on the organizational context in which such reproduction occurs (Argote and Ingram, 2000; Lane and Lubatkin, 1998).
Thus, learning from prior experience in specific modes of alliances (e.g., joint ventures, R&D consortia, etc.) may be inefficient or inapplicable in the context of alliances.
<!-- para 14 -->
For instance, Anand and Khanna (2000) distinguished learning benefits accumulated within joint ventures from those accruing within non-equity alliances, finding further nuances across different types of joint ventures.
Similarly, Lane and Lubatkin (1998) described relative absorptive capacity as a specialized mechanism that depends on specific attributes of alliances such as their governance mode and the relatedness of partners' knowledge.
Along the same lines, Lavie and Miller (2008) revealed that experience with foreign partners is more valuable than experience with domestic partners when firms seek to gain from internationalization of their alliance portfolios.
Extending these ideas, we suggest that learning is specific also to individual partners.
Thus, compared with GPE, which is not necessarily accumulated in a specific context, PSE evolves in recurrent alliances with the same partners and thus provides firms with a more consistent learning context.
In such context, experience is accumulated more efficiently, that is, it entails lower learning costs and exhibits faster learning than GPE.
In turn, this enhances the potential contribution of PSE relative to GPE.
<!-- para 15 -->
A second advantage of PSE over GPE arises from the range of partnering benefits that accrue from partnering experience.
Many benefits of partnering experience are inherently partner-specific in the sense that they cannot be applied in alliances with other partners.
Such benefits are associated with relational mechanisms that emerge in a particular relationship between two given partners and may involve their asset-specific investments. [^4] Hence, these benefits are available only in subsequent alliances with the same partners.
For example, Toyota's suppliers formed an economically efficient alliance network but could not apply the value-added logistics procedures learned in Toyota's facilities in their transactions with North American automobile manufacturers, because the latter implemented different facility design and operating procedures (Dyer, 2000).
By way of generalization, the relational mechanisms that emerge in recurrent alliance with specific partners generate a broader range of PSE benefits relative to the available GPE benefits.
These mechanisms typically involve partners' investments in relation-specific assets and knowledge-sharing routines as well as reliance on complementary resources and trust-based governance (Dyer and Singh, 1998).
The emergence of such relational mechanisms depends on the interaction with particular partners (Gulati and Sytch, 2007, 2008; Zaheer, McEvily, and Perrone, 1998), and thus is tied to the history of prior relationships with specific partners.
Consequently, partnering experience benefits are less fungible across alliances with other partners.
In particular, interfirm trust with a given partner does not imply trust of another partner; knowledge-sharing routines that economize on transaction costs with a certain partner may not be applicable in relationships with other partners; and mutually agreed upon conflict resolution mechanisms that are applied in recurrent alliances with the same partner are not necessarily applicable in other alliances.
Therefore, many experience benefits are available only in recurrent alliances with the same partners (i.e., PSE), whereas GPE does not fully leverage relational mechanisms and tends to be homogenous across firms.
<!-- para 16 -->
In sum, PSE is likely to induce positive market reaction to announcements of recurrent alliances between the same partners.
In addition, due to the broader range of benefits and the efficient learning process associated with its accumulation, the impact of PSE is predicted to exceed that of GPE.

Thus far, we have considered the direct effect of PSE on value creation in alliances.
It is possible, however, that PSE effects may be contingent and vary systematically with partner- and firm-specific factors.
Next, we consider such moderating effects.
First, we argue that the potential PSE benefits are
<!-- para 20 -->
likely to depend on the extent to which novel learning opportunities are made available by distinctive partners.
We then propose that PSE benefits may be contingent on the focal firm's own operational resources that support the application of accumulated PSE.
Finally, we examine how the market's perception of firm-specific uncertainty shapes the value of PSE.
<!-- para 22 -->
Here we argue that the contribution of PSE to value creation in alliances may depend on the richness of learning opportunities made available by distinctive partners in these alliances.
Although novel learning opportunities are typically associated with new partners, by studying the distinctiveness of partners we isolate partner characteristics from their identities, considering, for example, the unique contribution of prior partners whose attributes have changed over time.
We thus define distinctive partners as those that reveal idiosyncratic attributes relative to the attributes of prior partners (how are you different?) rather than those that have unique identities (who are you?).
We then propose that in order to create value through partnering activities, firms need to balance the 'rich' interaction in recurrent alliances with the same partners with 'reach' to new opportunities that emerge by partnering with partners that have distinctive attributes.
In fact, PSE and exposure to partner diversity highlight two distinct aspects of learning from alliances.
While the accumulated partnering experience in recurrent alliances contributes to the efficiency of the learning process, the distinctiveness of partners relative to prior partners increases the opportunities for novel learning (Dussauge, Garrette, and Mitchell, 2000).
<!-- para 23 -->
PSE and partner distinctiveness are mutually reinforcing factors that work in tandem to determine the impact on value creation in alliances.
Partner distinctiveness reinforces the PSE effect because each partner brings a different set of resources, skills, characteristics, and information into the alliance relationship (Koka and Prescott, 2002).
Such alliances with distinctive partners create an efficient configuration that introduces novelty and provides a firm with access to a diverse set of information and resources, which are vital to its success (Baum, Calabrese, and Silverman, 2000; Powell, Koput, and Smith-Doerr, 1996).
Moreover, since firms typically absorb and internalize knowledge in the course of their alliances (Hamel, 1991; Khanna, Gulati, and Nohria, 1998), the added value of forming alliances with partners that once offered access to a distinct set of resources diminishes over time.
Consequently, some of the efficiency gains that a firm receives from recurrent alliances with the same partners can be reinforced by benefits that are associated with partner distinctiveness.
In other words, the value creation impact of partnering experience depends on the ability of each new partnering transaction to offer some novel elements relative to prior transactions.
<!-- para 24 -->
Therefore, it is likely that the accumulated PSE of a firm will produce higher gains to the extent that the assets of the new partner differ from those of prior partners of the firm.
The simultaneous combination of PSE and the capitalization on learning opportunities in alliances is feasible because the assets of the firm and its partners change over time.
As these assets change, new learning opportunities emerge and these prior partners can still offer novel contributions in their new alliances with the firm.
Moreover, the firm can engage in recurrent alliances with a group of prior partners while simultaneously exploring opportunities with new partners, thus leveraging both PSE and partner distinctiveness.
<!-- para 25 -->
Consequently, we expect that prior experience with the same partners will generate higher gains to the extent that the new alliance provides the firm with additional learning opportunities.
Although PSE enhances the efficiency of learning by facilitating coordination, knowledge sharing, and internalization, learning benefits intensify with partner distinctiveness, which contributes to the value of accumulated knowledge.
PSE enhances the efficiency of the learning curve, while partner distinctiveness increases the magnitude of the potential learning benefits.
In sum, the value creation effect of PSE is moderated by the extent to which the assets of the partner participating in the new alliance differ from those of prior partners.

<!-- para 28 -->
We proceed by noting that the extent to which accumulated partnering experience translates to greater value in new alliances depends on some firm-specific factors.
In particular, the capacity to leverage such experience depends in part on the availability of firm-specific resources that a firm can employ in its alliances.
Although prior research has suggested that availability of internal resources substitutes for the need to form alliances and may restrict the benefits that a resource-rich firm can extract from its alliances (Lavie, 2006), we note that a firm's operational resources, including technological and financial resources, contribute to its capacity to apply its partnering experience in new alliances, and thus reinforce the benefits obtained from PSE.
<!-- para 29 -->
To generate value from its partnering experience, a firm should be able to identify and assimilate external knowledge derived from such experience as well as exploit it in new commercial applications (Cohen and Levinthal, 1990; Lee, Lee, and Pennings, 2001; Zahra and George, 2002).
Therefore, whereas recurrent alliances provide the firm with learning opportunities in which partnering experience is accumulated, experience benefits may be fully realized only if the firm can incorporate and apply its experience in its ongoing operations and new projects (Van den Bosch, Volberda, and Boer, 1999).
The contribution of accumulated partnering experience to value creation in new alliances thus depends on the firm's capacity to transform and exploit such experience (Zahra and George, 2002).
<!-- para 30 -->
Opportunities for exploiting partnering experience in new alliances emerge when operational resources, which encompass those resources that the firm can employ in its ongoing operations, are at its disposal.
In particular, prior research has highlighted the role of technological and financial resources in this respect (Chatterjee and Wernerfelt, 1991; Montgomery and Hariharan, 1991).
The munificence of technological and financial resources facilitates investments in the development of commercial applications, and thus extends the range of opportunities in which partnering experience can be employed.
A firm that can commit resources to the ongoing operations of its alliances can more fully exploit its partnering experience because resource-backed alliances enable substantial engagements with partners and joint value creation.
The firm's technological and financial resources serve as a catalyst that enhances the value of the firm's partnering experience.
<!-- para 31 -->
Although the above argument may apply to both GPE and PSE, there is an additional reason to expect operational resources to enhance the contribution of PSE to value creation in new alliances.
Specifically, experience accumulated in recurrent alliances with the same partners enables a more productive application of operational resources in new alliances.
Assuming possible opportunistic behavior by partners (Williamson, 1985), a firm may limit its collaborative efforts and resource investments in fear of knowledge spillovers and misappropriation of rents (Gulati, Khanna, and Nohria, 1994; Khanna et al, 1998; Lavie, 2006).
However, recurrent alliances with the same partners nurture interfirm trust (Gulati, 1995) and thus alleviate concerns of opportunism and encourage commitment of operational resources (Dyer and Singh, 1998).
In this sense, PSE reduces some of the transaction costs associated with the monitoring of partners' behavior and the use of formal safeguards and isolating mechanisms that protect the firm's operational resources in alliances.
Consequently, the market may expect a more productive allocation of technological and financial resources in recurrent alliances with the same partners.
The interplay of PSE and the munificence of operational resources bestows confidence that these resources are both available and can be diligently allocated to the firm's new alliances with limited concerns of misappropriation.
<!-- para 32 -->
> [!hypothesis]
> Hypothesis 4: The contribution of partner-specific experience to a focal firm's abnormal market returns following the announcement of an alliance will increase with the munificence of the firm's technological and financial resources.
<!-- para 34 -->
Uncertainty surrounding the expected performance of the focal firm is another firm-specific factor that may enhance the contribution of partnering experience to value creation in alliances.
To fully comprehend the role of such firm-specific uncertainty in shaping the contribution of partnering experience to market returns, we consider investors' perceptions of the firm's PSE.
Research employing abnormal market returns in the study of value creation in alliances is often criticized for the market efficiency assumption, that is, assuming that investors enjoy homogenous and superior access to information, which allows them to accurately predict the implications of corporate events (Kale et al., 2002).
In practice, firms vary in terms of the levels of uncertainty surrounding their operations and expected performance.
Firm-specific uncertainty refers to the lack of assurance about the probability and outcomes of corporate decisions (e.g., joint venture formation, mergers and acquisitions, entry to new markets, etc.), which derives from sources that are idiosyncratic and mostly internal to the firm (Beckman, Haunschild, and Phillips, 2004).
Firm-specific uncertainty can be distinguished from market uncertainty that is exogenous in nature and reflects exposure to the risk shared by all firms operating in the market.
The level of firm-specific uncertainty is critical for studying value creation in alliances, given high failure rates of alliances (Kale et al., 2002; Kogut, 1989) and the fact that only about half of the firms that engage in alliances gain positive market returns (Chan et al., 1997; Crutchley et al., 1991).
Under conditions of high-level firm-specific uncertainty, investors may find it challenging to assess the ex ante performance implications of alliances.
<!-- para 35 -->
PSE can attenuate firm-specific uncertainty since such uncertainty is often associated with the limited accessibility of relevant corporate information.
Besides direct communication, investors can rely on informational clues based on the firm's past behavior.
The firm's prior partnering experience provides such information concerning its capacity to manage new alliances.
Although GPE may affect investors' expectations in this respect, the firm's PSE provides essential clues about the expected compatibility between the firm and its partner as well as the parties' capacity to collaborate effectively in their joint alliances (Kale et al., 2000).
Recurrent partnering with the same partners, that is, PSE, thus provides more accurate indication of the expected nature of interaction with partners and enhances the legitimacy of the announced alliance.
<!-- para 36 -->
The value of relevant informational clues in alliance formation events increases with the level of firm-specific uncertainty due to the scarcity of relevant information needed to determine the likelihood of success of new alliances.
Since investors tend to value alliances in which the outcomes of collaboration occur with a high level of certainty, as the level of uncertainty increases, so does the value of information regarding prior relationships formed between the firm and the partner in question (Higgins and Gulati, 2003; Podolny, 1994).
Hence, PSE serves as a signal that establishes confidence among investors and alleviates their potential concerns about the value of the firm's new alliance; however, the impact of this signal varies with the level of firm-specific uncertainty.
Under conditions of high firm-specific uncertainty, where only limited information is available for assessing the likely success of the alliance, the PSE signal becomes highly valuable in assessing alliance outcomes.
Specifically, a history of prior alliances with the partner in question establishes confidence in the capacity of the firm to extract benefits from new alliances with that partner.
The influence of PSE diminishes, however, as supplementary information becomes available about the firm's operations, or when the firm can demonstrate a consistent track of historical performance, which makes forecasting more reliable.
Consequently, the contribution of PSE to value creation in new alliances may intensify with the level of firm-specific uncertainty.
<!-- para 37 -->
> [!hypothesis]
> Hypothesis 5: The higher the level of firm-specific uncertainty, the stronger the contribution of partner-specific experience to the focal firm's abnormal market returns following the announcement of an alliance.

## methods
<!-- para 3 -->
We study bilateral joint ventures formed among Fortune 300 firms (focal firms) between 1987 and 1996.
Joint ventures involve the formation of new business entities and thus are more likely to receive media coverage and elicit real market responses.
Investors are typically familiar with large publicly traded firms and can thus better assess their characteristics and likely performance.
As indicated earlier, prior research suggests that partnering experience is mode specific (Anand and Khanna, 2000) and therefore allows for the consideration of experience effects within a single mode of organization (e.g., joint venture).
This context allows for comparison of our findings to those of Anand and Khanna (2000) who considered GPE in joint ventures.
Consistent with prior studies, we used the joint venture formation event as the unit of analysis.
From the initial sample of Fortune 300 firms (as ranked in the year 1988) we discarded 20 firms that lacked valid financial data in the CRSP and Compustat databases and another 96 firms that had no joint ventures with other focal firms.
<!-- para 4 -->
The final sample included 184 focal firms [^5] that participated in a total of 628 joint ventures with other focal firms during the study period.
To construct the partnering experience measures, we incorporated data on all reported joint ventures in which each focal firm participated with any partner between the years 1975 and 1996.
Overall, we obtained 3,398 records referring to joint ventures with 2,008 unique partners, of which 814 were publicly traded with financial data available in Compustat.
The final database included 1,256 observations of joint ventures among focal firms (each joint venture accounted for two observations since it involved two focal firms).
<!-- para 5 -->
Data on joint venture announcements were extracted from detailed reports obtained from Lexis-Nexis (for the years 1975–1996) and from the Security Data Corporation (SDC) database (for the years 1986–1996). [^6] After cross-validation, duplicate records referring to the same transactions were eliminated by tracking the original press releases in the Dow Jones Interactive database and keeping the record that corresponded to an earlier announcement date.
Firm-level data were gathered from the CRSP and Compustat databases.
Data used for constructing the dependent variable were extracted from the CRSP database, which provided data on stock market returns for the period of 250 days prior to the joint venture announcement date until 10 days prior to the announcement (market model) as well as for the two-day event window that included the day of announcement and one day prior to the announcement (Gulati and Wang, 2003; Koh and Venkatraman, 1991; McConnell and Nantell, 1985; Reuer and Koza, 2000).
To construct the independent variables, we extracted from Compustat firm-level data corresponding to the focal firms and their 814 publicly traded partners during the years 1974–1995.
Independent variables were lagged by one year relative to the dependent variable to allow for causal interpretation of the results.
<!-- para 8 -->
***Cumulative abnormal returns (CAR).*** We measured firm-level value creation effects of joint venture formation events by calculating the cumulative abnormal stock market return for each joint venture announcement.
CAR is the most frequently used performance measure in joint venture event studies (Balakrishnan and Koza, 1993; Chung, Koford, and Lee, 1993; Finnerty, Owers, and Rogers, 1986; Koh and Venkatraman, 1991; McConnell and Nantell, 1985; Park and Kim, 1997; Reuer and Koza, 2000).
Our use of CAR as a dependent variable builds on a rich body of research on partnering experience in alliances (Anand and Khanna, 2000; Merchant and Schendel, 2000).
CAR is an ex ante market-based measure of expected return for a firm participating in a joint venture.
Yet, prior research indicates high correlation of about 40 percent between this objective measure and ex post qualitative performance measures based on managerial assessments (Kale et al., 2002; Koh and Venkatraman, 1991).
For our purposes, these attributes make CAR a suitable measure to gauge the extent of likely value creation in joint ventures.
<!-- para 9 -->
For the firms in our sample, investors could access sufficient relevant information to assess various aspects of partnering experience.
Information about technological and financial resources was also readily available for these publicly traded firms.
Joint venture announcements and related press releases typically mention prior joint ventures with the same partners as well as with other partners.
The Appendix reports representative excerpts from press releases concerning some of the joint ventures in our sample.
As illustrated by the example of Apple, IBM, and Motorola, these press releases often discuss the differences between the announced joint venture and prior joint ventures, thus allowing investors to evaluate some of the independent variables used in this study.

We estimated the market model for the period $t = [-250, -10]: r_{it} = \alpha_i + \beta_i r_{mt} + \varepsilon_{it}$, where $r_{it}$ is the common stock return of firm $i$ on day $t$, $r_{mt}$ is the corresponding daily market return on the equal-weighted S&P 500, $\alpha_i$ and $\beta_i$ are firm-specific parameters, and $\varepsilon_{it}$ is the error term.
The resulting estimates were used for predicting the daily returns for firm $i$ over the two-day event window $[-1, 0]: \hat{r}_{it} = \hat{\alpha}_i + \hat{\beta}_i r_{mt}$, where $\hat{r}_{it}$ is the predicted return and $\hat{\alpha}_i$ and $\hat{\beta}_i$ are the model estimates.
We calculated the daily firm-level abnormal returns: $\hat{\varepsilon}_{it} = r_{it} - \hat{r}_{it}$, and then computed the cumulative abnormal return of firm $i$ during the event window using the formula: $CAR_i = \sum_t \hat{\varepsilon}_{it}$, where $t$ ranges from $-1$ to $0$.

The choice of a narrow two-day event window demonstrates a conservative approach that excludes unrelated events occurring prior to or following the relevant announcement.
According to Woolridge and Snow (1990), researchers should capture the change in the stock price immediately following the alliance announcement.
Since most announcements are actually made prior to the close of the previous trading day, the optimal window is set at  $[-1,0]$.
Subsequent research has demonstrated that the two-day window is much more effective in capturing market responses than longer windows (e.g., Crutchley et al., 1991).
In fact, Anand and Khanna (2000), who considered a longer window of  $[-10,3]$, found that joint ventures elicited significant market responses only on the announcement day without evidence of information leakage.

***General partnering experience (GPE).*** For each focal firm, we measured GPE by calculating the accumulated number of all prior joint ventures in which the firm participated beginning in 1975 until the day prior to the recent joint venture announcement.
This measure was updated for each new joint venture event and used for testing Hypothesis 2.
<!-- para 15 -->
***Partner-specific experience (PSE).*** For each focal firm, we measured PSE by calculating the accumulated number of all prior joint ventures formed with the same partner beginning in 1975 until the day prior to the recent joint venture announcement.
This measure was updated for each new joint venture event between the focal firm and a focal partner and was incorporated as a main effect when testing Hypotheses 1 and 2.
<!-- para 16 -->
***Partner distinctiveness (PD).*** For each focal firm, we measured the extent to which the asset value of the firm's recent partner differed from that of the firm's prior partners.
This measure was preferred to alternative measures based on Standard Industry Classification (SIC) codes because it enabled us to construct a continuous variable that was heterogeneous even within a given SIC.
Such size-based measure reflects some organizational differences across partners and was positively correlated with an alternative measure of industry classification differences (r = 0.165, p < 0.001). [^7] Data on the value of partners' assets was extracted from Compustat for the year preceding the joint venture event.
The measure was calculated using the formula:
<!-- para 17 -->
$$ P D_{i,n_{i}}=\ln\left(\left|\left((n_{i}-1)P A_{i,n_{i}}\Big/\sum_{j=1}^{n_{i}-1}P A_{i,j}\right)-1\right|\right), $$
<!-- para 18 -->
where  $PD_{i,n_i}$ represents the asset distance of the new partner of focal firm i, which is a function of the absolute value of this partner's assets ( $PA_{i,n_i}$) divided by the average value of assets of all prior partners since 1975.  $n_i$ is an index representing the number of joint venture events for which partner asset data were available until the recent joint venture event.
The logarithmic transformation reduced the skewness of this measure and eliminated potential outlier problems (Maddala, 2001).
The higher this measure's value, the more distinctive the recent partner relative to partners participating in prior joint ventures of the firm.
<!-- para 19 -->
This measure was incorporated as an interaction effect with PSE for testing Hypothesis 3.
<!-- para 20 -->
***Technological firm resources (TFR).*** For each joint venture in which a focal firm participated between the years 1987 and 1996, we calculated the R&D intensity of the firm (R&D expenses divided by net sales) in the year preceding the recent joint venture event.
Data on R&D expenses and net sales were extracted from Compustat.
This variable measured the resources that firms can use to engage in technology-intensive initiatives and in innovation activity, because high proportion of investment in R&D indicates commitment to technology development.
This measure was incorporated as a main effect and in the interaction with PSE for testing Hypothesis 4.
<!-- para 21 -->
***Financial firm resources (FFR).*** For each joint venture event in which a focal firm participated between the years 1987 and 1996, we calculated a measure of solvency (a function of cash divided by long-term debt) in the year preceding the recent joint venture event.
Data on firms' cash and long-term debt were extracted from the Compustat database.
Because of the skewness of this measure, a natural logarithm was taken from the resulting value.
This measure was an appropriate proxy for the firm's financial resources because it represents the munificence of funds available for investing in prospective projects and in ongoing operations.
We incorporated this measure as a main effect and in the interaction with PSE when testing Hypothesis 4. [^8]
<!-- para 22 -->
***Firm-specific uncertainty (FSU).*** Following prior research (Beckman et al., 2004), we measured firm-specific uncertainty as a function of the volatility in the focal firm's stock price.
We operationalized this measure as the difference between the standardized monthly volatility of the focal firm's stock price in the year preceding the joint venture announcement and the average standardized monthly volatility in the stock price of the sampled firms in the corresponding year.
The sampled group of 184 Fortune 300 firms with bilateral joint ventures served as a reference group for measuring systematic market-specific uncertainty.
By subtracting the market-specific volatility component, we adopted a conservative approach of considering only the volatility that is idiosyncratic to the firm.
The standardized volatility was calculated as the standard deviation in monthly closing stock price divided by its mean value during the year preceding the joint venture announcement, with the standardization allowing for comparing volatility across firms.

Similarly,  $p_{mt}$ refers to the average closing stock price across all the sampled focal firms at the end of month t.
After controlling for market-specific uncertainty, the higher the variance in the firm's stock price relative to its mean value, the higher the perceived firm-specific uncertainty.
Data on firms' closing stock prices were extracted from the Compustat database.
The FSU measure was then used in testing Hypothesis 5.
Finally, we tested the robustness of the independent variables by considering alternative operationalizations. [^9]

We also controlled for absolute firm size (Anand and Khanna, 2000; Merchant and Schendel, 2000) by including a measure of the focal firm's total assets (FS).
Following the relative size hypothesis (Das et al., 1998; Koh and Venkatraman, 1991; McConnell and Nantell, 1985), which predicts higher abnormal returns to the smaller partner, we included a measure of the relative size of the partner, calculated as partner assets divided by firm assets in the year prior to the joint venture event (RPS).
Following the business relatedness hypothesis (Balakrishnan and Koza, 1993; Koh and Venkatraman, 1991; Reuer and Koza, 2000), which predicts positive gains to partners with related businesses, we included a dummy variable that received a value of 1 when there was a match between the four-digit SIC code of the focal firm and that of the partner (REL).

Firm-specific effects were not incorporated because the event-study methodology controls for such effects when calculating the abnormal market returns. [^10]

## results
<!-- para 2 -->
The reported results (Models 1–6) reveal that the control variables produced mostly insignificant effects on CAR.
This is consistent with some prior research that found no support for the effects of firm size (Anand and Khanna, 2000), relative partner size (Merchant and Schendel, 2000), and partner relatedness (Chang and Chen, 2002; Merchant and Schendel, 2000; Reuer and Koza, 2000) on CAR following alliance announcements.
<!-- para 3 -->
Model 1 reveals insignificant contribution of GPE to firm CAR, which persisted in subsequent models.
In contrast, Model 2 shows that PSE produces a marginally significant positive effect on CAR ( $\beta = 0.059$,  $p < 0.1$).
Therefore, Hypothesis 1, which predicted positive market returns on PSE, received moderate support.
These results suggest that PSE rather than GPE contributes to value creation in new alliances.
Hypothesis 2 was tested with a one-sided F-test, comparing the standardized coefficients of GPE and PSE while holding the other variables at their mean values.
In support of Hypothesis 2, we found that  $\beta_{PSE} > \beta_{GPE}$ ( $F_{1,1039} = 2.83$,  $p < 0.05$).
<!-- para 4 -->
In support of Hypothesis 3, Model 3 indicates a positive and significant interaction effect of PSE and partner distinctiveness (PD) ( $\beta = 0.064$,  $p < 0.05$). [^11] Model 4 introduced the interactions of GPE and PSE with the firm's own technological resources (TFR).
In support of Hypothesis 4, the interaction term was insignificant for GPE but positive and significant for PSE ( $\beta = 0.190$,  $p < 0.05$).
Still, the main effect of PSE became insignificant in this model because of the high correlation between the main effect of PSE and the interaction term PSE X TFR ( $r = 0.894$,  $p < 0.001$).
The lower F values in Models 3 and 4 can be ascribed to the reduced degrees of freedom.
<!-- para 5 -->
In Model 5, the interaction effect of GPE and financial firm resources (FFR) was insignificant, but the interaction of PSE and FFR had a significant positive effect on firm CAR ( $\beta = 0.154$,  $p < 0.01$), lending support to Hypothesis 4.
The main effect of PSE in Model 5 was positive and significant ( $\beta = 0.074$,  $p < 0.05$), but the main effect of FFR produced a negative effect on CAR ( $\beta = -0.113$,  $p < 0.05$).
This result may suggest that financially distressed firms benefit more from alliance formation.
Finally, according to Model 6, the interaction of PSE and firm-specific uncertainty (FSU) produced a positive and significant effect ( $\beta = 0.176$,  $p < 0.01$) in support of Hypothesis 5, which predicted stronger PSE impact under high levels of firm-specific uncertainty. [^12] The insignificant interaction of GPE and FSU is consistent with our conjecture about the superiority of PSE over GPE.
Overall, the results show that the value-creating effect of PSE is moderated by partner distinctiveness, firm resources, and firm-specific uncertainty.

## discussion
<!-- para 2 -->
This study advances research on partnering experience by resolving some of the conceptual and empirical discrepancies surrounding the role of prior partnering experience in shaping the value created in alliances.
It uncovers the contingent value of partnering experience by shedding light on the relative significance of PSE and GPE and identifying some of the conditions under which partnering experience benefits firms in their new alliances.
Our findings show that firm- and relation-specific factors influence the impact of accumulated partnering experience on the likely gains from alliances.
They reveal that not all types of partnering experience generate gains from alliances, and that the PSE effect depends on the availability of new learning opportunities, the munificence of operational resources, and the level of firm-specific uncertainty.
Finally, we complement the literature on knowledge management and learning in alliances (Dyer and Nobeoka, 2000; Khanna et al., 1998; Grant and Baden-Fuller, 2004; Inkpen and Beamish, 1997; Kumar and Nti, 1998; Simonin, 1997) by linking the nature of learned experiences to value creation in alliances.
<!-- para 3 -->
Extending research on value creation in alliances (Anand and Khanna, 2000; Zollo, Reuer, and Singh, 2002), we clarify the distinction between GPE and PSE.
We argue that GPE may contribute to the emergence of firm-specific relational capability, yet PSE also nurtures relational mechanisms in dyadic alliances.
In contrast to recent studies that suggest that PSE does not improve alliance performance (Goerzen, 2007; Hoang and Rothaermel, 2005), we provide evidence in support of the idea that the impact of PSE on a firm's gains from alliances is greater than that of GPE.
Such a discrepancy may emerge for several reasons.
First, Hoang and Rothaermel (2005) considered drug approval as a proxy for alliance success whereas our study considers the firm's own gains from the alliance rather than the overall success of the alliance as an outcome measure.
Additionally, Hoang and Rothaermel's (2005) sample is focused on R&D alliances between pharmaceutical and biotechnology firms.
In particular, firms involved in R&D alliances may place a premium on innovation rather than on efficiency, which can be enhanced through PSE.
Moreover, pharma-biothech alliances may establish a clear division of labor between the partners: the biotech firm specializes in product development, whereas the pharma firm supports product commercialization based on its complementary assets.
Under such conditions, recurrent alliances with the same partner will not necessarily lead to the expected learning, and perhaps even reinforce specialization, which limits PSE effects.
In contrast, the multi-industry joint ventures that we study operate at various stages of the value chain and entail the formation of a separate corporate entity.
Thus, collaborative activities in our setting are likely to be distinct from the activities that each partner pursues independently.
This may explain the role of PSE in building the capacity of a specific dyad to interact effectively.
<!-- para 4 -->
Similarly, our learning perspective complements Goerzen's (2007) social network perspective for studying the effects of partnering experience.
By distinguishing between partners' distinctiveness and the firm's PSE we suggest that the negative performance implications of redundancy observed by Goerzen may be related to low partner distinctiveness rather than to PSE.
Furthermore, our finding that firm-specific uncertainty increases the positive impact of PSE complements Goerzen's (2007) finding that technical uncertainty in the industry increases the negative effect of PSE.
However, a comparison with Goerzen's (2007) study is limited because his study does not control for GPE, it relies on a sample of Japanese multinationals whose joint venturing strategies may differ from that of U.S.-based firms, and it considers a firm's short-term accounting performance as opposed to long-term market performance.
<!-- para 5 -->
In this study we demonstrate that the differential effect of PSE can be tied to the specificity of the learning process, the relative efficiency of learning, and the richer learning opportunities available in recurrent alliances with the same partners, which are emblematic of PSE. [^13] Our findings highlight the importance of developing long-term relationships with partners and exploring learning opportunities within the existing alliance portfolio.
Such a partnering strategy generates experience benefits that are not necessarily available to firms that seek occasional alliances with a wide range of partners.
Further, the specific experience accumulated with prior partners enriches firms' relational capabilities (Dyer and Singh, 1998; Gulati, 2007).
<!-- para 6 -->
A key contribution of this study derives from identifying the contingent role of partnering experience in creating value in alliances.
Value creation is enhanced when firms entering alliances balance familiarity with novelty.
Our results demonstrate that partnering experience and partner distinctiveness are mutually reinforcing rather than mutually exclusive factors.
This is akin to Lavie and Rosenkopf's (2006) finding that alliances enable firms to pursue short-term efficiency while engaging in exploration that may secure their long-term objectives.
In the current study we demonstrate that by leveraging existing alliances and simultaneously exploring new alliances that can introduce some novelty relative to prior alliances, firms can maximize their expected gains.
<!-- para 7 -->
This study also contributes by clarifying the link between firm-specific operational resources
<!-- para 8 -->
and the leveraging of learning opportunities in alliances.
Unless firms can furnish resources necessary for leveraging their partnering experience, such experience may not translate to effective learning.
Our findings suggest that a firm's technological and financial resources improve its capacity to apply and utilize its partnering experience in new alliances.
It is also possible that prior experience with a particular partner facilitates commitment of available resources, thus enhancing the expected gains from new alliances with the same partners.
Nevertheless, future research may consider how resource endowments influence value appropriation in addition to their contribution to value creation in alliances (Lavie, 2007).
<!-- para 9 -->
This study also informs the organizational learning literature in several ways.
First, we suggest that knowledge application is a key element of the learning chain that enables firms to translate accumulated experience to economic gains.
It supplements processes of knowledge creation, retention, and transfer, which have received more attention in the learning literature (Argote and Ophir, 2002).
Second, we contend that recurrent partnering provides the means to overcome some of the barriers for effective interorganizational learning.
Finally, by revealing some of the nuances of the contingent nature of partnering experience, we extend research that focused on the choice of governance mode of alliances as a key determinant of interorganizational learning outcomes (Anand and Khanna, 2000; Dussauge et al., 2000; Lane and Lubatkin, 1998).
<!-- para 10 -->
One of our most intriguing findings concerns the role of firm-specific uncertainty in shaping partnering experience effects.
We find that the perceived value of a firm's PSE increases with the challenge of predicting firm-level outcomes.
This finding highlights the role of partnering experience as an informational clue used by investors.
Hence, our study complements prior studies that considered the contribution of endorsing alliance partners under conditions of market uncertainty (Gulati and Higgins, 2003; Stuart et al., 1999) by noting that the history of alliance formation in and of itself may impact value creation under conditions of uncertainty.
Here again, our study extends the dominant view in the alliance literature, according to which uncertainty motivates alliance formation (Beckman et al., 2004; Pfeffer and Salancik, 1978; Podolny, 1994) by revealing that uncertainty also increases the value of recurrent alliances with the same partners.
In this regard, firms can use their prior partnering experience not only to build their relational capability (Dyer and Singh, 1998) but also to signal the expected outcomes of their alliances to the market.
In sum, we demonstrate that firms can gain by leveraging their accumulated partnering experience in their new alliances, yet such gains depend in part on the attributes of the firms' partners and on the firms' own resources and perceived levels of uncertainty.
<!-- para 12 -->
This study overcomes some limitations of prior research by elucidating the various facets of partnering experience and uncovering some of the boundary conditions that moderate its effects.
Yet, our study still relies on traditional measures of experience based on the accumulated number of prior alliances (Anand and Khanna, 2000; Merchant and Schendel, 2000).
Clearly, this proxy may not capture the duration of alliances and the actual nature of interaction with partners.
Thus, in-depth case studies or surveys of alliances can shed more light on the nature of experience accumulated in alliances (Hamel, 1991; Gulati, 1998).
The use of elaborated proxies for partnering experience should allow researchers to assess realized experience rather than limit investigation to potential experience or learning opportunities.
Additionally, our outcome measure of abnormal market returns, while widely used in prior research, also suffers from limitations.
For example, positive abnormal returns may reflect the market's favorable response to the reputation of the prior joint ventures of the firm and its partner regardless of actual learning processes that enable the parties to reproduce their success.
Future research may consider alternative measures such as ex post alliance performance to enable researchers to generalize our findings and examine various issues such as the potential impact of prior alliance success on recurrent alliance formation.
<!-- para 13 -->
In this study we analyzed joint ventures that entail the establishment of separate legal entities.
Future research may examine whether the observed effects hold for other modes of alliance, in an international context, and in populations of smaller firms.
However, scholars should realize the challenge of gathering data on alliances that involve less formal arrangements and for which archival data sources are limited.
Construct validity may be also enhanced by operationalizing partner distinctiveness along other dimensions besides the value of partners' assets.
For instance, distinctiveness can be measured in terms of knowledge bases, business lines, organizational structure, or strategic focus of the partners.
<!-- para 14 -->
Finally, future research should consider whether under certain conditions or beyond a certain threshold, accumulated partnering experience may hinder a firm's capacity to extract value from new alliances (Park and Kim, 1997).
In particular, it is possible that beyond a certain number of repeated alliances with the same partner, PSE produces diminishing returns.
We have not captured such a pattern in our data because of the relatively low mean level of PSE.
Nevertheless, we have demonstrated that without distinctive partner contributions, the impact of prior partnering experience is limited.
Yet, it may be possible that in practice, firms restrict their exploration activities to the existing portfolio of alliance partners because they find it difficult to adjust their organization in accordance with new alliance opportunities.
Some research in this area (Lavie and Rosenkopf, 2006; Li and Rowley, 2002) confirms that alliance portfolios are subject to inertial forces that restrict firms' partner selection decisions to proximate partners.
<!-- para 16 -->
This study advances research on the benefits of partnering experience in alliances by revealing the advantages of partner-specific experience relative to general partnering experience and by highlighting the contingency of partnering experience benefits.
Experience benefits become available to firms that develop long-term relationships while simultaneously exploring novel learning opportunities under conditions of uncertainty.
A relational capability that enables firms to manage alliances needs to be accompanied by operational resources that enable firms to exploit their experience in value creation efforts.
Efficiency and novelty are mutually reinforcing elements in the process of learning how to manage alliances.
<!-- para 17 -->
This study encourages firms to explore alliance opportunities within their existing portfolio without losing sight of distinctive partners that may introduce novel contributions.
More importantly, the success of new alliances depends on the partnering history of the firm.
Our findings concerning the relative importance of partner-specific experience reinforce the idea that decision makers need to be judicious in their choice of new partners and pay attention to the emerging pattern of alliances to which they have become committed over time.
In the search for suitable partners, decision makers would be well served to scrutinize prior alliances and evaluate the contributions of prior partners.
Today's choice of alliance partners not only guides future alliance choices, but also affects the extent to which these future alliances can produce significant gains to firms.
