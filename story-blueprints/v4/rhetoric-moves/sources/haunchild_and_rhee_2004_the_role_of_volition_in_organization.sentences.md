# haunchild_and_rhee_2004_the_role_of_volition_in_organization 句子库存

## introduction
<!-- para 2 -->
Organizational learning is typically represented as arising from internal organizational experience in a history-dependent, routine-based incremental way (cf.
Cyert and March 1963, March 1991).
There are, however, situations where learning or innovation may occur as a result of an unanticipated external challenge.
In fact, early literature on the diffusion of innovations emphasized the role of such external inducements (e.g., Downs 1967, Zaltman and Duncan 1977).
Which is more effective in producing learning—internal or external forces?
One example of external pressure is a government requirement thrust upon organizations because of a problem in the organization's product; a problem that poses a threat to public safety.
In this paper, we investigate the effects of external and internal forces for improvement on organizational learning.
We do this in the context of automotive product recalls: investigating the effect of voluntary and involuntary recalls on subsequent recall rates.
We also extend prior work on organizational learning by asking whether organizations with different structural characteristics learn differently from these recall experiences, investigating the role of generalism and specialism in the learning process.
In doing so, we contribute to the understanding of the role of volition in organizational learning and to our understanding of the different learning processes of generalist and specialist organizations.

## theory
<!-- para 2 -->
In 1966 Congress passed the National Traffic and Motor Vehicle Safety Act (15 U.S.C. 1381), which is enforced through the National Highway and Traffic Safety Administration (NHTSA).
Federal Motor Vehicle Safety Standards set minimum performance levels for those parts of the vehicle that affect its safe operation and protect drivers and passengers from death or serious injury in the event of a crash.
These standards are applicable to all vehicles and equipment manufactured for sale in the United States and certified for use on public roads and highways.
Automakers whose vehicles are deemed noncompliant with standards are subject to motor vehicle or equipment recalls (http://nhtsa.gov/cars/).
The source of automotive recalls varies, however, with some initiated by the automakers themselves and some initiated by the NHTSA.
Many decisions to recall and remedy safety defects on new vehicles are made voluntarily by automakers prior to any involvement by NHTSA.
Through their own tests, inspection procedures and information-gathering systems, automakers often discover that a safety defect exists or a safety standard requirement has not been met.
<!-- para 3 -->
In other cases, the NHTSA—rather than the automaker—initiates a recall.
Problems with vehicles may come to the NHTSA's attention in three ways.
First, certain design and performance problems may occur that may be detected and reported directly to the NHTSA Office of Defects Investigation by vehicle owners.
Defect information is also provided by other government agencies such as the Environmental Protection Agency.
Finally, the NHTSA Office of Vehicle Safety Compliance buys new vehicles each year and funds laboratories to test these vehicles.
Therefore, consumers, other government agencies, and the NHTSA's own testing processes may reveal defects, which may then result in an NHTSA-initiated recall.
<!-- para 5 -->
In this study, we are interested in asking three questions: (1) whether automakers [^1] learn from experience to reduce recalls; (2) whether subsequent recall performance is affected more by recalls initiated by the automaker or those done by NHTSA; and (3) whether different types of automakers (generalists and specialists) learn differently from involuntary and voluntary recalls.
We use the term learning to reflect organization processes that result in a subsequent reduction in the number of recalls for a given automaker.
In doing so, we follow a long tradition of research on the learning curve where performance improvements are a key learning outcome (Dutton and Thomas 1984; also, see Argote 1999 for a review of numerous other studies).
A key assumption underlying this work is that organizational learning leads to an increase in favorable outcomes or a decrease in unfavorable outcomes (Lave and March 1975).
For example, the more automakers learn, the less likely they are to experience subsequent recalls, especially serious recalls.
Studies in the learning tradition typically use cumulative production experience as a proxy for learning and knowledge improvement, and typically measure performance improvements in terms of increased productivity (Argote 1999).
More recently, however, quality and service responsiveness (Argote 1999), profitability (Ingram and Simons 2000), and organization survival (Ingram and Baum 1997b) have also been related to production experience.
The effects of experience on error reduction have not been studied.
It is possible, however, that production experience will also lead to a reduction in errors, including errors that lead to product recalls.
This is because experience increases levels of organizational knowledge (Argote 1999), and some of this knowledge is likely to be reflected in improvements in local error detection, better product design, or improved organizational processes that will reduce the incidence of errors.
<!-- para 6 -->
We therefore use as our starting hypothesis a basic learning effect: More production experience will lead to fewer future errors in the form of product recalls.
<!-- para 7 -->
HYPOTHESIS 1.
The higher the level of prior production experience, the lower the number of subsequent recalls.
<!-- para 8 -->
In addition to the organizational knowledge that comes from increasing levels of production experience, learning theories also suggest that recalls themselves will stimulate organizational learning and change.
Organizations tend to search for solutions when results fail to meet aspirations (March and Simon 1958, Cyert and March 1963, Greve 1998), and a recall can be seen as a case of failure to meet aspirations.
This is likely because, as noted earlier, recalls are serious events for automakers, resulting in financial losses as well as other indirect costs (e.g., Barber and Darrough 1996).
In general, expected outcomes tend to produce little effect, while negative surprises act as strong catalysts of search and learning effects (Sitkin 1992).
This is because they suggest that current methods are not working and because they bring new types of information into organizations relative to ordinary experience.
Thus, several contemporary learning studies show that failure (and recalls can be considered a form of failure) is an important stimulant to organizational learning and change (Sitkin 1992, Miner et al. 1999, Haunschild and Sullivan 2002, Chuang and Baum 2003, Kim and Miner 2004).
While this theory suggests that automakers will learn from their errors, there is still likely to be variance in the motivation to learn, as well as the ability to learn from different types of errors.
There is substantial variance in learning rates across organizations, and attempting to understand the sources of variation has been noted as a critical issue for future research by Argote (1999).
One source of variance is likely to be whether the learning is voluntary or mandated.
<!-- para 10 -->
What is the role of volition in organizational learning?
This is a relatively unexplored question, yet one that is likely to have important implications for predicting organizational responses to events, and for policy regarding the government enforcement of product recalls.
There are a handful of studies that investigate organizational responses to government-mandated change (e.g., Marcus 1988, Saari et al. 1993, Majumdar and Marcus 2001).
Marcus (1988) investigated nuclear power plant responses to National Regulatory Commission requirements for adopting new practices.
Marcus (1988) showed that plants that were performing well were able to adapt to these requirements in ways that led to further increases in performance.
Poorer performing plants, on the other hand, tended to comply with regulations mechanically, which resulted in further poor performance.
While this study provides valuable insights into responses to external pressures, it does not predict or explain variation in learning responses across external and internal pressures, which is what we seek to do here.
<!-- para 11 -->
In dealing with the question of whether organizations learn more from externally mandated or internally generated events, one can turn to the rather large literature on organizational learning processes and outcomes.
Basically, the question reduces to the following: do we learn more when we choose to or when we are told to?
Learning theories provide conflicting answers to this question.
Theories of the benefits of volition and the problems of rule-bound responses suggest automakers will learn more from voluntary recalls (e.g., Marcus and Nichols 1999).
Theories of attention suggest that they will learn more from government mandated recalls, as these recalls represent significant failures.
Failures tend to be salient, well-publicized events that overcome inertia and stimulate organizational change (Ocasio 1997, Ingram and Baum 1997a).
In the following sections, we outline these competing theories and hypotheses regarding the effect of volition on learning.
We then develop arguments for the effect of organizational form on organizational learning.
<!-- para 13 -->
There is some suggestion in the literature that voluntary recalls will be better for learning than involuntary ones.
For example, Marcus and Nichols (1999) studied a nuclear power plant and found that when solutions to problems were internally developed and voluntarily adopted, the plant was better able to develop competencies related to improved safety.
Therefore, voluntary recalls might lead to competencies that lead to reduced recall rates in the future.
There is also much individual-level research showing the benefits of individual autonomy over jobs and decisions (e.g., Hackman and Oldham 1980, Pfeffer 1995), and that when decisions are imposed on people, greater resistance and poorer decision quality results (Guth and MacMillan 1986, Fidler and Johnson 1984).
It may be that organization-level autonomy over decisions about what is learned and when it is learned is more effective than involuntary learning processes, precisely because the voluntary nature of this activity is better accepted by organizational members and results in more permanent change to the organization's routines and practices.
<!-- para 14 -->
According to these arguments, then, the low resistance, improved competencies, and acceptance of decisions that are voluntarily made in response to problems will result in organizational processes that lead to a reduction in future recalls.
This leads to the following hypothesis.
<!-- para 15 -->
HYPOTHESIS 2.
Voluntary recalls will lower the subsequent recall rate.
<!-- para 16 -->
One additional reason why voluntary recalls may produce more learning than involuntary is that mandated events may induce a defensive response precisely because the change is being generated externally (Marcus 1988).
Organizations may respond to external mandates by complying only as required or necessary.
This type of shallow, unwilling response is unlikely to result in knowledge that penetrates the organization's routines and practices.
With little retention of knowledge in the organization, the recurrence of similar problems in the future is likely.
In support of this idea, Perrow (1984) argues that centralizing authority over procedures to prevent and detect errors is a bad idea.
This is because the centralization of authority demotivates organizational members, resulting in mechanical compliance with rules and procedures.
Perrow (1984) further argues that such mechanical compliance increases the chances of future error.
Similarly, Meyer and Rowan (1977) give several examples of how organizations respond to institutional pressures (including government mandates) by adopting the required programs and procedures, but then decoupling them from their core activities (see also Oliver 1991).
Thus, organizations may symbolically comply with mandated procedures, but these procedures are unlikely to result in any type of deep learning that might help the detection and correction of future errors.
If this is true, then we should see evidence for shallower learning processes with involuntary than voluntary recall events.
<!-- para 17 -->
HYPOTHESIS 2A.
Involuntary recalls will result in shallower learning processes than voluntary recalls.
<!-- para 19 -->
On the other hand, involuntary recalls may produce better learning responses than voluntary, because involuntary recalls are more likely to draw organizational attention to a problem.
Although the activation of attention is not identical to learning, a typical finding is that learning occurs when attention is focused on the problem area, whereas learning is either slowed or completely suspended when attention shifts away from that problem area (Radner and Rothschild 1975, Winter 1981).
Because Simon's (1947) work on decision-making processes, organizational learning theorists have characterized organizations as systems of structurally distributed attention, defined as encompassing the noticing, encoding, interpreting, and focusing of time and efforts by organizations' decision makers (March and Simon 1958, Cyert and March 1963, Hutchins 1995).
We argue that involuntary recalls are salient events, and are thus likely to draw organizational attention to problems.
Salient events also help overcome organizational inertia, which often interferes with learning (March et al. 1991).
One might think that inertia would not be a problem in the product recall context, i.e., any problem that poses a potential threat to consumer safety (such as a recall issue) would be swiftly dealt with.
This is not, however, always the case.
For example, in the recent Firestone/Bridgestone tire recall, Ford Motor Company said that they knew of problems with Firestone tires on Explorer sport utility vehicles in Venezuela in 1998, two years before they began replacing tires for customers there (CNN USA 2000).
<!-- para 20 -->
In support of the idea that organizations are inert, and only external shocks can stimulate learning and change, Cyert and March (1963) argue that organizations are stable until a significant difference between expectations and aspirations is noted, or until the imposition of external demands occurs (see also Greve 1998 and Sitkin 1992).
As noted by Van de Ven (1986, p. 591), people are programmed to "focus on, harvest, and protect existing practices," March (1991) notes the tendency for organizations to engage in exploitation of current practices over the exploration of new ones.
But inertia can be overcome in the presence of salient events (March et al. 1991).
Thus, salient events might promote learning because they cause organizational members to pay more attention to these events—explore them more deeply and to work to prevent them in the future.
<!-- para 21 -->
We argue that involuntary recalls are more salient than voluntary recalls.
As automakers in the United States undergo, on average, about 2.5 recall campaigns per year, it seems that recalls are likely to be a relatively standard part of doing business in the automotive industry.
Most of these recalls, however, are voluntary (80%, on average), which means the involuntary recalls are likely to be more salient than voluntary ones.
Furthermore, involuntary recalls are associated with more press coverage than voluntary recalls—and much of this press coverage is likely to be negative.
According to the NHTSA, there were a total of 235 recalls involving autos, trucks, and buses sold in the United States during 1998.
Of these 235 recalls, 78% (183) were voluntary and only 22% (52) were involuntary.
We used the ABI INFORM index of business periodicals to count the number of business press articles about automotive recalls during the year 1998.
There were 368 articles published in the business press that year that dealt with these recalls.
Of these 368 articles, 47% (172) articles discussed involuntary recalls and 53% (196) discussed voluntary ones.
A test of expected versus actual numbers of articles is significant (Pearson chi-square(1)=37.2067, p<0.001), supporting the idea that involuntary recalls are associated with much more press coverage than voluntary recalls.
The press coverage of involuntary recalls is likely to be both a cause and a consequence of the salient aspect of these events.
<!-- para 22 -->
According to these arguments, then, the salience and attention-getting aspects of involuntary recalls help overcome organizational inertia and result in organizational processes leading to a reduction in future recalls.
This leads to the following hypothesis.
<!-- para 23 -->
HYPOTHESIS 3.
Involuntary recalls will lower the subsequent recall rate.
<!-- para 25 -->
The prior discussion assumes that all automakers learn equally from recall experiences.
This assumption may not be warranted.
A handful of studies exists showing that generalist firms (those that depend on a wide range of environmental resources) learn differently from specialist firms (those that depend on a narrower range of environmental resources).
These studies include Barnett et al. (1994), who found that specialist banks have higher returns on their experience than generalists.
Also, Ingram and Baum (1997a) found that generalists are less affected by their experience than specialists.
We extend this work by investigating differences between generalists and specialists in their ability to learn from voluntary versus mandated experience.
<!-- para 26 -->
One important distinction between generalists is their level of complexity.
Generalists are more complex because their larger resource space and broader range of products for customers mean that they have to deal with a wider range of issues than specialists.
Complexity magnifies the problems organizations have in controlling and coordinating behavior.
Complex organizations are less likely to change (Barnett and Carroll 1995), and thus may be less likely to learn from any type of experience, relative to specialists.
This idea is supported by Dobrev et al. (2001), who find that generalist automakers are less likely than specialists to repeat changes they have made in the past.
While some of these studies were not conducted in the auto industry, there is no reason to think they might not apply to automakers.
Complex organizations (including automakers) tend to have more personnel, more hierarchy, and more compartmentalization, which makes it difficult to coordinate the activities necessary for organizational learning to occur.
Having fewer absolute numbers of recalls is one indicator that learning has occurred.
Thus, it may be the case that, controlling for size, generalists will have more recalls than specialists.
<!-- para 27 -->
It also may be the case than generalists will learn less from voluntary or involuntary recalls than specialists will.
Haunschild and Sullivan (2002) found that generalist airlines are less likely to learn from characteristics of their own accident experiences than specialist airlines.
The same may be true of the characteristics of recalls, including whether they are voluntary or involuntary.
Thus, when it comes to learning from recalls, we propose that generalists will have more recalls than specialists.
In addition, generalists will learn less from recalls, whatever their type, than specialists.
<!-- para 28 -->
HYPOTHESIS 4A.
Generalist automakers will have more recalls than specialists.
<!-- para 29 -->
HYPOTHESIS 4B.
The ability to learn from prior voluntary and involuntary recalls will be weaker for generalist automakers than specialists.

## methods
<!-- para 2 -->
We analyze our data using a model similar to that used in the learning-curve literature (e.g., Argote 1999).
We first model the effects of cumulative production on recalls.
Then, as we are interested in the effect of cumulative recall experience, we model this directly by using cumulative recall experience (separated into voluntary and involuntary) as independent variables.
We also use an alternative measure, the proportion of voluntary recalls.
Finally, we add measures of generalism and specialism.
We also add several other control variables to the model.
<!-- para 4 -->
We obtained data on all recalls experienced by automakers that sold passenger cars in the United States (regardless of where they were manufactured) from 1966 (the year the National Traffic and Motor Vehicle Safety Act was passed) to 1999.
We chose the “automaker” (e.g., Buick, Lexus), rather than the “auto firm” (e.g., GM, Toyota) or “model” (e.g., Reatta, Regal) as our unit of analysis.
This is because each automaker has independent research and development (R&D) and production teams and, thus, takes independent responsibility for product recalls. [^2] Some automakers are the divisions of their umbrella companies (e.g., Buick of GM and Lexus of Toyota Motor Corporation) and other automakers are independent auto companies (e.g., BMW and Hyundai).
<!-- para 5 -->
Recall information was obtained from NHTSA reports.
These reports are filed for all recalls, whether voluntary and announced by automakers or involuntary and mandated by the government. [^3] The reports include information on the number of recalls by automakers and the severity of the potential hazard to safety.
We also obtained organizational data on each automaker from a variety of sources, including Wards Automotive Yearbook, three series of Motor Vehicle Facts and Figures (American Automobile Manufacturers Association, Japanese Automobile Manufacturers Association, Korean Automobile Manufacturers Association), Automotive News, Automotive News Market Data Book, Standard Catalog of American Cars 1946–1999, Standard Catalog of Imported Cars 1946–1990, Worldscope, COMPUSTAT, Disclosure, and 10-K Reports.
<!-- para 6 -->
We exclude the smallest automakers (those that produced fewer than 100 cars a year on average) from our sample because interviews with NHTSA officers revealed that these makers are not subject to the same level of enforcement under the law as the larger automakers. [^4] Therefore, our final sample consists of 47 automakers.
These 47 automakers experienced a total of 2,287 recalls during the period studied.
All variables are measured at the automaker level.
<!-- para 8 -->
Our dependent variable is the annual number of severe recalls for each automaker.
This is because recalls generally apply at the automaker level (i.e., they do not cross automakers), and each automaker's R&D division is individually responsible for such recalls.
There is considerable overlap in recalls at the model level, as recalls tend to cross different models within the same automaker (and the automaker's R&D division is responsible for all models).
<!-- para 9 -->
We concentrate on a reduction in severe recalls rather than all recalls for two reasons.
First, in a series of interviews with industry experts, we found that product recalls are not done with the intention of reducing all future recalls, but only severe recalls.
Our interviewees stated that limited resources and energy make it difficult for automakers to attempt to prevent all types of recalls, and automakers do not necessarily see nonsevere recalls as a negative event.
This is because automakers sometimes recall products with minor issues to signal that they are paying attention to the quality of their products.
Thus, automakers concentrate their learning efforts on preventing recalls producing more negative consequences, such as more severe recalls.
These statements are confirmed by Rhee and Haunschild (2003), who found that automaker sales are enhanced when they do nonsevere recalls, and are hurt when they do severe recalls.
We checked this by analyzing severe and nonsevere recalls separately, and found that only severe recalls affect subsequent recalls (details of these analyses available from the authors).
<!-- para 10 -->
We obtained data on recall severity from NHTSA recall reports.
The NHTSA has established several criteria against which each recall's level of severity is judged.
The NHTSA reports include the hazard level of the recall, with type "a" representing significant hazard without automaker's warning, type "b" a significant hazard with automaker's warning, type "c" a potential safety hazard, and type "d" a low risk of significant harm.
Our industry experts note that types "a" and "b" are considered problematic for automakers, whereas types "c" and "d" are not.
We, therefore, count the annual number of recalls of types "a" and "b" for each automaker for each year.
As a check on this classification, we also ran analyses including type "c" with severe and the results are in the same direction, but significantly weaker than when type "c" is excluded.
The same effect occurs with type "d." Thus, it appears that types "c" and "d" recalls are not targets of automaker learning, but types "a" and "b" are.
We also separate severe voluntary and severe involuntary recalls in some of our analyses, as preliminary analyses show that the factors affecting these types of recalls significantly differ.
<!-- para 12 -->
Cumulative Production.
To measure cumulative production, we collected annual data for each automaker on the number of cars produced since they began production.
We constructed a variable counting the number of cars produced and lagged this variable one year.
As this variable was highly skewed, we also took the logarithm (which is standard in learning-curve studies).
<!-- para 13 -->
Cumulative Voluntary and Involuntary Recalls.
We constructed two independent variables to test Hypotheses 2 and 3 (whether voluntary or involuntary recalls result in learning).
The first variable is prior automaker experience with recalls, separated into voluntary recalls and involuntary recalls.
Data on recalls and whether they were voluntary or not were obtained from the NHTSA database.
The NHTSA codes recalls according to the original source of the recall—whether it was initiated by the company or by the NHTSA.
Sometimes the NHTSA initiates a recall and the company, subsequently, voluntarily complies and takes over the recall.
For example, in the famous Ford Pinto case, Ford agreed to recall the Pinto, which had killed several people in rear-end collisions that caused the gas tank to explode.
This was due to a defect in the fuel tank design, which Ford had known about but decided not to fix (Center for Auto Safety 2003).
However, Ford did not agree to do this until a year after the NHTSA initiated the recall investigation and several negative press articles about the company and the design occurred.
Many recalls initiated by the NHTSA where companies voluntarily take them over have this flavor, which we think suggests that if the underlying processes of rule-bound responses and shallow learning are occurring, they will arise at the time of the original involuntary recall, and will not change later because of inertial forces and escalation of commitment processes (Staw and Ross 1989).
These recalls, even though later voluntarily taken over by companies, are still coded as involuntary in our data.
We, therefore, have a reasonably precise measure of voluntarism in the data, and one that matches our underlying theoretical processes.

<!-- para 17 -->
We constructed our independent variable as follows.
Prior research shows that the effect of prior experience tends to decay over time (e.g., Argote 1999), though there is little in the way of theory to guide the choice of discounting specification, and which discounting specification works best seems to vary from context to context (cf.
Baum and Ingram 1998, Haunschild and Sullivan 2002).
To account for the fact that older experience has less effect, we use only the prior three years of recall experiences in the analysis.
We do this because three years has been found to be a reasonable period of time over which learning effects occur in contexts where the events to be learned from are relatively frequent (e.g., Haunschild and Sullivan 2002).
We also tested other methods of accounting for the fact that the effects of older experience decays over time, with no change in the results (results of these analyses are available from the authors).
In addition, the three-year lag with no discounting produces the best model fit in all models, relative to other forms of discounting (e.g., the square root of the age of experience).
We therefore constructed an annual variable consisting of the number of recalls experienced during the three years prior to the current year.
<!-- para 18 -->
We include cumulative voluntary and involuntary recalls simultaneously in the models, as they are expected to have independent effects.
The correlation between cumulative voluntary and involuntary recalls is 0.18, which is not high enough to present multicollinearity concerns.
<!-- para 19 -->
Our second independent variable used to test Hypotheses 2 and 3 is the relative proportion of voluntary to involuntary recalls (cumulative voluntary recalls divided by cumulative total recalls) over the prior three years.
We include this proportion variable because it may be that the relative proportion of voluntary to involuntary recalls is more important than the absolute number of either.
Attention and salience triggers may be more a matter of relative numbers than absolute numbers.
That is, if an automaker has 1 involuntary recall and 10 voluntary recalls in a year, the salience of the involuntary recall may be less than having 1 involuntary recall and 1 voluntary recall in a year.
Because we could not calculate the proportion of voluntary recalls for those cases where an automaker had no recalls during the prior three years, these cases were coded zero and a dummy variable was coded one to separate unique effects for these cases from those with prior recalls.
We also ran models in which we dropped these particular cases from the analyses, with little difference in results (details of these analyses are available from the authors).
<!-- para 20 -->
Generalism and Specialism.
Measures of generalism and specialism tend to vary substantially from study to study, as they tend to be rooted in the specifics of the industry being researched (e.g., Carroll 1985).
However, Dobrev et al. (2001, 2002) studied generalism in the auto industry, so we follow them in using technological niche, or the spread of engine capacity (measured in liters) across all models that an automaker produces in a given year as an indicator of generalism and specialism.
The choice of engine capacity made by automakers reveals the range of their technological offerings and their competitive market strategies, because technological space directly affects automakers' marketing and supply chain management (for a more detailed rationale for this measure, see Dobrev et al. 2001, 2002).
The spread of engine capacity is measured as the largest minus the smallest engine capacity.
Specialists tend to produce one or two vehicles with large engine capacity, such as Porsche and Lancia.
Generalists tend to produce vehicles across a large range of capacities, such as Ford, which has the Festiva (~1.5 liter), the Focus (1.5 liter–2.0 liter), the Contour (2.0 liter–3.0 liter), the Mustang (3.0 liter–4.0 liter), and the Excursion (4.0 liter~).
Thus, higher values for the spread of engine size are an indicator of the level of generalism.
The generalism variable is a continuous variable with a mean of 1.72.
<!-- para 22 -->
Prior studies suggest several control variables that may be related to the number of an automaker's recalls.
First, we include age as a control because researchers have previously investigated the effects of aging on the behavioral processes of organizations and organizational performance (Tushman and Anderson 1986, Henderson 1999, Sorenson and Stuart 2000).
An automaker's age is measured as the difference between the current year and the time the automaker began car production.
Second, we include organizational size because size is an important factor that has been found to affect organizational learning (e.g., Greve 1999a, b).
We use an automaker's sales as our size measure.
We lagged this variable one year and took the log to reduce skewness.
<!-- para 23 -->
Third, dummy variables designed to capture the effects of different presidential administrations are included.
Prior studies have shown that the number of automotive recalls tends to increase during Democratic administrations and to decrease during Republican ones (Bromiley and Marcus 1989).
We, therefore, use dummy variables for the Johnson, Nixon, Ford, Carter, Reagan, and Bush administrations, with the Clinton administration as the excluded category.
Fourth, we control for industry competition, which has been known to affect organizational learning (Ingram and Baum 1997b).
Following Hannan and Carroll (1992), we measure industry competition as population density, or the total number of automakers existing in a given year.
However, it may be that automakers do not regard other divisions within the same automaker as competitors.
Therefore, we also ran analyses where automakers not in the same auto company were excluded, with no difference in the hypothesized results.
We therefore report analyses with these automakers included.
Finally, we include a variable measuring years elapsed from 1966 to capture any time trend effects (such as market trends or the development of error detection technology) that may be associated with changes in the dependent variables.
<!-- para 24 -->
Because we use a (conditional) fixed-effects model for our analyses, the time-invariant variables, such as an automaker's home country, are included in the within-group estimator of the coefficients on the time-varying variables.

## results
<!-- para 2 -->
If $L_{i}$ denotes reduction in the rate of subsequent product recalls of automaker i in a selected year, [^5] we model the determinants of this learning outcome using the following equation:
<!-- para 3 -->
$$ L_{i}(t)=\alpha P_{i}+\beta V_{i}+\lambda IV_{i}+\gamma G_{i}+\delta X_{i}+\mu_{i}. \tag{1} $$
<!-- para 4 -->
Here, $P_i$ refers to the prior cumulative production of the automaker, $V_i$ is the number of voluntary recalls during the prior three years, $IV_i$ is the number of involuntary recalls during the prior three years, $G_i$ refers to the level of generalism (i.e., spread of engine capacity) of the automaker during the prior year, $X_i$ refers to control variables measured in the prior year, and $\mu_i$ denotes an error term.
We substitute the proportion variable for $V_i$ and $IV_i$ in certain models, and we test Hypothesis 4b by adding an interaction term $(G_i \times IV_i)$ to the model.
<!-- para 5 -->
Because we operationalize the dependent variable as a count variable indicating the number of an automaker's severe recalls in a given year, the Gaussian distribution is not appropriate as a specification of the distribution for the dependent variable.
Accordingly, we treat recall events as an event count that follows a Poisson process, which is appropriate for relatively rare count variables (Hausman et al. 1984).
Poisson regression rests on the assumption that the mean and variance of the event counts are equivalent (Hausman et al. 1984).
However, our preliminary analysis shows that the event count over time has an overdispersion problem.
Thus, we adopt the negative binomial estimator, which accounts for and accommodates overdispersion (Barron 1992).
<!-- para 6 -->
Finally, we also use a fixed effects specification to control for unobserved heterogeneity in the automakers.
For example, there may be substantial differences across automakers in learning styles and other features of their operations (MacDuffie 1997).
We could use a fixed-effects model simply by including a dummy variable for each of the automakers.
However, this standard fixed-effects model yields inconsistent estimates of coefficients because of the “incidental parameters problem” (Cameron and Trivedi 1998) unless the number of units is fixed and relatively small.
Thus, we used a “conditional” approach in which the fixed effects are conditioned on the sum of the event counts within the panel and then concentrated out of the maximum likelihood (Cameron and Trivedi 1998).
Consequently, this conditional method completely captures maker-specific unobserved heterogeneity and thus effectively controls for any automaker-level differences in response to recalls.
We use the XTNBREG procedure in STATA 8.0 to report these conditional fixed-effects negative binomial models.
<!-- para 10 -->
To test Hypothesis 1, whether more production experience lowers subsequent recall rates, we add the log of cumulative production in Model 2.
As can be seen in Model 2, the log of cumulative production is negative and marginally significant ($p = 0.055$).
This effect is stronger in subsequent models, especially those restricted to voluntary recalls, which indicates some support for Hypothesis 1.
Thus, consistent with other learning-curve studies, which show productivity benefits as a result of experience (cf.
Argote 1999), we find an additional benefit: a reduction in subsequent errors in the form of product recalls.
<!-- para 11 -->
As analyses indicate substantial heterogeneity in the determinants of voluntary and involuntary recalls, we next separate the dependent variable into severe voluntary and severe involuntary recalls.
We first investigate the effects of prior recalls on subsequent involuntary recalls.
The dependent variable in Model 3 is involuntary recalls alone.
As can be seen in Model 3, prior voluntary recalls reduce subsequent involuntary recalls.
There is no effect of prior involuntary recalls on subsequent involuntary recalls.
This result supports Hypothesis 2 and not Hypothesis 3: Voluntary recalls reduce subsequent recalls.
The coefficient on cumulative voluntary recalls is estimated at $-0.0911$ (standard error $= 0.0259$).
Thus, an increase of 4.57 voluntary recalls (one standard deviation) decreases the expected involuntary recall rate by a factor of $0.66 (= \exp^{-0.0911 * 4.57})$.
<!-- para 12 -->
In Model 4, we switch to the proportion of voluntary to involuntary recalls as the independent variable.
Model 4 replicates the effect of Model 3: The higher the proportion of voluntary recalls, the lower the subsequent involuntary recall rate.
Thus, Hypothesis 2 is supported (and Hypothesis 3 is not) for both the number of prior voluntary recalls and the proportion of voluntary recalls.

We next investigate the effects of prior recalls on subsequent voluntary recalls.
The dependent variable in Model 5 is voluntary recalls alone.
As can be seen in Model 5, prior recalls (both voluntary and involuntary) increase subsequent voluntary recalls, which is opposite to Hypothesis 3.
Thus, it appears that learning, as measured by subsequent recalls, is restricted to involuntary recalls.
Voluntary recalls appear to be the result of more inertial forces: prior recalls of all types are positively related to subsequent voluntary recalls.
Model 6 shows that there is no effect of proportion of voluntary recalls on the subsequent voluntary recall rate.
This makes sense when one considers that both cumulative voluntary and cumulative involuntary recalls are positively related to subsequent voluntary recalls (Model 4)—if both are positive, then it is unlikely that the proportion of one to the other will have a significant effect.
<!-- para 22 -->
We now turn to an analysis of Hypothesis 2a: whether involuntary recalls cause shallow responses, unlikely to penetrate the organization's systems and processes.
To explore whether this process might be occurring, we collected data on the number of pages involved in the technical reports issued by companies for each recall campaign.
It is likely that longer technical reports reflect deeper underlying learning processes than shorter technical reports, and thus the length of these recall reports is an indicator of the depth of analysis and/or the willingness of the organization to change in response to a recall event.
These reports are also a means by which knowledge is retained in the organization, which means that shorter reports will probably result in less knowledge retention.
We therefore compare the number of pages in technical reports associated with voluntary recalls and involuntary recalls.
We find that there are significantly more pages in voluntary recall technical reports than involuntary ones (t = 4.03, df = 879, p < 0.001), which suggests the learning from involuntary recalls may be shallower and less likely to penetrate the organization or be stored in organizational memory.
<!-- para 23 -->
We also obtained data from the NHTSA on whether each recall problem was eventually solved; not all recall problems are solved.
Sometimes automakers will exchange a defective component for another version.
Sometimes they will simply pay the consumer some money for the defect, if the defect does not present a threat to safety.
Such outcomes are likely to reflect a shallower or more unsuccessful learning process on the part of the automaker.
We therefore compare the number of solved recall problems for voluntary and involuntary recalls.
Results show that voluntary recalls are more likely to be solved (chi-square = 15.138, df = 1, p < 0.001), which again suggests that the learning from involuntary recalls may be shallower.
One alternative explanation for these results is that involuntary recalls are more difficult to solve than voluntary (which is why they are involuntary).
But in a series of analyses, we explored if there is a relationship between whether a given recall is voluntary and whether it is severe.
There is no such relationship (details are available from the authors upon request).
Together, these results support Hypothesis 2a: Involuntary recalls produce shallower learning responses on the part of organizations than voluntary recalls do, which is likely to be part of the reason why voluntary recalls produce more learning (Hypothesis 2) than involuntary recalls (Hypothesis 3).
<!-- para 24 -->
Finally, we turn to the analysis of Hypotheses 4a and 4b: differences in learning by generalist and specialist automakers.
We report models using the proportion of voluntary to involuntary recalls for these analyses, but the results are the same when we use the number of voluntary and involuntary recalls (results available from the authors).
In Model 7 we add the variable measuring generalism and specialism to Model 4.
The dependent variable in Model 7 is severe involuntary recalls.
As can be seen in Model 7, there is no main effect for generalism, in that generalists do not have higher involuntary recall rates than specialists.
Thus, Hypothesis 4a receives no support when we look at involuntary recalls.
When we add the interaction of generalism with the proportion of voluntary recalls (Model 8), however, the effect is significant and negative.
This indicates that generalists with a high proportion of voluntary recalls reduce their subsequent involuntary recall rates more than specialists.
This effect is opposite to Hypothesis 4b: generalists learn more from voluntary recalls than specialists when the learning target is reducing subsequent involuntary recalls.
Figure 1 illustrates how generalism affects the impact of cumulative voluntary recalls on subsequent involuntary recalls, using the estimates of Model 8 in Table 3.
The line at the top of the figure represents the effect for the specialist automakers, and the line below it is for generalist automakers.
The decreasing trend of both lines shows the negative main effect of voluntary recalls on the subsequent involuntary recall rate.
However, the sharply steeper decrease for generalist automakers shows that generalists are better able to learn from voluntary recalls than specialists.
<!-- para 25 -->
In Model 9 we add the variable measuring generalism and specialism to Model 6.
The dependent variable in Model 9 is voluntary recalls.
As can be seen in Model 9, the effect of the generalism level is positive and significant, indicating that generalists have more severe voluntary recalls than specialists.
Thus, Hypothesis 4a is supported for voluntary recalls.
When we add the interaction of generalism with the proportion of voluntary recalls (Model 10), however, the effect is not significant.
Thus, Hypothesis 4b is not supported for voluntary recalls—generalists and specialists do not learn differently from voluntary recalls.
<!-- para 27 -->
To summarize: we find a basic learning effect in that prior production experience reduces subsequent recall rates, but only when the learning target is reducing voluntary recalls, not involuntary recalls.
We find support for Hypothesis 2: Voluntary recalls result in more learning, but only when the learning target is involuntary recalls, not voluntary recalls.
Both the number and proportion of voluntary recalls are helpful in reducing subsequent involuntary recalls.
In addition, we find support for Hypothesis 2a: Involuntary recalls produce shallower learning responses (shorter technical reports and fewer solved problems) than voluntary recalls.
We find partial support for Hypothesis 4a: Generalists have more voluntary recalls than specialists.
Finally, we find the opposite effect for Hypothesis 4b: Generalists learn better from voluntary recalls than specialists, but this holds for only the situation where the learning target is reducing involuntary recalls.

## discussion
<!-- para 2 -->
Returning to our original research question of how volition affects learning, it appears that voluntary action has a more positive effect on learning than involuntary action does.
There has been little discussion of the possible benefits of volition for learning and no work looking at how volition might have different effects on different organizational forms.
Responding to this gap in our understanding of volition and learning, we theorized and modeled improvements in automotive product recalls as a function of the volition of prior recall experience.
We find that voluntary learning (e.g., voluntarily recalling a product) leads to fewer future errors than involuntary learning.
We also find that involuntary learning leads to processes likely to result in shallower learning and more defensive responses.
The voluntary learning effect, however, only applies to reducing future involuntary errors.
Future voluntary recalls appear more inertial, where prior recalls lead to future recalls.
<!-- para 3 -->
These findings suggest some interesting boundary conditions on learning effects.
First, why does volition affect only subsequent involuntary recalls rather than all recalls?
One possibility is that reductions in voluntary recalls may not be as good an indicator of proactive learning processes as reductions in involuntary recalls, i.e., not all “failures” are alike.
We conducted a series of interviews with executives in the auto industry subsequent to this study to inform this question.
These executives stated that involuntary recalls are negative in the eyes of all constituents—automotive executives, consumers, and other parties.
We had discussed recalls earlier as though they were all negative events, in part, because there are studies showing negative stock market returns for firms that announce recall campaigns (e.g., Barber and Darrough 1996).
But these studies have not analyzed voluntary and involuntary recalls separately.
Thus, it may be that the stock market does not always react negatively to recall campaigns—only to involuntary recall campaigns.
Or it could be that even if the stock market does react negatively, customers still react positively, or management believes that there will be some positive effect of announcing (voluntary) recalls to the public.
This finding suggests that our theories of learning from failure need to be sensitive to the context of failure, specifically how various organizational constituents interpret it.
Some types of failures might be viewed positively, which means these failures are not necessarily a target of learning processes.
Only where failure is viewed negatively are we likely to see learning from failure effects.
Studies of learning from failure have primarily focused on failures that are rather unambiguously negative, e.g., firm death (Ingram and Baum 1997b, Chuang and Baum 2003) and airline accidents (Haunschild and Sullivan 2002).
This means that future studies that investigate different types of failures, if they are seen as negative in the eyes of various constituencies, and their effects on organizational learning would be valuable.
<!-- para 4 -->
A second complexity in the findings is that recalls of all types tend to be positively related to future voluntary recalls (though not involuntary ones).
This finding is consistent with the view of organizational learning as the result of history-dependent, routine-based systems that adapt only incrementally to experience (Cyert and March 1963).
These voluntary recalls may come from incremental organizational procedures that increase the likelihood of announcing a voluntary recall.
For example, a product defect may cause tighter inspection of the components in that product, causing a chain reaction in defects.
An automobile engine has more than 100 subcomponents.
If a defect is found in 1 of the subcomponents, the other 99 subcomponents might be at increased risk for successive recalls.
Such internal detection processes will result in more voluntary recalls, while simultaneously reducing involuntary recalls.
Also, when automakers experience an involuntary (NHTSA-initiated) recall, they are likely to receive more subsequent attention and inspection from the agency.
This may lead automakers to pay more attention to their own procedures and proactively initiate a voluntary recall rather than waiting for a recall order from NHTSA.
Thus, both voluntary and involuntary recalls may result in organizational processes that increase subsequent voluntary recall rates.
Again, this suggests a boundary condition on prior work on how organizational failures stimulate learning (cf.
Sitkin 1992, Chuang and Baum 2003, Haunschild and Sullivan 2002).
This prior work has shown that failure leads to a reduction in future failures.
But our results show that in some cases, such reductions do not occur, as organizations seem to proactively respond and initiate some types of failures (voluntary ones) in order to prevent others (involuntary ones).
<!-- para 5 -->
Our other results also show that the effect of production experience on learning is more complex than was originally thought and more complex than suggested by existing learning theories.
The basic learning effect occurs in that more production experience lowers voluntary recall rates.
Yet the benefits of experience, while aiding in reducing subsequent errors, only reduce the voluntary ones—there is no effect of production experience on involuntary recall rates.
This is likely to be due to the internally focused, exploitive focus of learning that comes from production experience.
March (1991) discusses the differences between exploitation, which is essentially learning to do existing procedures better, and exploration, which is essentially learning to do something different.
Exploitation tends to drive out exploration (March 1991).
The learning that comes from increasing experience with producing cars is likely to be exploitation-type learning, focused on improving existing processes.
These processes are less likely to detect the types of problems that would result in an external recall than those that could be internally detected.
This suggests a boundary condition on prior research looking at the effects of experience on learning: The knowledge produced by experience may be primarily exploitative in nature.
Explorative knowledge may be generated through other processes such as the voluntary learning that results in reduced involuntary recalls in this study.
<!-- para 7 -->
We find generalists have more voluntary recalls than specialists.
When combined with the finding that generalists also learn to reduce their subsequent involuntary recalls, this finding suggests that the inertial disadvantages of generalists are primarily linked to improving existing processes (exploitive learning).
However, experiencing a voluntary recall produces learning effects for generalists in that they learn to reduce subsequent involuntary recalls.
Their better responses in terms of reducing involuntary recalls may be because generalists, being larger automakers, get more publicity from their involuntary recalls than specialists.
It also appears that the difference between involuntary and voluntary recalls in terms of the number of models affected is greater for generalists than specialists.
Specialists average 3.26 models affected by voluntary recalls, 4.64 for involuntary (t = 2.11, p < 0.05).
Generalists average 3.75 models affected by voluntary recalls, but 8.04 for involuntary (t = 13.05, p < 0.001).
Thus, it seems that generalists may be more concerned about avoiding involuntary recalls than specialists, and thus learn more from their recall experiences to reduce subsequent involuntary recalls.
This result adds to our understanding of the different learning processes of generalists and specialists.
We based our original hypothesis on the results of other studies that show that generalist organizations tend to be inert, and thus learn less or learn more slowly than specialists (e.g., Barnett et al. 1994).
We find that this is true with respect to the generalist tendency to have more voluntary recalls (suggesting they are learning less than specialists).
But we find this is not true when the learning involves events that have more negative consequences for generalists than specialists.
<!-- para 9 -->
As noted earlier, our results have interesting implications for organizational learning theories.
They also have policy implications.
For our sample, external mandate does not seem to have a positive effect in terms of reducing subsequent recalls.
Of course, organizations must respond to the specific event that generated the policy (in our case, the recall event), but it seems that the learning from this event does not translate into procedures that prevent future negative events.
This is supported by our finding that the recall reports generated in response to externally mandated recalls tend to be shorter than those of voluntary recalls, and the item causing the recall is less likely to be fixed.
The idea that involuntary recalls do not translate into procedures preventing future negative events is also supported by our interviews with automotive executives, who indicate that they tend to view involuntary recalls as unfair and tend to attribute such recalls to bad luck, uneven imposition of NHTSA recall criteria, or some uncontrollable external factor rather than as a signal of issues with product quality.
<!-- para 10 -->
The idea that involuntary regulation enforcement has negative effects, such as inhibiting learning, is also supported by studies in the policy literature.
Several studies in this literature have looked at the effects of different types of regulations on firms, and have noted that well-designed regulations tend to focus on flexible approaches that allow organizations some discretion in their responses (e.g., Porter and van der Linde 1995, Marcus 1988).
These “flexible regulations” are generally better in terms of producing favorable firm (and societal) outcomes, because they tend to encourage entrepreneurial responses and risk taking in the area that is being regulated (Majumdar and Marcus 2001).
While we have a single type of regulation, the fact that voluntary compliance with this regulation produces future benefits for organizations is consistent with the key finding in this literature: Flexible regulation is better for organizations than non-flexible regulation.
<!-- para 11 -->
One limitation of this study is that, although we find evidence consistent with various forms of learning, the underlying processes that produce the relationship between voluntary recalls and learning can only be explored in a limited way (i.e., our analyses of the number of pages of technical reports and whether the recall problem was solved).
Thus, future research investigating the underlying process in more depth would be valuable.
For example, automakers' responses to recall events could be tracked over time, and detailed data on responses to voluntary and mandated recalls could be collected.
<!-- para 12 -->
The above findings are important, especially given that they occur in the context of events of consequence for organizations and consumers (product recalls).
Theoretically, our findings help establish links between learning theories and theories of organizational form (i.e., niche width theories).
Our contribution to learning theories lies in developing and showing the effects of a variable that is not yet well studied—volition of learning experiences.
Our results show that volition has different effects on the recall rate of generalists and specialists, which helps link learning theories with theories of organizational form.
We find some evidence that generalists learn more than specialists, and some that they learn less.
Future research investigating the different learning patterns of generalists and specialists, and why they seem to differ in this case, would be valuable.
The differential findings for generalists and specialists contribute to one tension in the learning literature—that of understanding the sources of variation in learning rates across different organizations (Argote 1999).
<!-- para 13 -->
The results of this study also have implications for institutional theories that discuss how firms address institutional pressures by adopting new programs, activities, and structures (Oliver 1991), although sometimes, organizations respond to these pressures with symbolic adoption (Meyer and Rowan 1977, 1978; Westphal and Zajac 1994; Zajac and Westphal 1995).
In effect, our results show that institutionally mandated change is not as effective as voluntary change if the measure of effectiveness is the organization's ability to reduce subsequent violations of institutional rules.
This may be the result of symbolic adaptation to mandated change.
Thus, while automakers are conforming to the letter of the law in these cases, they may not be conforming to its spirit—using the experience as a tool from which to learn to prevent recalls in the future.
This discussion is necessarily speculative, as we have only an indirect measure of responses to mandates.
Future studies that examine the relationship between external product mandates and organizational responses would be valuable.
<!-- para 14 -->
Finally, we also contribute to the small but growing body of work on how and when organizations learn from failure (Sitkin 1992, Ingram and Baum 1997b, Miner et al. 1999, Haunschild and Sullivan 2002, Chuang and Baum 2003).
Recalls are a form of failure, and we showed that voluntary failures can be a source of learning.
One issue with all research on failure is that failures are generally rare events.
Yet our evidence shows that such failures can lead to learning.
This is in keeping with studies showing that failure is an important stimulant to organizational learning, in part because failures are highly salient and difficult to hide (Sitkin 1992, Greve 1998, Miner et al. 1999, Kim and Miner 2004).
We also find different learning effects when comparing learning from common, nonsalient events such as cumulative production experience with learning from relatively rare, salient events like recalls.
Much of the learning-curve research to date has focused on common, nonsalient events, and yet this study showed that rare events also promote learning.
Research could profit from investigating whether all failures affect learning, possibly by comparing across different industries and different types of failure events (e.g., accidents, financial crises, organizational failures).
Such differences are a fruitful place to begin to deepen our understanding of the conditions under which failures can be a source of learning (cf.
Ocasio 1995).
<!-- para 16 -->
Our findings about the role of volition in learning by automakers suggest important features of the learning process.
Beyond automakers, the research presented here points to the importance of investigating learning processes and the interaction of learning and organizational form in other areas.
For example, the rate of other important product recalls like pharmaceuticals and children's toys may be similarly affected by volition.
The underlying processes of learning and knowledge management resulting from these events are clearly important and deserving of further study.
