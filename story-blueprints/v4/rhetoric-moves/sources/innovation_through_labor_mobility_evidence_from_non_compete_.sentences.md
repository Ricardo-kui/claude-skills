# innovation_through_labor_mobility_evidence_from_non_compete_ 句子库存

## introduction
<!-- para 2 -->
Policymakers are actively grappling with the optimality of a variety of policies that restrict labor mobility. [^1] Proponents argue that such restrictions provide innovation incentives to firms that outweigh negative worker outcomes.
Accordingly, these policies shape labor markets around the world.
For example, a large share of the United States workforce is subject to non-compete agreements (NCAs) [^2] – restrictive covenants in employment contracts that prevent employees from joining or starting a rival firm for some duration post-separation.
The use of NCAs has grown in the US over the last few decades, in parallel with an increase in the legal enforceability of NCAs in many states. [^3] More recently, however, some states have started to restrict the enforceability of NCAs, and NCAs have become a hotly contested public policy issue.
In particular, the Federal Trade Commission (FTC) voted 3-2 to approve a rule to ban NCAs at the federal level in April 2024.
<!-- para 3 -->
In this paper, we focus on an oft-touted benefit of NCAs: their potentially positive effect on innovation.
As discussed in additional detail below, relatively few papers in the existing literature consider the overall net impact of NCAs on innovation. [^4] We help fill this gap – together with papers like He (2025) and Johnson et al. (2023a) – by providing robust causal evidence on the economy-wide impacts of NCAs on innovation.
As shown in Figure 1, recent data from the Bureau of Labor Statistics (BLS) National Longitudinal Survey of Youth (NLSY97) [^5] [BLS (2019)] suggest that NCAs are especially prevalent among professional sector “knowledge workers” – the executives, managers, computer specialists, engineers, researchers, and scientists, whom we might expect to be the most involved in innovation – with nearly 50% of these workers reporting being subject to an NCA. [^6] Kitch (1980) summarizes the intuition behind the presumed positive effect of NCAs on innovation: if “the courts leave employees free to leave the firm and exploit the information in competition with the firm[.] this competition eliminates the return that would otherwise generate the incentive for investment in the production of that information.” There is a common presumption in the legal literature and related policy discussions that it is this benefit to innovation that must be traded off against negative impacts on workers like reduced wages and mobility.

<!-- para 7 -->
However, there are reasons to think that NCAs might harm innovation.
For example, NCAs could have a negative effect on entry when new firms cannot hire the workers they need because those workers are covered by incumbent NCAs. [^7] NCAs may also reduce the cross-firm flow of new ideas and technologies that facilitate knowledge diffusion and innovation spillovers. [^8] Indeed, some have argued that Silicon Valley’s success relative to Route 128 stemmed in part from California’s general refusal to enforce NCAs, enabling greater labor mobility and knowledge flows between firms (Gilson, 1999).
<!-- para 8 -->
Thus, the net impact of NCAs on innovation is theoretically ambiguous.
Decreased enforceability could enhance innovation through increased knowledge spillovers but could also reduce the protection of intellectual property and thereby dampen firms' incentives to invest in R&D and innovation.
This tradeoff ultimately requires comprehensive empirical analysis.
In this paper, we therefore tackle the following two questions.
First, what is the net impact of a change in NCA enforceability on innovation?
And second, what channels explain this effect?
<!-- para 9 -->
To estimate the effect of NCAs on innovation, we leverage judicial rulings and legislative policy changes as sources of plausibly exogenous variation in the state-level enforceability of NCAs. [^9] And we measure innovation and inventor mobility using detailed US Patent and Trademark Office (USPTO) data on patent filings.
Our analysis begins with a case study to investigate the effects of a single judicial change that strengthened NCA enforceability in Ohio.
We find that patenting in Ohio fell significantly relative to a synthetic control.
We then estimate the effect of state-level changes in NCA enforceability on innovation using all variation nationwide from 1991 to 2016 and a state-year difference-in-differences approach.
This more comprehensive analysis identifies an economically and statistically significant decline in patenting due to increases in NCA enforceability: for an increase in enforceability of the mean size in our sample, in-state patenting decreases by 14%.
Thus, contrary to the hypothesis laid out by NCA proponents in policy debates, the net effect of NCAs on innovation seems to be negative.
<!-- para 10 -->
This negative effect is particularly pronounced when focusing on more novel patents, patents in younger technological fields, and those by venture capital-funded firms, with effects roughly double the baseline estimates in each case.
These striking differences across multiple plausible measures of innovation quality suggest that our baseline results may actually understate NCAs' true impact on innovation.
Moreover, the innovation-dampening effects of NCAs extend beyond patenting to broader measures of firm performance: using Compustat data, we find that increased NCA enforceability leads to significant declines in average total factor productivity, with a 6% decrease after five years for an average-sized increase in enforceability.
<!-- para 11 -->
Alternative explanations for this estimated decline in state-level patenting are also unlikely to explain our results.
Our results do not seem to be driven by changes in the propensity of firms to patent a given innovation (i.e., to substitute between patents and trade secrets), and cross-state migration appears to be minimal in this setting such that it neither threatens the validity of our estimated treatment effect nor limits us from using the results to think about the effect of a national policy.
We also provide a number of checks in the appendix to show that our results are robust to alternative sample definitions, econometric specifications, location definitions, etc.
<!-- para 12 -->
To understand the mechanisms behind the overall effect of NCAs on innovation, we consider three specific channels through which NCAs may affect innovation: incumbent innovation incentives, firm entry, and labor mobility.
The estimated negative net effect of highly enforceable NCAs on patenting indicates that incumbent innovation incentives are outweighed by one or both of the other channels.
Therefore, we assess the entry and labor mobility channels individually to disentangle the overall net effect.
We find that the entry channel (defined either in terms of new business applications or entrant patent applications) does not drive the overall decline in patenting.
In contrast, we find compelling evidence that reduced labor mobility plays a central role: for example, we observe both a statistically significant decline in inventor moves across firms and a substantial increase in the share of firms' citations to their own patents, suggesting decreased knowledge flows across organizations.
These results indicate that labor mobility and knowledge diffusion may be key determinants of innovation effects in this setting.
<!-- para 13 -->
This paper contributes to a growing literature on NCAs.
A number of papers address specific NCA-related labor market outcomes.
For example, Lipsitz and Starr (2022) and Young (2024) look at the effect of banning NCAs on wages for low-wage workers in Oregon and Austria, respectively.
Balasubramanian et al. (2020) examine the impact of NCA restrictions in Hawaii on the careers of technology workers.
And Johnson et al. (2023b) look at the implications of changes in enforceability for earnings and job mobility.
<!-- para 14 -->
Several papers that consider the impact of NCAs on specific dimensions of innovation are also informative to our analysis here but, together, point to persistent empirical tensions in the literature.
For example, Xiao (2022) finds that increased NCA enforceability leads to a higher rate of total innovation in the medical device industry.
But Jeffers (2023) finds that increased NCA enforceability leads firms to increase investment in physical but not intangible capital like R&D.
Moreover, despite Xiao (2022)'s estimated overall increase in innovation, he estimates a decrease in "exploratory" medical device innovation under higher NCA enforceability, which seems potentially at odds with Conti (2014)'s finding that higher NCA enforceability is associated with "riskier" R&D.
And, while Barnett and Sichelman (2020) argue that "empirical studies fail to support a causal relationship between [NCAs], reduced labor mobility, and reduced innovation," Marx et al. (2009) show that an inadvertent repeal of Michigan's NCA prohibition led to a decline in inventor mobility.
<!-- para 15 -->
A number of papers also focus on the entry dimension, with different settings producing a mix of findings.
Carlino (2021) finds no impact of increased NCA enforceability on entry in Michigan.
In contrast, Johnson et al. (2023a) and Jeffers (2023) find that stronger NCA enforceability reduces entry based on the Census Bureau's Business Dynamics Statistics and matched employee-employer data from LinkedIn, respectively.
Relatedly, Starr et al. (2018) and Baslandze (2022) find that higher NCA enforceability reduces spinouts.
And Samila and Sorenson (2011) find that stronger NCAs dampen the effects of venture capital (e.g., firm starts).
Our paper contributes to this debate by bringing in evidence from the Census Bureau's Business Formation Statistics, which cover the universe of prospective new firms, and from Ewens and Marx (2024)'s Founding Patents dataset on young firm innovation.
In this context, we find no impact of NCA enforceability on new firm entry and negative but statistically insignificant effects on newly founded firms' patenting.
<!-- para 16 -->
Some of these contrasting results may be driven by different empirical settings or different focal policy changes.
But, in tandem with the theoretical tensions discussed above, there remains a clear need for comprehensive evidence on how NCAs affect innovation across the broader economy.
Our analysis helps fill this gap by exploiting a rich set of policy changes to provide robust causal evidence on the economy-wide impacts of NCAs on innovation.
The closest work to our findings on innovation are He (2025) and Johnson et al. (2023a), which affirm our baseline patenting results.
He (2025) finds that stronger NCA enforceability leads to a 33% decline in firm patent values over assets, while Johnson et al. (2023a) find that an average-sized increase in NCA enforceability leads to 16-19% fewer citation-weighted patents over the following 10 years.
These findings are consistent with our estimate of a 14% loss in patenting. [^10] In addition to establishing the negative causal effect of NCAs on innovation, our paper provides novel evidence on the mechanisms driving this effect, highlighting how reduced inventor mobility dampens innovation by limiting knowledge spillovers across firms, and suggestive evidence of associated productivity declines in firm productivity.
In this regard, we also contribute to the broader literature on innovation incentives and spillovers.
<!-- para 17 -->
This paper proceeds as follows.
In Section 2, we describe the data used in this paper, including how we quantify changes in NCA enforceability.
In Section 3, we introduce a case study to fix ideas before outlining our empirical approach for the all-state analysis.
In Section 4, we present our all-state results that document the negative average treatment effect of an increase in NCA enforceability on patenting, and explore to what extent these results reflect a true decline in innovation.
In Section 5, we discuss our conceptual framework for the mechanisms through which NCA enforceability might affect innovation and examine the empirical evidence on the possible drivers of the observed overall effect.
Finally, in Section 6, we discuss our results and briefly consider national policy counterfactuals.
<!-- para 20 -->
In order to estimate the impact of NCAs on innovation, we track and quantify state-level changes in NCA enforceability by following Bishara (2011) and utilizing an index of state-level enforceability.
Bishara (2011) develops a set of seven questions about enforceability, each of which is scored out of ten and then weighted by importance to create an index of state-level NCA enforceability over time out of a total possible score of 600. [^11] A score of 600 means that NCAs are very enforceable, and a score of 0 means that NCAs are hardly enforceable at all.
<!-- para 21 -->
Bishara (2011) calculates this index for 1991 and 2011, and Marx (2022) extends the index to cover the years between 1991 and 2014.
This paper newly extends the annual index through 2022 using a catalog of NCA enforceability changes broken down by state (e.g., due to state supreme court rulings or statutory changes) and focusing on changes that affect all workers rather than a subset of occupations (e.g., low-wage workers). [^12] We then normalize the index on a 0 to 1 scale and focus our results on the years 1991-2016, which ensures a reasonable horizon of post-treatment effects. [^13]
<!-- para 22 -->
We observe large and small changes in enforceability in both directions. [^14] However, the majority of our changes in this period are “strengthenings” in NCA enforceability – i.e., changes greater than zero – which reflect the general trend toward stronger NCA enforceability during this period.
The average magnitude of change in this period is 0.09 points in our baseline sample (as defined below), which approximately corresponds to a 9 percentage point change in enforceability.
<!-- para 24 -->
We use patent filings from the USPTO (2016)'s PatentsView project to measure innovation.
Patents are not a perfect measure of innovation – e.g., there are innovations that firms do not patent, either because they are not eligible to be patented or because the firm would prefer to keep the technology as a trade secret.
Nonetheless, like much of the innovation literature, we contend that patents are a useful proxy for broader innovation, and one for which we have detailed disambiguated data for the universe of filings in the US since 1976.
Although NCAs may not be directly relevant to protecting ideas already under patent, they have a material effect on firms' abilities to protect "tacit knowledge" – e.g., the work-in-progress research, follow-on innovations, research methods, negative knowledge, unpatentable byproducts of research, etc., which are generated alongside patent disclosures.
<!-- para 25 -->
For the analysis discussed below, we restrict our sample to granted patents, with an identified filing location within the US, and we take each granted patent's date of application to construct our panel of state-level patenting by year.
We also focus on patents assigned to US corporations. [^15] And we restrict our sample to 1991-2016, as lags due to patent examination mean that post-2016 data are likely to be incomplete. [^16] In addition to information about patenting inventors, the USPTO provides information about assignees (which are typically inventors' affiliated companies), inventor and assignee locations, technology fields, and citations.
For our baseline estimates, we take the location of the assignee as the location of the patent.
However, in Appendix Section D.1 we also replicate our analysis when using inventor location as the patent location and when restricting our sample to patents where the assignee and inventor locations are the same; we find that our results are robust to both alternative specifications.
<!-- para 26 -->
We use unweighted patent counts as our baseline measure of innovative activity to avoid conflating the effect of NCA enforceability on innovation with the effect of NCA enforceability on citation networks/flows.
Nonetheless, in Section 4.2 we also use different citation measures to weight patents by approximations of their value rather than assuming (as we would when focusing on patent counts alone) that all patents are equally valuable or innovative.
Our results are robust to this type of value-weighting.
<!-- para 27 -->
Finally, we use these data to construct a panel for the entire sample period on inventors and assignees linked to each other and to their patents across years.
Doing so yields broad-based insights into inventor-specific mobility across US corporate assignees, capturing how often inventors switch from patenting with one firm to patenting with a different firm.
<!-- para 29 -->
We use financial and operational data from Standard & Poor's Compustat North America database [Compustat (2016)], which provides comprehensive accounting data for publicly-traded firms in the United States.
Compustat covers virtually all firms listed on major US exchanges (NYSE, NASDAQ, and AMEX) as well as many over-the-counter securities, with historical coverage extending back to 1950 for annual data.
Our sample includes all US-incorporated firms reporting in US dollars from 1991-2016, matching our period of analysis for NCA enforceability changes.
Following standard practice in the productivity literature, we exclude financial firms (SIC 6000-6999) and utilities (SIC 4900-4999) due to their distinct regulatory environments and production technologies.
We further restrict our sample to firms with non-missing and positive values for key production inputs (sales, capital stock, labor expenses, and intermediate inputs).
For firms with non-December fiscal year ends, we allocate flow variables proportionally across calendar years to ensure temporal alignment with our state-level NCA enforceability measures, and aggregate to the annual level to match the index of state-level NCA enforceability.
<!-- para 31 -->
We also utilize data on firm entry from the US Census Bureau (2022b) BFS that measure new business applications and formations.
We observe the total count of applications submitted for Employer Identification Numbers by entrepreneurs and corporations for each state. [^17] BFS data are reported quarterly from 2004 to the present.
For the analysis discussed below, we aggregate applications to the annual level to match the index of state-level NCA enforceability.

## methods
<!-- para 2 -->
There are several challenges to estimating the causal impact of NCAs on innovation.
For example, the use of NCAs by firms is likely to be endogenous to their future innovation.
That is, if a firm anticipates that they are likely to have valuable innovations in the future, they may be more incentivized to include NCAs in their employment contracts.
Ignoring this endogeneity could bias estimates of the impact of NCAs on innovation and spuriously suggest a positive relationship.
For this reason, even where direct information on NCA use is available, this information does not identify the causal effect of NCAs on innovation.
<!-- para 3 -->
To deal with this endogeneity, we focus on NCA enforceability rather than actual employment contract terms.
Enforceability itself may be endogenous in the cross-section if a firm's location choice depends on its innovativeness.
For example, highly innovative firms may choose to locate in states with high NCA enforceability, but that would not imply that the high degree of NCA enforceability causes that innovation.
This type of spurious positive correlation in cross-sectional patenting may have contributed to the popular narrative that NCAs support innovation.
<!-- para 4 -->
Instead, we use plausibly exogenous state-level changes in NCA enforceability as our independent variable. [^18] These changes reflect judicial rulings that modify the case law of what is and is not legally enforceable and some statutory changes that directly alter a state’s NCA enforceability, overriding past legislation as well as past case law.
Even in cases where one might worry that legislatures may change NCA policy in response to state-level conditions, Johnson et al. (2023b) show that these changes are not predicted by a range of state-level characteristics.
<!-- para 6 -->
To fix ideas and provide intuition on the setting, we consider a case study before jumping into the analysis across all states.
In March 2004, the Supreme Court of Ohio expanded the circumstances under which NCAs would be enforceable.
Specifically, the Court's decision in Lake Land Employment Group of Akron, LLC v.
Columber overturned the previous Ohio rule that an employee who signed an NCA after the commencement of employment had to be compensated for the agreement to be enforceable, ruling instead that continued employment would constitute adequate consideration to enforce an NCA going forward.
This represented a sudden and substantial increase in the enforceability of Ohio NCAs. [^19] This change in enforceability was both unanticipated and would have meant that Ohio firms could relatively costlessly distribute NCAs to their employees the next day without compensating them.
<!-- para 7 -->
To assess how this change might have affected patenting, Figure 2 compares Ohio patenting before and after the change to that of a synthetic control for Ohio constructed from states in our sample that never had a change in their NCA enforceability. [^20] The green line in the figure documents observed Ohio patenting each year.
The red dots represent the optimal synthetic control when we include all control states in the set of potential controls.
The blue dots represent the average optimal synthetic control from 500 permutations where we randomly omit control states from the set of potential controls; from this, we can also construct standard errors and the 95% confidence intervals shown in the blue bars.
This comparison suggests that Ohio patenting fell relative to the synthetic control after the Lake Land decision in 2004.
When we restrict to firms that only patent in a single state, the effect is even stronger (Appendix Figure 4).

We can verify that this observed drop in Ohio patenting is not driven by industry compositional effects by also examining industry-level effects.
For example, by focusing on a high-tech industry, we can avoid confounds like Ohio's exposure to the transport industry during the Great Recession.
Appendix Figure 5 shows that there is also a sharp negative effect on Ohio patenting in the field of information technology (IT) methods for management following the Lake Land ruling. [^21] It is intuitive that experience with and tacit knowledge around software would be valuable in a fast-moving and frontier field.
And indeed, this comparison shows that Ohio patenting in IT methods fell to an even greater degree than aggregate patenting after the Lake Land decision in 2004.
<!-- para 12 -->
To confirm that it is the Lake Land decision driving these effects and not just some other event that happened around the same time, we can also look directly at in-state inventor mobility in Ohio during this period.
Appendix Figure 6 shows that the number of within-state moves drops sharply below the number of across-state moves in 2004 and afterward.
This is precisely what one would expect an increase in NCA enforceability to achieve, as NCAs generally have within-state geographic scope.
And these results suggest that Lake Land played a significant role in changing the innovative landscape of Ohio by restricting worker mobility.
<!-- para 14 -->
For the estimation across all states in our sample, we adopt a staggered difference-in-differences estimation approach: local projections difference-in-differences (LP-DiD) developed by Dube et al. (2023).
This estimator is similar to other popular estimators like Callaway and Sant'Anna (2021) and Borusyak et al. (2021) and shares their desirable features of, for example, avoiding the negative weights bias under staggered heterogeneous treatment effects of two-way fixed effects models.
However, LP-DiD also permits non-absorbing and non-binary treatments as we have in our setting here. [^22] Our estimating equation is the following:
<!-- para 15 -->
$$
\begin{aligned}y_{i,t+h}-y_{i,t-1}&=\beta_{h}\cdot\mathbb{I}_{i,t}\cdot\Delta X_{i,t}\quad&treatment(change in index)\\ &+\delta_{t+h}-\delta_{t-1}\quad&time effects\\ &+\epsilon_{i,t+h}\quad&for h=-H,...,H,\\ \end{aligned} \tag{1}
$$
<!-- para 16 -->
where i is the state and t is the year.
We restrict the sample to observations that are either (i) clean controls – i.e., not-yet or never treated states; or (ii) clean treatments – i.e., state-years with only one treatment in the past H years and with treatments greater than a threshold c such that  $\mathbb{I}_{i,t} = 1$ if  $|\Delta X_{i,t}| \geq c$. [^23] That is,  $c$ is a cutoff for the absolute value change in the index, above which observations are included in the treatment group and below which observations are dropped from the analysis.
In this specification,  $y_{i,t+h} - y_{i,t-1}$ is the difference in the relevant outcome variable (log patents, log business applications, etc.).  $\Delta X_{i,t}$ is the change in our continuous treatment variable – the normalized state-level index of NCA enforceability. [^24] LP-DiD allows for outcome lags to be included on the right-hand side of the estimating equation.
Although not part of our baseline specification, we include a version of our estimation with these lags in Appendix Section D.2 to show robustness.
Finally, we also control for time-fixed effects,  $\delta$.
For our baseline specification we use  $H = 5$ years and  $c = 15/600$.
We cluster standard errors at state-level and weight each state by its population share in the previous year [US Census Bureau (2022a)]. [^25]
<!-- para 17 -->
Clean treatment and control conditions help ensure the validity of the LP-DiD estimator; they require that a state must be either newly or not-yet treated such that it is not experiencing some ongoing treatment effect from a previous change in order to be included as either a treatment or control observation in the estimation.
With these restrictions, we have 26 total treatments in 1991-2016 for our baseline sample, of which 17 are instances where NCA enforceability increased (“strengthenings”), and 9 are instances where enforceability decreased (“weakenings”). [^26]

<!-- para 12 -->
Next, we consider which patents are the most affected by changes in NCA enforceability.
In theory, an estimated negative effect of increases in NCA enforceability on patent counts could arise even if NCAs simply screen low-value patents out of the system in a socially efficient manner. [^28] To test this theory, we compare how the estimated treatment effect changes across patents of heterogeneous quality or value.
If it were the case that highly enforceable NCAs simply screen low-value patents, we would expect to see the estimated treatment effect shrink as measures of patent value increase.
<!-- para 13 -->
A popular method in the existing literature to approximate patent value is to weight patents by their number of citations.
Neither forward nor backward citations perfectly captures value, but these objects are seen as measuring correlated patent characteristics like impact and novelty.
Forward citations are a common proxy for impact; they measure how many subsequent patents cite the patent in question and therefore are thought to approximate the extent to which a given patent has led to follow-on inventions.
Backward citations are a slightly less common measure, but recent research suggests that they may actually capture technological value more effectively; [^29] they measure how many previous patents were cited by the patent in question, with the intuition that having fewer backward citations indicates a more original invention. [^30]
<!-- para 15 -->
In addition to thinking about patent novelty using citations, we can think about the novelty of entire fields.
Table 2's third and fourth columns show the same analysis as above but now broken down by WIPO field age (i.e., when a field received its first patent application). [^32] We classify a field as a 'young' field if the first patent assigned to this field was issued after 1970. [^33] These results again suggest that it is patents in younger fields that are disproportionately affected by changes in NCA enforceability.
We find that an increase in enforceability of the average size observed in our sample (0.09 points) causes patenting in young fields to fall by as much as 32% after 5 years, compared to 13% for old fields.
<!-- para 16 -->
Another strategy to approximate the value of innovations captured in patents is to consider which patent assignees have ever received VC funding. [^34] The existing literature suggests that VC-funded firms tend to produce highly innovative patents. [^35] Table 2's fifth and sixth columns show the estimated treatment effect of changes in NCA enforceability on patenting separately for firms with VC funding and those without VC funding. [^36] Although the estimated effects on both VC-funded firms' patents (in red) and non-VC-funded firms' patents (in blue) are negative and statistically significant at the 5 year mark, changes in NCA enforceability have a more pronounced effect on the patenting of VC-funded firms – an increase in enforceability of the average size (0.09 points) would be expected to decrease VC-funded firms' patenting by 23% after 5 years.
This finding again suggests that high-value patents are particularly sensitive to changes in NCA policies and contradicts the screening out hypothesis.

While patents provide a useful measure of innovative activity, they capture only a subset of innovation and may not fully reflect real economic impacts.
To complement our patent-based analysis, we examine the effect of NCA enforceability on firm productivity using data from Compustat (2016).
Total factor productivity (TFP) provides a comprehensive measure of firms' ability to generate output from inputs, capturing both patented and unpatented innovations as well as improvements in organizational practices, tacit knowledge, and other efficiency gains.
<!-- para 29 -->
We calculate TFP as total factor revenue productivity (TFPR), following Foster et al. (2008), from a Cobb-Douglas production function with labor, capital, and intermediate inputs:
<!-- para 30 -->
$$
\mathrm{tfpr}_{it}=py_{it}-\alpha_{l}\cdot l_{it}-\alpha_{k}\cdot k_{it}-\alpha_{m}\cdot m_{it}, \tag{2}
$$
<!-- para 31 -->
where  $tfpr_{it}$ is firm-level log TFPR,  $py_{it}$ is log revenue,  $l_{it}$ is log labor input,  $k_{it}$ is log capital input,  $m_{it}$ is log intermediate input, and  $\alpha_{l}, \alpha_{k}$, and  $\alpha_{m}$ are the respective factor shares.
Factor shares are calculated as cost shares at the two-digit SIC industry level to allow for industry-specific production technologies while maintaining sufficient observations for stable estimates.
We then calculate average firm-level TFPR at the state-year level using headquarters locations. [^38]

Before concluding that our headline estimates represent a true innovation effect, we must also consider potential alternative explanations.
In this section, we evaluate the extent to which the estimated negative effect might instead reflect (i) firms substituting away from patents to trade secrets (i.e., decreasing their propensity to patent a given innovation), or (ii) cross-state brain drain of inventors moving out of the treated state.
Neither of these alternative explanations seem to explain our results.
As discussed above, the appendix also provides a number of other checks to show that our results are robust.
Therefore, our results do appear to be driven by true changes in innovation.
<!-- para 41 -->
NCAs may affect not only innovation but also the propensity of firms to patent a given innovation.
That is, if firms view NCAs and patents as substitutes in protecting their inventions and ideas, we might pick up substitution effects alongside innovation effects if firms increase their reliance on trade secrets under stronger NCA enforceability and forego patenting.
Theoretically, such substitution is unlikely to be an issue here.
Discussions with legal scholars have revealed that the risk of not patenting an eligible invention is generally too large for firms to adopt such a strategy, even under fully enforceable NCAs – e.g., if a competitor independently invents and patents your same invention, it obtains the right to exclude you from use of the invention.
And, even short of a competitor patenting your invention, independent discovery and reverse engineering would destroy any right you have to exclude others.
<!-- para 42 -->
Empirically, we can consider the treatment effect of changes in NCA enforceability on the relative frequencies of product and process patents. [^40] Product innovations are hard to protect through trade secrets alone due to the potential for reverse-engineering, so firms are unlikely to choose not to patent eligible product innovations even in the face of high NCA enforceability.
But substitution between trade secrets and process patents may be more of a concern.
Therefore, if firms use trade secrets as a substitute for patents under stronger NCA enforceability, we should see most (if not all) of our effect operating through the process-only patents channel such that the relative frequency of product- and process-only patents changes too.
Our results in Figure 6 show, however, that the mix of product and process patents granted within a state does not change in response to changes in NCA enforceability, such that substitution away from (process) patenting seems unlikely to be generating our results.
This supports the interpretation of our baseline estimates as reflecting a true impact of NCAs on innovation rather than simply capturing the type of ancillary substitution behavior that would show up in this type of product-process patenting comparison. [^41]

So far, we have also estimated the state-level impact of changes in NCA enforceability.
However, in order to confirm the validity of SUTVA and to extrapolate to the expected effects of a national policy (e.g., the FTC's recent ban), we need to account for the potential impact of cross-state spillover effects.
Accounting for certain spillover effects could intensify our estimated net effect.
For example, accounting for how declines in patenting would inhibit follow-on innovation in other states could increase our estimated magnitudes.
However, accounting for other spillover effects could dampen our net effect.
In particular, if workers move states to escape NCAs (following an increase in enforceability in their initial state of residence), they may increase innovation in their destination state.
Marx et al. (2015) document the possibility of brain drain out of high enforceability states when an inventor wants to move firms and then also has to move states to escape the scope of an NCA.
To ensure that our estimated effect is conservative, therefore, we now also control for out-of-state inventor migration effects, which have the potential to dampen our baseline estimated treatment effect. [^42]
<!-- para 49 -->
We can estimate the change in the number of inventors moving out of state that is induced by a change in NCA policy using the following regression equation:
<!-- para 50 -->
$$
M_{o,t+h}-M_{o,t-1}=\theta_{h}\cdot\mathbb{I}_{o,t}\cdot\Delta X_{o,t}+\gamma_{t+h}-\gamma_{t-1}+\varepsilon_{o,t+h}, \tag{3}
$$
<!-- para 51 -->
where  $M_{o,t}$ is the log count of inventors moving out of state o in year t, and everything else is analogous to the specification in Equation 1.
The result of this analysis is shown in Figure 7, where we see no significant out-of-state migration effects following a change in NCA enforceability.
This helps mitigate concerns about SUTVA violations in the empirical approach outlined above and is consistent with existing literature that finds that accounting for individual workers' out-of-state migration does not change some optimal state-level policies, such as income taxation (Mazerov, 2023). [^43]

Having ruled out a number of competing theories for how NCA enforceability could lead to a decline in patenting without true innovation effects, we now turn to assess what channels might explain our baseline results.
Figure 8 summarizes our conceptual framework.
Specifically, we consider three channels through which NCAs may be expected to affect innovation.
First, NCAs might increase innovation due to incumbent innovation incentives.
Just as patents are thought to encourage innovation by allowing firms to better appropriate the returns on R&D expenditures (e.g., by allowing them to charge higher markups on innovative products for a period of time following a novel invention), NCAs may also incentivize incumbent innovation by helping firms protect their confidential information (e.g., trade secrets, tacit knowledge, negative knowledge, etc.) and maintain a competitive advantage over rivals. [^44] Therefore, we would expect to see incumbent firms increasing innovation following an increase in NCA enforceability. [^45] The negative estimated net effect suggests that this channel is outweighed by the other channels, so we discuss this channel less extensively than the others in the sub-sections below.
<!-- para 58 -->
Incumbent firms are not the only source of innovation.
Our second channel,  $\underline{\text{firm entry}}$, is important as well.
Garcia-Macia et al. (2019) estimates, for example, that firm entry accounts for approximately 20% of TFP growth in the US.
But, without empirical analysis, the effect of NCAs on entry is theoretically ambiguous.
On the one hand, NCAs might have a positive effect on firm entry to the extent that they increase the expected profits of a successful entrant (i.e., as with incumbents, by allowing firms to earn super-normal profits on their innovations or by reducing the wages and bargaining power of workers).
However, NCAs could also have a negative effect on entry by imposing barriers to entry.
For example, a worker covered by an NCA is legally prohibited from entering the market as a rival firm.
Moreover, even if a would-be entrepreneur is not covered by an NCA, they may still find it difficult to enter the market if most workers in the local industry are covered by NCAs such that it proves difficult to recruit talent.
Among other things, this paper assesses the net impact of NCAs on this entry channel, which is otherwise uncertain.
<!-- para 59 -->
Third, NCAs may negatively affect productivity and innovation by reducing  $\underline{\text{knowledge diffusion}}$ – i.e., the flow of new ideas and technologies between firms.
This channel impacts the ability of other firms to learn about and improve upon the new technologies produced by rivals. [^46] If workers moving between firms is an important mechanism through which innovations diffuse, then direct limits on employee mobility will hamper this channel of knowledge flows.

Given that our findings cannot be fully explained by reduced firm entry, we now examine labor mobility and associated knowledge diffusion as potential channels through which NCA enforceability affects innovation.
Labor mobility could affect innovation through multiple mechanisms.
For example, when workers can move freely between firms, they carry with them not just their formal knowledge but also tacit knowledge, work practices, and expertise that can spark new innovations at their destination firms.
Mobile workers also create and maintain professional networks that facilitate knowledge exchange even after they have moved.
And the promise of mobility may incentivize workers to maintain high effort and creative engagement, knowing their skills will be valued in the broader labor market. [^50]
<!-- para 77 -->
As shown in Table 4's first column and Figure 11, we find strong evidence when examining inventor mobility directly that increases in NCA enforceability significantly reduce worker movement between firms.
We measure inventor moves from the patent data using disambiguated inventor and assignee names. [^51] For an average-sized increase in enforceability (0.09 points), the share of inventors changing employers drops by 31% after five years.
Table 4's second column and Figure 12 shows that this effect is even more pronounced among inventors at VC-funded firms, where mobility declines by 64% – consistent with our earlier finding that the most innovative segments of the economy are particularly sensitive to NCA policy.
<!-- para 78 -->
This reduction in inventor mobility appears to directly impact the flow of knowledge between firms, as evidenced by changes in patent citation patterns.
Table 4's third column and Figure 13 show that stronger NCA enforceability corresponds to an increase in self-citation by firms (or, equivalently, a decrease in the share of citations to work by other firms).
Following an average-sized increase in NCA enforceability, firms' share of self-citations (i.e., citations to their own prior patents as a share of all total citations) increases by 17%.
This suggests the type of increase in technological isolation that would arise if reduced labor mobility impairs knowledge spillovers across firms. [^52] That is, fewer ideas may flow from other firms when inventors are less able to move.
When inventors are constrained from moving, firms appear to instead build more heavily on their own prior innovations rather than incorporating insights and approaches from across the broader innovative ecosystem. [^53]

## discussion
<!-- para 2 -->
Using these results, we can also think about the effect of hypothetical federal policies that weaken NCA enforceability nationwide.
Our preferred policy counterfactual is one that matches up well with the average treatments observed in our data.
Our reduced-form approach limits our ability to evaluate potential non-linear effects of larger policy changes in NCA enforceability. [^58] We do not claim in this paper that the optimal level of NCA enforceability is zero; rather, we only claim that, on average, the current level of enforceability appears to be too high to maximize innovation.
Therefore, we consider what the effect of a 0.09 point weakening in our normalized index would do to patenting across the country. [^59] We use our baseline  $\beta$ estimate from 5 years after a hypothetical change for this exercise.
Our point estimates suggest that this federal decrease in national enforceability would increase patenting by 14% nationwide.
<!-- para 3 -->
One could take our results a step further and extrapolate linearly to think about what these results might imply for the FTC's recent ban on NCAs.
We heavily caveat such extrapolation.
However, the sign and economically significant magnitude of the predicted effect points to a large potential upside to innovation of such a ban.
Our estimates presented above suggest that, 5 years after such a change, patenting would more than double nationwide.
<!-- para 4 -->
The preceding analysis raises a number of other considerations that are beyond the scope of this paper due to data limitations, but which we hope to explore in future work.
For example, our focus on patented inventions necessarily excludes a potentially significant portion of non-patented innovative activity.
Moreover, this analysis examines the effects of NCA enforceability at the state and national levels, which likely masks significant heterogeneity across workers, firms, and industries, all of which may be insightful into how these channels operate and what the key determinants of knowledge diffusion are.
Although we do not explore these extensions in this paper, they would likely be valuable areas of future research.
<!-- para 6 -->
This paper suggests that NCAs have a significant negative net impact on innovation, in contrast to what is often assumed in policy discussions.
The impact is not only statistically significant but also economically significant: a strengthening from the median to the maximum observed enforceability in our sample (i.e., 0.59 to 0.80 on our normalized index) is associated with a 28% decrease in patenting after 5 years.
For a more modest change in enforceability, our estimates suggest that an increase of 0.09 points in our normalized index (the mean observed change) would decrease patenting by 14% after 5 years.
And these effects become even larger when focusing on high-novelty, high-value patents such as those produced by VC-funded firms.
These innovation losses translate into real economic costs, with average firm-level TFP declining by 6% in response to the same average change.
<!-- para 7 -->
This effect does not seem to be explained by stories about patent screening, changes in firms' propensity to patent, or cross-state brain drain.
Moreover, the effect does not seem to be driven solely by NCAs restricting entry.
Our results show no statistically significant impact on new business applications from changes in NCA enforceability or entrant patenting, and large declines in patenting for incumbent firms.
This result could be consistent with a setting where highly enforceable NCAs might simultaneously introduce positive profitability incentives for entry and negative barriers to entry that partially or entirely cancel each other out overall.
Given this, entry alone cannot explain the large overall negative effect.
Instead, our work here finds significant effects on both incumbent patenting and inventor mobility between firms, with inventor moves declining by 31% in response to stronger NCA enforceability in our sample, and firms becoming more technologically isolated as measured by their citation patterns.
These parallel changes in mobility and knowledge flows suggest that labor mobility plays a critical role in innovation by facilitating knowledge diffusion across firms.
