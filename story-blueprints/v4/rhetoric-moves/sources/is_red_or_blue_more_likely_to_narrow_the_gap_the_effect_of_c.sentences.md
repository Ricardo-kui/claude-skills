# is_red_or_blue_more_likely_to_narrow_the_gap_the_effect_of_c 句子库存

## introduction
<!-- para 2 -->
The issue of income inequality has received growing attention from researchers (Amis et al., [2021](#joms12917-bib-0003); Bapuji et al., [2020](#joms12917-bib-0007); Patel et al., [2021](#joms12917-bib-0084); Suddaby et al., [2018](#joms12917-bib-0097)).
One particularly salient indicator is CEO-employee pay disparity, defined as the extent to which a CEO is paid more than a typical firm employee.
The CEO-employee pay gap refers to how much more a firm pays its CEO versus ordinary employees.
This discrepancy may impede employee morale and commitment (Breza et al., [2018](#joms12917-bib-0011)) as workers at firms with relatively large income inequality can demonstrate higher dissatisfaction and turnover rates (Fredrickson et al., [2010](#joms12917-bib-0039); Pfeffer and Langton, [1993](#joms12917-bib-0086); Siegel and Hambrick, [2005](#joms12917-bib-0095)).
Prior research suggests that firms may determine their pay differences by considering external referents for socially acceptable behaviours (DiMaggio and Powell, [1983](#joms12917-bib-0030); Meyer and Rowan, [1977](#joms12917-bib-0076)).
Meanwhile, employee bargaining power can also affect the extent of such pay disparity.
Relative to low-skilled employees, highly-skilled employees often secure better pay, reducing potential within-firm income inequality (Shin, [2014](#joms12917-bib-0094)).
<!-- para 3 -->
While insightful, prior studies have fallen short of thoroughly examining the role of CEOs in establishing within-firm pay disparity.
This is an important theoretical inadequacy since executives' personal views profoundly affect their decisions and behaviours (Liu et al., [2018](#joms12917-bib-0071); Wowak et al., [2017](#joms12917-bib-0106)).
Upper echelons theory asserts that managers have not only unique skills, but also varying perspectives (Finkelstein et al., [2009](#joms12917-bib-0037); Hambrick and Mason, [1984](#joms12917-bib-0051)).
As Hambrick and Mason ([1984](#joms12917-bib-0051)) maintain, organizational outcomes are likely to be shaped by ‘the values and cognitive bases of powerful actors in the organization’ (p. 193).
As executives' particulars affect how they absorb and interpret information, outline alternatives, and implement eventual decisions, corporate strategies are likely to be altered.
Prior research suggests that CEO attributes such as age (Rajagopalan and Datta, [1996](#joms12917-bib-0088)) and tenure (Miller and Shamsie, [2001](#joms12917-bib-0077)) have strong bearings on firm strategies and outcomes.
In this study, we suggest that CEOs are likely to play a crucial role in pay disparity within firms.
<!-- para 4 -->
Specifically, we contend that CEO political ideology – as reflected in the degree to which a CEO is politically liberal versus conservative – affects his or her motivation to address the issue of within-firm pay gaps.
Researchers have found that CEO political ideology is crucial in influencing firm behaviours including corporate social responsibility (CSR) (Chin et al., [2013](#joms12917-bib-0020); Gupta et al., [2021](#joms12917-bib-0048)), top management team (TMT) pay gaps (Chin and Semadeni, [2017](#joms12917-bib-0019)), and acquisitions (Elnahas and Kim, [2017](#joms12917-bib-0032)).
Nevertheless, it remains unclear how CEO political ideology affects vertical pay disparity within firms.
To fill this void, we ask: how does CEO political ideology affect within-firm pay disparities between a CEO and his or her employees?
Which contextual factors moderate this proposed effect?
<!-- para 5 -->
Our study endeavours to further the research on executive political ideology (Briscoe and Joshi, [2017](#joms12917-bib-0012); Chin et al., [2013](#joms12917-bib-0020); Gupta and Wowak, [2017](#joms12917-bib-0045)) by suggesting that a liberal CEO will be more inclined to lower the pay inequality between himself or herself and regular employees within firms because liberals are more aware of inequality issues and open to social changes.
In contrast, conservative CEOs are less motivated to address vertical pay inequality within firms as they attend less closely to inequality issues and strongly adhere to the existing social order.
Building upon this central argument, we investigate two contingent factors that may strengthen or weaken the main effect including board political ideology and CEO power.
We consider these contingencies as they can alter the extent to which a liberal or conservative CEO is aware of the inequality issue and the degree to which the CEO may act upon his or her influence.
Examining such contextual factors helps validate our core argument.
Results based on a sample of United States public firms support our arguments.
<!-- para 6 -->
Our contributions to the literature are twofold.
First, we contribute to the research on within-firm pay inequality by highlighting CEO political ideology as a novel determinant.
In contrast with prior studies suggesting external factors such as institutional requirements (DiMaggio and Powell, [1983](#joms12917-bib-0030); Meyer and Rowan, [1977](#joms12917-bib-0076)), we contend that CEOs' political ideologies may affect how they perceive the external environment and make important decisions accordingly (Finkelstein et al., [2009](#joms12917-bib-0037)).
In doing so, we seek to demonstrate that executives' personal values matter for organizational inequality as manifested in CEO-employee pay disparity.
Second, our study also enriches the research on executives' political ideology.
Existing research suggests that political ideology can affect firms' acquisition tendencies (Elnahas and Kim, [2017](#joms12917-bib-0032)), social engagement (Chin et al., [2013](#joms12917-bib-0020)), tax sheltering (Francis et al., [2016](#joms12917-bib-0038)), and the susceptibility to external influences (Gupta et al., [2021](#joms12917-bib-0048)).
Meanwhile, a board's political orientation may alter its decisions to keep or dismiss a CEO (Park et al., [2020](#joms12917-bib-0083)).
Here we postulate that whether a CEO is politically liberal or conservative will influence the CEO's motivation to address the within-firm income inequality.
The CEO-employee pay gap has been a highly contentious social issue (Klein, [2019](#joms12917-bib-0063)).
By highlighting the pivotal role of CEO political ideology behind CEO-employee pay disparity, we hope to broaden the discussion scope of this issue.
<!-- para 7 -->
Before proceeding, it is useful to distinguish between our study and that by Gupta and Wowak ([2017](#joms12917-bib-0045)).
In their seminal work, Gupta and Wowak ([2017](#joms12917-bib-0045)) show that conservative boards tend to pay CEOs more and emphasize firm performance in designing CEO compensation.
Our study differs from Gupta and Wowak ([2017](#joms12917-bib-0045)) in two ways.
First, Gupta and Wowak ([2017](#joms12917-bib-0045)) investigate CEO compensation, a crucial issue for corporate governance and firm strategy.
In comparison, we are interested in CEO-employee pay gaps, a notion central to income inequality (Amis et al., [2021](#joms12917-bib-0003); Bapuji et al., [2020](#joms12917-bib-0007)).
Second, Gupta and Wowak ([2017](#joms12917-bib-0045)) consider board political liberalism as their main predictor.
In contrast, we focus on CEO's political ideology.
Since a CEO must work closely with the board in making strategic decisions, our study complements the insights by Gupta and Wowak ([2017](#joms12917-bib-0045)), enriching our understanding of within-firm income inequality.

## theory
<!-- para 3 -->
Income inequality is a salient social issue.
At the society level, some top earners receive much greater income than others, given their jobs and other factors.
Likewise, income inequality exists in industries and firms.
Within a firm, certain individuals are paid more in accordance with their roles and responsibilities.
Nevertheless, the issue of social inequality has widely been acknowledged and become more pronounced in recent decades.
For example, while CEOs at Standard and Poor 500 firms on average earn 347 times more than their typical employees, the CEO-employee pay gap is extremely high in some firms (e.g., 3,566 times at Gap, 3,660 times at Abercrombie and Fitch Co., and 40,668 times at Tesla) (Klein, [2019](#joms12917-bib-0063)).
<!-- para 4 -->
Researchers in several disciplines have been keen to understand this issue.
Economists suggest that such discrepancies reflect differences in individuals' capabilities and job demands.
Given that managers have more valuable skills, they are likely to be paid more than ordinary employees.
Moreover, income inequality could exist because pay disparity can motivate employees to work harder.
Due to the tournament effect (Connelly et al., [2016](#joms12917-bib-0024)), a relatively large income inequality may encourage employees to give their best to firms.
On the other hand, sociologists recognize that individuals occupy varying structural positions in society, and the discrepancy in individual pay manifests individuals' endowment and power (Shin, [2014](#joms12917-bib-0094); Western and Rosenfeld, [2011](#joms12917-bib-0101)).
<!-- para 5 -->
In synthesizing the economic and sociology perspectives, management research contends that income inequality can have both benefits and costs.
While a moderate pay gap may induce individuals to demonstrate more productive work behaviours, an overly large pay disparity can backfire (Ridge et al., [2015](#joms12917-bib-0089)).
For example, employees may feel dissatisfied and even deprived when they see that colleagues are paid much more than they are (Crosby, [1984](#joms12917-bib-0026)).
This can lead to undesired consequences including absenteeism (Bloom and Michel, [2002](#joms12917-bib-0010); Messersmith et al., [2011](#joms12917-bib-0075)) and deteriorating mental health (Christie and Barling, [2010](#joms12917-bib-0021)).
Moreover, large pay gaps could cause negative consumer perceptions and decrease firm sales (Mohan et al., [2018](#joms12917-bib-0079)).
Research has also examined the issue of income inequality at the country level.
As higher levels of inequality imply that resources and opportunities are distributed unevenly among citizens, economic inequality can hamper entrepreneurial initiatives within a country (Patel et al., [2021](#joms12917-bib-0084)).
<!-- para 6 -->
Central to our study is CEO-employee pay disparity, or the extent to which a CEO is paid more than a typical firm employee.
By typical employees, we mean non-executive individuals in a firm, which include middle managers, front-line supervisors, and subordinates.
As the top leader, a CEO is often the highest-paid individual within a firm.
An average of non-top executives' compensation has been used to describe the general pay of a regular employee.
Thus, the ratio of CEO compensation to an ordinary employee's pay can gauge the within-firm pay disparity (Connelly et al., [2016](#joms12917-bib-0024); Faleye et al., [2013](#joms12917-bib-0035); Greckhamer, [2016](#joms12917-bib-0042)).
A relatively large CEO-employee pay gap suggests that a CEO earns much more than a typical employee, while a relatively small pay discrepancy indicates a reduced disparity between a CEO and an ordinary employee.
<!-- para 8 -->
One insight of the upper echelons theory is that managers have distinct views and perspectives (Finkelstein et al., [2009](#joms12917-bib-0037); Hambrick and Mason, [1984](#joms12917-bib-0051)).
That is, executives may exhibit varying orientations to external stimuli, demonstrating different styles in their decision-making (Hambrick and Mason, [1984](#joms12917-bib-0051)).
Specifically, executives' values ‘affect their field of vision, selective perception, and interpretation’ (Hambrick, [2007](#joms12917-bib-0049), p. 337).
Thus, given their personalized views, executives could have different tendencies to notice, interpret, and utilize external information through the lens of their experiences and values, which in turn influences their strategic choices.
<!-- para 9 -->
Within the literature of upper echelons theory, political ideology is a newly developed concept recognizing the political views that an executive personally favours (Briscoe and Joshi, [2017](#joms12917-bib-0012); Chin et al., [2013](#joms12917-bib-0020); Gupta et al., [2018](#joms12917-bib-0047)).
The political psychology research suggests that in countries with two dominant parties such as the United States, individuals' political views can be described along a liberalism-conservativism spectrum (Jost, [2006](#joms12917-bib-0055); Jost et al., [2003b](#joms12917-bib-0058); Jost et al., [2009](#joms12917-bib-0059)).
Among a given cohort of individuals, some may enthusiastically support the direction outlined by the Republican Party, while others appreciate the vision developed by the Democratic Party.
Individuals between two extreme points are neither exceptionally politically liberal nor conservative.
One report indicates that in 2019, 36 percent of respondents in the United States identified themselves as conservatives, 35 percent as moderate, and 25 percent as liberals (Saad, [2020](#joms12917-bib-0090)).
One's political orientation is consequential: 91 percent of Republicans and 86 percent of Democrats would see the opposite party unfavourably (Pew Research Center, [2014](#joms12917-bib-0085)).
<!-- para 10 -->
Political ideology is a pivotal portion of individuals' personal values.
According to England ([1967](#joms12917-bib-0033)), values provide ‘a relatively permanent perceptual framework which shapes and influences the general nature of an individual behavior’ (p. 54).
By this view, values define which actions and decisions are acceptable, right, and imperative.
As part of the values system, political ideology describes an individual's beliefs and attitudes toward politics (Erikson and Tedin, [2008](#joms12917-bib-0034)).
An individual's favoured political view could differ from that of another individual's.
For example, some individuals advocate that governments should be small and more hands-off, while others argue that governments should maintain a broader scope to correct market imperfections.
The extent to which an individual supports the direction pursued by liberals versus conservatives is part of his or her political ideology (Jost, [2006](#joms12917-bib-0055); Jost et al., [2009](#joms12917-bib-0059)).
Such inclinations are stable and enduring across one's life stages (Prior, [2010](#joms12917-bib-0087)).
<!-- para 11 -->
In a recent review, Swigart et al. ([2020](#joms12917-bib-0098)) suggest that political ideology has three interrelated aspects: cognitive, social, and behavioural.
First, the cognitive lens focuses on the values of individuals that vary across a spectrum of value systems such as embracing change versus tradition and emphasizing equality versus hierarchy.
Generally, liberals tend to attend more closely to the issues of social justice, economic inequality, and energy saving.
In contrast, conservatives care more about the free market, individualism, and respect for authority.
Second, the social lens focus on political ideology as a source of identification with a social group.
Hence, by noting that ‘I am liberal-leaning or I belong to the liberal group’, the individuals ‘affiliate themselves with the group whose members they perceive as similar to them but also to differentiate themselves from members of another group’ (Swigart et al., [2020](#joms12917-bib-0098), p. 1067).
Finally, the behavioural lens suggests one's political affiliation in that he or she is likely to ‘donate to, campaign for, and vote for’ a specific party (Swigart et al., [2020](#joms12917-bib-0098), p. 1068).
<!-- para 12 -->
Our study applies the cognitive lens of political ideology to postulate that liberal executives differ from their conservative counterparts in three ways (see Table [I](#joms12917-tbl-0001 "Link to table") for a summary).
First, liberals and conservatives differ in their views on the market.
In contrast with conservatives who believe that the market is mostly efficient, liberals recognize that the market may not be perfect as assumed (Jost et al., [2003a](#joms12917-bib-0057)).
Liberals would therefore ask for intervention, while conservatives generally do not do so.
Second, the focus of attention also varies from liberals to conservatives.
Generally, liberals have a more holistic perspective on firm-society interaction as they believe that any social actors must show sufficient support for stakeholders (e.g., customers and suppliers).
Yet, conservatives tend to have a relatively narrow view while focusing on economic issues.
Greenstein et al. ([2021](#joms12917-bib-0043)) find that political ideology affects individuals' tendencies to contribute to the online knowledge community via editing on Wikipedia.
Specifically, liberals recognize broader socio-political factors (e.g., civil rights and trade deficits), while conservatives largely focus on economic issues (e.g., economic growth and forbidding foreign immigrants).
Finally, liberals and conservatives have different decision styles.
Liberals tend to emphasize less on prevailing norms as well as traditions and are open to new, progressive ideas.
Conversely, conservatives value hierarchy and the existing social orders.
Due to this tendency, conservatives are less likely to embrace change.
This explains why conservatives engage less in innovations such as developing new products (Kashmiri and Mahajan, [2017](#joms12917-bib-0061)) and adopting energy-saving technologies (Gromet et al., [2013](#joms12917-bib-0044)).

<!-- para 14 -->
Our core argument is that liberal CEOs are more likely to address the issue of firm vertical income inequality, as these CEOs are more attentive to social inequality issues and more receptive to progressive ideas such as egalitarianism.
We develop this argument further in the next section.
<!-- para 17 -->
We suggest that CEOs' political ideologies reflect their values through the lens that they use to interpret the pay-disparity situation.
Specifically, the degree to which a CEO is more liberal or conservative influences a firm's CEO-employee pay disparity for two reasons.
First, liberals are more aware of the inequality issue.
While employees have endowments and capabilities for earning greater pay, liberal CEOs would note that the labour market is not optimal (Jost et al., [2003a](#joms12917-bib-0057)).
For example, when employers represent monopsony in the market, few individuals can negotiate with firms effectively.
This inferior bargaining positions results in employees being paid less than their deserved market value.
Additionally, wage adjustment within the labour market is often slow and rigid (Goette et al., [2007](#joms12917-bib-0041)).
Yet inflation and basic life expenditures may increase continuously.
Thus, many employees must live with reduced available income when employers push back requests for wage increases.
<!-- para 18 -->
From the perspective of liberal CEOs, these situations warrant their attention and action.
If one does not act, then employees will eventually receive inadequate treatment.
Ultimately, most employees do not have strong labour market positions as top executives.
However, insufficient compensation and a relatively large income inequality will compromise employees' quality of life.
Indeed, an excessively high level of pay gap could evoke deprivation and envy (Nickerson and Zenger, [2008](#joms12917-bib-0081)).
Liberal CEOs see these consequences as an imperative issue and are likely to conceive of alternatives addressing the potential ramifications.
<!-- para 19 -->
Second, liberals are more open to ideas for social change.
Due to their liberal inclination, these executives are more receptive to progressive thoughts (Jost et al., [2003b](#joms12917-bib-0058), [2009](#joms12917-bib-0059)).
The press and social groups may have advocated for change, but individuals may not respond immediately.
Liberals, however, believe that it is crucial to make changes quickly (Briscoe et al., [2014](#joms12917-bib-0013); McDonnell and Cobb, [2020](#joms12917-bib-0074)).
While implementing such actions may cause short-term turbulence, doing so can make a firm form a more cohesive group and improve its long-term prospects.
On this basis, a liberal CEO is inclined to consider alternatives that are more socially progressive.
The liberal CEO will have a higher tendency to reduce, narrow, and ameliorate income inequality within his or her firm.
<!-- para 20 -->
In contrast, a conservative CEO may behave differently.
On the one hand, the conservative CEO attend less closely to issues of inequality as he or she thinks that the labour market functions efficiently and individuals should work hard to achieve positions they desire to hold with higher rewards (Jost et al., [2003a](#joms12917-bib-0057)).
From this perspective, any additional intervention could impair rather than improve the labour market's functioning.
A conservative CEO would therefore contend that a relatively large pay gap within a firm can motivate employees to work diligently as moving up can significantly improve their welfare (Connelly et al., [2014](#joms12917-bib-0023)).
On the other hand, conservatives are less open to change since they psychologically adhere to the existing social order.
Thus, a conservative CEO is likely to oppose the change as such action disrupts rather than improves the firm operations.
This tendency compels the conservative CEO to shy away from devising or implementing any actions to address the within-firm income inequality issue.
<!-- para 21 -->
Apart from liberals and conservatives, there are CEOs whose orientations are politically moderate.
These executives do not particularly support the views proposed by either the Democratic or Republican Parties, occupying the middle area in the continuum of liberalism-conservativism.
Such CEOs do not see the labour market as highly efficient, nor do they have a clear preference either advocating for or opposing progressive ideas.
Moderate CEOs are, therefore, unlikely to narrow or broaden existing vertical pay gaps in their firms.
They are likely to follow either current practices within their firms or prevailing industry ones.
As Hambrick and Mason ([1984](#joms12917-bib-0051)) maintain, ‘the manager's eventual perception of the situation combines with his/her values to provide the basis for strategic choice’ (p. 195).
We thus propose:
<!-- para 22 -->
Hypothesis 1.CEO political liberalism will be negatively related to a firm's CEO-employee pay disparity.
<!-- para 24 -->
Our first hypothesis contends that liberal CEOs will better recognize inequality issues within their firms and be more motivated to address these matters relative to conservatives.
This argument asserts that political ideology is a fundamental value that shapes a CEO's awareness of, and motivation to, address the potential income inequality within a firm (Jost, [2006](#joms12917-bib-0055); Jost and Amodio, [2012](#joms12917-bib-0056)).
Notably, this reasoning focuses on the degree to which a CEO is politically liberal or conservative, assuming that apart from this factor, all CEOs operate in similar contexts.
In reality, CEOs' operating contexts vary considerably, which affects the extent to which they can follow their views in constructing within-firm pay disparity.
To gain more insight, we examine contingent factors.
Specifically, we anticipate contingencies that strengthen (weaken) a CEO's awareness of the potential income inequality issue should amplify (dampen) the proposed main effect.
Alternatively, contextual factors that magnify (attenuate) a CEO's capacity to address the inequality issue should strengthen (weaken) the main effect.
We accordingly consider two contingencies: (1) board political liberalism and (2) CEO power.
As a board has opportunities to work with a CEO, the board's political orientation (Gupta and Wowak, [2017](#joms12917-bib-0045); McDonnell and Cobb, [2020](#joms12917-bib-0074)) could alter a CEO's awareness of the inequality issue.
Similarly, a CEO's power (Combs et al., [2007](#joms12917-bib-0022); Finkelstein, [1992](#joms12917-bib-0036)) may shape the CEO's capacity to put his or her own idea into practice.
These two factors collectively denote the latitude of action a CEO has (Hambrick and Finkelstein, [1987](#joms12917-bib-0050); Li and Tang, [2010](#joms12917-bib-0069)) in exercising his or her ideologically-evoked thinking, improving our understanding of how CEO political liberalism affects within-firm income inequality.
<!-- para 26 -->
Board political liberalism describes the overall political orientation of a board leaning toward liberalism versus conservativism.
Boards of directors are part of the corporate elite (Finkelstein et al., [2009](#joms12917-bib-0037)), and their decisions are influenced by directors' personal dispositions (Westphal and Zajac, [2013](#joms12917-bib-0104)).
The board plays a crucial role in regulating CEO effects, given its monitoring and advising functions (Johnson et al., [1993](#joms12917-bib-0054)).
Meanwhile, informal relationships between a board and a CEO can also affect a firm's strategic decisions (Westphal, [1999](#joms12917-bib-0102)).
<!-- para 27 -->
The extent to which a board is politically liberal could alter the effect of CEO political liberalism, as a liberal board would attend more closely to egalitarianism and social justice issues.
That is, a liberal board is likely to increase a liberal CEO's awareness of recognizing the importance of egalitarianism within a firm.
For example, a liberal board may formally discuss the issue of inequality, which can create opportunities for a liberal CEO to share his or her view to address the income inequality issue in a firm.
Meanwhile, a liberal board could informally ask a CEO to express his or her perspective on the inequality issue and ask if there is anything the firm could do in response.
Under this condition, a liberal CEO will be more aware of the issue of income inequality.
As research documents, the counselling from the board can be instrumental for improving a CEO's decisions (McDonald et al., [2008](#joms12917-bib-0073)).
In our context, this means that the ‘ideological fit’ between a liberal CEO and a liberal board may expand the CEO's awareness of firm income inequality.
<!-- para 28 -->
Conversely, a liberal CEO may be less likely to address the CEO-employee pay disparity when the board is conservative.
Conservatives believe that market mechanisms are fair and just (Jost et al., [2003a](#joms12917-bib-0057)).
As the market rate accurately reflects one's contributions, any income inequality reflects relative merits and capabilities.
Thus, a conservative board will be unlikely to recognize and notice the potential limitations of the market.
Under this condition, a liberal CEO's awareness of the inequality issue would be contained.
Even if the liberal CEO may attempt to discuss this issue, a conservative board may respond that the existing compensation scheme in the firm is sound and well.
For the firm's sake, it is the best to stay on the existing course.
In other words, the ‘ideological misfit’ between a CEO and a board can operate to compromise the liberal CEO's capacity to narrow income inequality within a firm.
<!-- para 29 -->
Hypothesis 2.Board political liberalism will strengthen the negative relationship between CEO political liberalism and CEO-employee pay disparity.
<!-- para 31 -->
As another contingency, CEO power could moderate the effect of CEO political liberalism.
By CEO power, we mean the amount of influence a CEO has over a firm.
While CEOs are the top leaders of their firms, their actual influence is not identical across all firms (Daily and Johnson, [1997](#joms12917-bib-0027); Finkelstein, [1992](#joms12917-bib-0036)).
In some firms, CEOs exert a higher level of influence on their firms by serving as the chairman of the board (Zajac and Westphal, [1996](#joms12917-bib-0108)).
In other firms, the CEOs' actual impact would also be magnified when they appoint independent board directors (Westphal and Zajac, [1995](#joms12917-bib-0103)).
<!-- para 32 -->
We postulate that CEO power may alter the effect of CEO political liberalism on CEO-employee pay disparity as greater power increases a CEO's capacity to address the issue of within-firm pay disparity.
In a typical firm, some influential actors can form coalitions to counteract a CEO's proposal.
If certain organizational members or external entities (e.g., shareholders) do not fully agree with a CEO's idea, the CEO with sufficient power will not be deterred from implementing these plans as the holding of substantial power allows the CEO to pursue the direction he or she personally likes (Haynes and Hillman, [2010](#joms12917-bib-0053)).
Tang et al. ([2011](#joms12917-bib-0099)) demonstrate that greater power enables CEOs to depart from existing firm courses and industry norms.
Hence, a solid power base enables CEOs to push back while leading their firms in the desired direction.
Accordingly, a liberal CEO with much power will have a greater capacity to address the income inequality issue in the firm.
Similarly, a conservative CEO with considerable power can also use his or her influence to maintain or even enlarge the pay disparity within the firm.
<!-- para 33 -->
Alternatively, with limited power, a CEO, whether liberal or conservative, may be less able to manage the issue of within-firm pay inequality based on his or her view.
For example, a liberal CEO with less power must accommodate the opinions expressed by various internal and external stakeholders, as these influential actors may not actively support the CEO's plan.
These entities could draw on credible external sources to question the CEO's proposal, or even force the CEO to leave (Shen and Cannella, [2002](#joms12917-bib-0093)).
Under this circumstance, a liberal CEO with limited power will be less able to follow his or her preferences in managing the issue of within-firm income inequality.
For this CEO, it seems more reasonable not to push the envelope too hard.
Likewise, a conservative CEO with little power may not be able to construct the within firm vertical pay gap by following his or her view.
The holding of limited power means that the conservative CEO must be more receptive to the opinions of other entities (e.g., buyers) in his or her decisions.
Ultimately, with limited power, both liberal and conservative CEOs' capacity to manage within-firm pay disparity will be constrained.
Hence,
<!-- para 34 -->
Hypothesis 3.CEO power will strengthen the negative relationship between CEO political liberalism and CEO-employee pay disparity.

## methods
<!-- para 2 -->
We test our hypotheses using a sample of public firms operating within the United States from 2003 to 2014.
First, we gathered our sample using the firms listed on the *Fortune* 500 during the year 2002.
Our sample was reduced to 341 firms after removing firms not headquartered in the US and those subsequently acquired by other firms.
Second, we utilized Execucomp, firm annual reports, and proxy statements to identify CEOs appointed by our sample firms.
This led to a total of 570 CEOs.
Third, we used individual political donations data to develop our measures of CEO, board, and organizational political liberalism following prior research (details will follow).
Compustat and Riskmetrics provided the information for generating control variables.
After discarding observations with incomplete information, we had a sample of 193 firms with 871 firm-year observations for analysis.
A sizeable portion of these firms was dropped due to incomplete information on total staffing expenditures, which is essential for constructing our measure of CEO-employee pay disparity (more will follow).
A series of *t* -tests suggest that no significant differences exist between the firms dropped and those kept in terms of firm age (t = 0.35, p = 0.72), firm performance (t = 0.87, p = 0.38), CEO political liberalism (t = 0.95, p = 0.33), board political liberalism (t = −0.34, p = 0.72), or CEO power (t = 0.76, p = 0.44).
As will be discussed, we examined this issue using the Heckman model.
<!-- para 4 -->
Our dependent variable is CEO-employee pay disparity.
To measure this variable, we first obtained CEO compensation information (item ‘TDC1’ in Execucomp), which comprises CEO total compensation including both short- and long-term compensation.
A typical employee's compensation was calculated by subtracting all TMT compensation from a firm's annual staff expenses (item ‘xlr’ in Compustat) and divided by the firm's total employment.
As a discretionary item ‘xlr’ documents major employee-related expenses including salaries, wages, pension costs, and other employee benefits.
This item has been used by prior research (Ballester et al., [2002](#joms12917-bib-0004); Conte and Kruse, [1991](#joms12917-bib-0025); Shin, [2014](#joms12917-bib-0094)).
The variable of CEO-employee pay disparity was measured as follows:
<!-- para 5 -->

Higher values suggest a greater pay gap between a CEO and a typical employee in a firm in a given year.
A natural log-transformation was applied to reduce the skewness of the distribution.
In our data firms with relatively high CEO-employee pay disparity include Bank of America, Capital One, and Navient.
<!-- para 7 -->
Our primary predictor is CEO political liberalism referring to an executive's political view along the liberalism-conservatism dimension (Jost et al., [2009](#joms12917-bib-0059)).
In US politics the Republican Party represents a conservative viewpoint, while the Democratic Party signifies a liberal perspective.
Following Chin et al. ([2013](#joms12917-bib-0020)), we used executives' personal political donations during a 10 year period *before* taking a CEO position to develop the measure of CEO political liberalism.
Pre-CEO personal donation history ensures that these gifts reflect an executive's personal preferences rather than a firm's objectives.
The Federal Election Commission (FEC) is a rich and comprehensive source for understanding an individual's political orientation because any donations exceeding US $200 must be reported by law.
When making donations, individuals are asked to provide personal details including name, address, occupation, employer, gift amount, gift date, and recipient name.
<!-- para 8 -->
Consistent with Chin et al. ([2013](#joms12917-bib-0020)), we used the following four indicators to measure CEO political liberalism: (1) number of donations to the Democratic Party divided by the total number of donations to both the Republican and Democratic Parties, (2) dollar amount of donations to the Democratic Party divided by the total dollar amount of donations to both parties, (3) number of distinct years with donations to the Democratic Party divided by the total number of years with donations to either party, and (4) number of distinct donation recipients in the Democratic Party divided by the total number of donation recipients to either party.
In constructing these indicators, we added 0.1 to all numerators and 0.2 to all denominators following Chin et al. ([2013](#joms12917-bib-0020)).
For example, if a CEO made 10 donations to the two parties among which eight were to the Democratic Party, then first indicator would be documented as 0.79 \[= (8 + 0.1)/(10 + 0.2)\].
According to Chin et al. ([2013](#joms12917-bib-0020)), doing so can account for the possibility that some executives did not make donations to political parties.
As these individuals have no clear preferences for either liberalism or conservatism, they can be viewed as politically moderate.<sup>[</sup>[<sup>1</sup>](#joms12917-note-0018_123 "Link to note")<sup>]</sup>
<!-- para 9 -->
We developed a computer program to download political donations data from the Center for Responsive Politics ([https://www.opensecrets.org/](https://www.opensecrets.org/)).
The original data were verified using a CEO's work and personal information (e.g., employer name and address).
Records with unreliable match results (e.g., inconsistent middle names or unrelated occupations) were discarded.
In total, the CEOs in our sample firms made 2,701 donations worth more than US $4 million to 494 unique candidates.
Exploratory factor analyses indicate that these four indicators were loaded on a single factor (Cronbach's α = 0.90).
An average of the four indicators forms the measure of *CEO political liberalism*.
This indicator ranges from 0 and 1, with higher values suggesting that a CEO is more liberal.
<!-- para 11 -->
While the measurement of executive political liberalism has been developed (e.g., Chin et al., [2013](#joms12917-bib-0020); Chin and Semadeni, [2017](#joms12917-bib-0019); Gupta and Wowak, [2017](#joms12917-bib-0045)), it is useful to validate the measure.
Here we offer two validation tests.
First, we examine whether or not our CEO political liberalism measure is consistent with the CEO's publicly reported political orientation.
To this end, we consulted the Notable Names Database ([https://www.nndb.com/](https://www.nndb.com/)) to understand CEOs' political orientations.
A dummy variable was coded to indicate whether or not a CEO was oriented toward the Democratic Party (1 = yes, 0 = no).
Regression analyses suggest that our CEO political liberalism indicator is positively related to the measure coded from the Notable Names Database (β = 0.52, p < 0.01).
Second, we collected a CEO's political donations after he or she was appointed as the top leader of a firm.
If an executive's political orientation truly reflects his or her ideal view as manifested in the dimension of liberalism–conservatism, then a CEO donation orientation *before* the appointment should be positively correlated with his or her donation patterns *after* the CEO appointment.
Correlational analyses suggest that the measurement of pre-CEO assignment political liberalism is positively correlated with that of post-CEO assignment political liberalism (r = 0.59, p < 0.01).
These validation tests strengthen confidence in our measurement.
<!-- para 13 -->
We have suggested that two contingencies may alter the effect of CEO political liberalism.
One is *board political liberalism*.
We measure this variable by following Gupta and Wowak ([2017](#joms12917-bib-0045)) in using the following indicators: (1) the number of a director's personal donations to the Democratic Party divided by the total number of donations to both the Republican and Democratic Parties, (2) the dollar amount of donations to the Democratic Party divided by the total dollar amount of donations to both parties, (3) number of distinct years with donations to the Democratic Party divided by the total number of years with donations to either party, and (4) the number of distinct donation recipients in the Democratic Party divided by the total number of donation recipients to either party.
We consulted Riskmetrics and firm proxy statements to identify a firm's board members.
As with our CEO political liberalism measurement, we used donations made by directors within the 10-year span before they held board positions.
We identified 3,742 unique directors who made a total of 113,402 donations.
An average of these four indicators leads to our measure of board political liberalism (α = 0.93).
<!-- para 14 -->
*CEO power* is the other contingency.
Following Kang et al. ([2021](#joms12917-bib-0060)), we measured this variable using: (1) CEO duality, (2) CEO tenure, (3) CEO ownership, (4) proportion of inside directors, and (5) proportion of firm ownership by institutional investors.
All else being equal, a CEO can have more influence on a firm when he/she also holds the board chair title, when he/she is long-tenured, when he/she has a considerable proportion of firm ownership, and when she/she appoints many inside directors.
Conversely, the presence of a high level of ownership held by institutional investors implies that a CEO's decision autonomy will be constrained.
Thus, we reverse-coded the level of ownership held by institutional investors.
After generating the above indicators, we standardized them within our sample on a yearly basis and aggregated them to form the measure of CEO power (α = 0.76).
As a sensitivity analysis, we used an alternative measure devised by Krause et al. ([2016](#joms12917-bib-0065)) including (1) CEO duality, (2) proportion of inside directors, and (3) institutional ownership, and obtained corroborative results.
<!-- para 16 -->
A firm's CEO-employee pay disparity could be affected by other factors apart from the CEO's political orientation.
One is norms and shared beliefs within an industry.
Institutional theorists note that these beliefs define appropriate firm behaviours (DiMaggio and Powell, [1983](#joms12917-bib-0030); Meyer and Rowan, [1977](#joms12917-bib-0076)).
To account for this effect, we included *industry CEO* - *employee pay disparity* in our models.
To this end, we obtained information regarding the pay disparity between a CEO and an average employee for all firms within an industry from Compustat while excluding the focal firm from the calculation.
The industry was defined at the two-digit SIC level.
<!-- para 17 -->
Second, we controlled for several firm-level factors that may be correlated with a firm's pay gap.
The extent of organizational political liberalism can exert an influence as the presence of many liberal employees may ask for pay egalitarianism.
In contrast, conservative employees may be more willing to tolerate larger pay discrepancies between themselves and a CEO.
Thus, we constructed the variable of *organizational political liberalism* following the procedure suggested by Gupta et al. ([2017](#joms12917-bib-0046)).
Using company names, we downloaded employee contribution data from the Center for Responsive Politics.
After removing donations by the top management team and directors, we had 82,176 records of donations made by 50,949 unique employee donors with a total amount of US $12,360,514.
We used this information to construct the measure of organizational political liberalism.
<!-- para 18 -->
Third, a firm's diversification could affect the pay gap between a CEO and his or her employees.
If larger business scope makes a CEO's job more demanding (Hambrick et al., [2005](#joms12917-bib-0052)), then a firm with more diversified areas would pay the CEO more, exhibiting greater CEO-employee pay disparity.
We measured *firm diversification* using Palepu's ([1985](#joms12917-bib-0082)) entropy index.
This variable was constructed using the Computat segment database.
Similarly, firms may pay employees more when workers exhibit superior productivity.
We controlled for this effect by including *employee productivity* measured by firm sales per employee (logarithm) (Datta et al., [2005](#joms12917-bib-0028)).
Also, *firm performance* could be pertinent because good operational results may allow CEOs to ask for greater pay (Bell and Van Reenen, [2013](#joms12917-bib-0008)).
We controlled for this effect by using return on assets (ROA).
<!-- para 19 -->
Fourth, CEO compensation design can be relevant.
In the business world, some CEOs are compensated mainly with cash and bonuses, while others are paid primarily via performance-based factors (e.g., stock ownership and options).
If a compensation scheme emphasizes short-term performance, then a CEO will be less likely to address within-firm pay inequality, given the immediate performance pressure.
We included *CEO long-term pay* as a covariate measured by the proportion of long-term compensation (e.g., stock ownership) out of total pay.
Specifically, we subtracted short-term pay including cash and bonus (items ‘salary’ and ‘bonus’ in Execucomp) from a CEO's total compensation (‘tdc1’) and then scaled it by total compensation.
Similarly, CEO tenure could be pertinent because long-tenured CEOs may be less open to changes relative to short-tenured CEOs (Musteen et al., [2006](#joms12917-bib-0080)).
We controlled for this tendency by considering *CEO tenure*.
A CEO's gender may also make a difference since females tend to pay greater attention to inequality issues than males (Schwartz and Rubel-Lifschitz, [2009](#joms12917-bib-0091)).
A binary variable of *female CEO* (1 = female, 0 = male) was accordingly included.
Likewise, CEOs approaching retirement age may be motivated to lower within-firm pay disparity to improve their reputations.
Thus, we included *CEO age* in the models.
Similarly, CEOs hired from outside the firm and/or CEOs with higher education levels may have more valuable skills (Brockman et al., [2016](#joms12917-bib-0014)).
These CEOs could have greater bargaining power, increasing CEO-employee pay disparities. *Outsider CEO* is a binary variable coded as 1 for externally hired CEOs. *CEO education* is a continuous variable measuring a CEO's education level (1 = high school, 2 = Bachelor's, 3 = Master's, and 4 = Ph.D.).
<!-- para 20 -->
Fifth, employees supported by strong unions have greater bargaining power (Shin, [2014](#joms12917-bib-0094)), reducing within-firm income inequality. *Unionized workers* were measured as the proportion of workers joining a union.
This information was from Bureau of Labor Statistics.
Furthermore, firms covered extensively by financial analysts may be less likely to demonstrate excessively high within-firm income inequality since financial analysts represent a strong external governance force (Aguilera et al., [2015](#joms12917-bib-0001)). *Analyst coverage* was measured as the number of financial analysts following a firm in a year.
The Institutional Brokers Estimate System (IBES) provided the needed information.
We log-transformed this variable since the distribution of this variable is right-skewed.
Meanwhile, firm market performance may be pertinent because satisfactory market performance can prompt shareholders to bear a relatively higher CEO-employee pay disparity.
We measured *market return* by a firm's market performance over the past 12 months (logarithm).
The information was from the Center for Research in Security Prices (CRSP).
In parallel, employees with more valuable job skills could have more bargaining power to negotiate better pay.<sup>[</sup>[<sup>2</sup>](#joms12917-note-0019_142 "Link to note")<sup>]</sup> To account for this effect, we created the variable of *employee patent ratio*.
This variable was measured by the number of firm patents scaled by employment (industry-adjusted).
In their study, Kogan et al. ([2017](#joms12917-bib-0064)) examine the economic impact of innovation by developing a comprehensive database for US firms' patenting.
We accessed firm patent data from Professor Noah Stoffman's website.
<!-- para 21 -->
Finally, as our sample firms are from ten unique sectors, we had nine industry dummies including primary metals (two-digit SIC 33), depository institutions (60), non-depository credit institutions (61), security and commodity brokers (62), insurance carriers (63), insurance agents (64), real estate (65), holding and other investment offices (67), and business services (73).
Because our study period is from 2003 to 2014, we had a host of year dummies to control for the potential periodical effect.
<!-- para 23 -->
We seek to examine the association between CEO political liberalism and CEO-employee pay disparity.
In testing this relationship, we must consider two issues.
First, a firm's decision to report total staffing expenses may not be exogenous.
If certain unobservable variables prompted firms to report this expenditure and this propensity was not controlled for, then one's conclusions could be biased.
We addressed this issue by employing Heckman's two-stage model.
Following Certo et al. ([2016](#joms12917-bib-0016)), we established a probit model examining a firm's total staff expenditures reporting during a given year (1 if yes and 0 if not).
These predictors include *CEO political liberalism*, *board political liberalism*, *CEO power*, and *firm size*.
Our first-stage model included two instrumental variables (Larcker and Rusticus, [2010](#joms12917-bib-0067)): (1) *democratic state* (1 if a firm was headquartered in a Democratic Party-oriented state, and 0 if otherwise), and (2) *the proportion of firms within an industry reporting total staff expenditures*.
The former is considered an instrumental variable because the overall atmosphere in more liberal states can induce firms to be more transparent.
Moreover, firms operating within an industry where the implicit norms include reporting staffing expenditures may feel compelled to follow suit (DiMaggio and Powell, [1983](#joms12917-bib-0030)).
Since these two variables are strongly correlated with a firm's reporting of total staffing expenditures but are not highly correlated with within-firm income inequality, they meet the conditions for instrumental variables.
We consulted Gallup to recognize whether a state is more oriented toward the Democratic Party.
We used all our sample firms in constructing the probit model.
The Appendix summarizes the results.
Based on these results, we calculated the *Inverse Mills ratio* and included it as a control during the second stage.
<!-- para 24 -->
Second, our dataset is an unbalanced panel with a set of firms repeatedly observed over time.
To analyse this kind of data, we used the generalized estimating equations (GEEs) model.
GEEs have the advantage of both controlling for unobserved differences across firms and accounting for intertemporal correlations among outcomes within firms (Ballinger, [2004](#joms12917-bib-0005); Liang and Zeger, [1986](#joms12917-bib-0070)).
Given these advantages, GEE has been used widely by researchers (Sine et al., [2006](#joms12917-bib-0096); Wowak et al., [2011](#joms12917-bib-0105)).
For all models we specified a Gaussian distribution identifying the link and exchangeable correlation structures using the firm set as the grouping variable.
All predictors were lagged for one year.

Hypothesis [^1] contends that liberal CEOs are more likely to address vertical within-firm pay disparity as shown in smaller CEO-employee pay gaps relative to either politically conservative or moderate CEOs.
According to Model 2 of Table [III](#joms12917-tbl-0003 "Link to table"), the estimate of *CEO political liberalism* is negative and significant (β = −2.555, p < 0.01).
The Wald test indicates that this coefficient is significant (χ <sup>2</sup> = 5.18, p < 0.05).
We interpret the coefficient as follows: a one-standard-deviation increase in CEO political liberalism will lower the CEO-employee pay gap by 17.8 percent (−2.555 × 0.25/3.57 = −0.178).
Given the effect, Hypothesis [^1] is supported.
<!-- para 8 -->
Hypothesis [^2] and [^3] examine the contingencies altering the CEO political liberalism effect.
Specifically, Hypothesis [^2] contends that board political liberalism will strengthen the effect of CEO political liberalism on a firm's CEO-employee pay disparity.
Model 3 of Table [III](#joms12917-tbl-0003 "Link to table") shows that the interaction of *CEO political liberalism* and *board political liberalism* is negative (β = −7.359, p < 0.05).
This coefficient means that when board political liberalism is at a low level, an increase of one standard deviation in CEO political liberalism will lower a firm's CEO-employee pay gap by 12.8 percent.
However, when board political liberalism is at a high level, the same amount of increase in CEO political liberalism will reduce a firm's CEO-employee pay gap by 25.5 percent.
We graphed the effects in Figure [1](#joms12917-fig-0001).
As shown, while CEO political liberalism is negatively related to a firm's pay disparity for both low and high board political liberalism, the slope is steeper in the group of high board political liberalism.
Hypothesis [^2] is thus supported.
<!-- para 11 -->
Finally, Hypothesis [^3] argues that CEO power will magnify the effect of CEO political liberalism on a firm's CEO-employee pay disparity.
According to Model 4, the interaction of *CEO political liberalism* and *CEO power* is negative and significant (β = −0.759, p < 0.05).
This estimate means that when CEO power is at a low level, an increase of one standard deviation in CEO political liberalism will reduce a firm's CEO-employee pay gap by 7.7 percent.
Yet when CEO power is at a high level, the same amount of increase in CEO political liberalism will lower the CEO-employee pay gap by 10.2 percent.
We plotted the moderating effect in Figure [2](#joms12917-fig-0002).
As can be seen, with limited power, a liberal CEO will have a reduced capacity to manage pay disparity as manifested in the more flatting slope.
Yet with more power, a liberal CEO will be more able to lower the CEO-employee pay gap as shown in the steeper slope.
Hypothesis [^3] is accordingly supported.<sup>[</sup>[<sup>3</sup>](#joms12917-note-0020_223 "Link to note")<sup>]</sup>

Aside from our main results, we performed several post-hoc analyses to ensure that our findings are robust including (1) alternative CEO-employee pay disparities measures, (2) the potential endogeneity issue, (3) performance implications of CEO-employee pay disparities, and (4) CEO and average employee compensation as separate dependent variables.
<!-- para 17 -->
In our main results, we used the ratio of CEO pay to an ordinary employee's pay to measure within-firm income inequality.
While we have addressed the potential selectivity issue of reporting total staffing expenditures, some may conjecture that this measurement remains noisy.
If certain unobservable industry or firm factors prompt firms to pay their CEOs or employees in a particular way, then our measurement could be biased.
We address this issue by developing two alternative measures: (1) industry-adjusted CEO-employee pay disparity, and (2) size-adjusted CEO-employee pay gap.
The former is an indicator accounting for the general tendency toward vertical income disparity within an industry.
To this end, we subtracted the industry average CEO-employee pay disparity from the observed values and then divided it by the standard deviation (i.e., mean = 0 and S.D = 1) to create the measurement.
The second indicator is to control for firm size.
Specifically, we regressed the observed CEO-employee pay disparity on firm size (employment; logarithm) to remove the potential size effect.
We then calculated the residual and scaled it by the standard deviation.
We used these two measures to replace the original CEO-employee pay disparity and re-estimated the models.
In results not shown here to conserve space, we find that our conclusions with these two alternative pay disparity measures are consistent.
<!-- para 19 -->
This study aims to examine the relationship between CEO political liberalism and firm pay disparity.
While we find supportive results in our main findings, it is crucial to consider the endogeneity issue here.
As Semadeni et al. ([2014](#joms12917-bib-0092)) maintain, models with endogenous variables could be biased.
In our study, one likely endogenous variable is CEO political liberalism.
If certain variables prompt firms to appoint liberal or conservative individuals as CEOs and this propensity is not adequately controlled for, then our conclusions could be tentative.
Following Chatterjee and Hambrick ([2007](#joms12917-bib-0017), pp. 370–1), we addressed this issue via two steps.
We first regressed observed *CEO political liberalism* on our control variables along with *CEO democratic oriented home state*, a binary variable indicating whether a CEO grew up in areas that are more toward the Democratic Party.
This variable was included as an instrumental variable (IV) as formative years affect one's cognition and behavioural tendency (Kish-Gephart and Campbell, [2015](#joms12917-bib-0062); Yonker, [2017](#joms12917-bib-0107)).
Hence, executives may recognize themselves as liberals rather than conservatives if they had their early life in a liberal oriented local environment.
To code this variable, we manually searched CEO biographies from Wikipedia, Notable Names Databases, and company proxy statements to identify the regions in which CEOs grew up.
In cases where the precise states cannot be clearly identified, we used the institution from which a CEO received a Bachelor's degree or lower (e.g., high school) as a proxy.
We find that our CEOs were originated from 46 states, with the top three being Massachusetts (10.5 percent) New York (10.25 percent) and California (8 percent).
We consulted Gallup to determine if a CEO's home state is democratic oriented or otherwise (1 = yes, 0 = no).
Using this instrumental variable, we re-estimated our models.
In results not shown here, we find that *CEO democratic oriented home state* is positively related to observed *CEO political liberalism* (β = 0.098, p < 0.05).
This suggests that CEOs who had their formative years in a democratic state tend to identify themselves as liberals rather than conservatives.
Second, based on the regression results, we calculated the predicted values *endogeneity control* and included it as an additional variable in the second-stage model.
As shown in Table [IV](#joms12917-tbl-0004 "Link to table"), *endogeneity control* is insignificant, and the results with this variable remain consistent.
Hence, the endogeneity issue seems not a major concern.

In our main results, the dependent variable is within-firm income inequality.
Measurement of this variable involves comparing the compensation of a CEO versus a typical firm employee.
To gain greater insight, we regressed *CEO pay* and *average employee pay* on *CEO political liberalism* in separate equations together with other variables as noted earlier.
As summarized in Table [VI](#joms12917-tbl-0006 "Link to table"), *CEO political liberalism* is not significantly related to *CEO pay* (β = −0.718, n.s.), but *CEO political liberalism* is positively related to average employee's pay (β = 0.532, p < 0.05).
Hence, the main channel for liberal CEOs to affect vertical income inequality is through improving employee pay rather than lowering CEO compensation.
Taken together, these analyses suggest that CEO political liberalism plays a central role in affecting within-firm income inequality.

## discussion
<!-- para 2 -->
How does CEO political ideology affect CEO-employee pay disparity within a firm?
Which contingencies moderate this proposed effect?
While the issue of income inequality has received increasing attention (Amis et al., [2021](#joms12917-bib-0003); Bapuji and Neville, [2015](#joms12917-bib-0006); McCall and Percheski, [2010](#joms12917-bib-0072); Suddaby et al., [2018](#joms12917-bib-0097)), scholars largely consider external factors in their investigations.
Whether internal factors prompt a firm to demonstrate higher or lower CEO-employee pay disparity remains unclear.
In this study, we draw on research regarding executive political ideology (Briscoe et al., [2014](#joms12917-bib-0013); Chin et al., [2013](#joms12917-bib-0020); Gupta et al., [2018](#joms12917-bib-0047)) to posit that a CEO's political orientation as manifested on the liberalism-conservatism spectrum affects the CEO-employee pay gap.
We contend that because liberals are more aware of the potential inequality and open to social changes, CEOs with a liberal orientation are more inclined to undertake proactive actions narrowing the pay gap within their firms.
By assembling a sample of US public firms, we find support for our arguments.
Our results also suggest that the effect of CEO political liberalism on within-firm pay disparity is strengthened when the board is more liberal and when the CEO has more power.
