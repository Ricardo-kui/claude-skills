## introduction

<!-- para 4 -->
Product recalls have become more frequent over the past two decades.
According to the National Highway Traffic Safety Administration (NHTSA), tens of millions of automobiles are recalled every year.
In the pharmaceutical industry, the Food and Drug Administration (FDA) recalled more than 300 products in 2020. [^1] In 2018, NHTSA reported 914 recalls affecting 10.7% of registered vehicles in the United States, a 131% increase compared to 1999 (NHTSA, 2019; Statista, 2020).
There have been more than 22000 automobile recalls in the United States in the last forty years. [^2] Thus, product recall announcements are frequent, and a new recall announcement typically occurs in a recall environment constituted by recalls of other firms in the industry.
<!-- para 5 -->
The firm that announces a product recall usually incurs a market penalty in the form of a decline in its stock price (e.g., Chen et al., 2009).
However, the impact of any specific recall announcement on a firm's performance is uncertain at the time of the announcement (Liu et al., 2016).
For organizational failures such as product recalls with an uncertain impact, decision-makers could evaluate the impact against the context of similar announcements by other firms in the industry (Desai, 2014).
Consequently, we investigate whether the recall environment influences the market penalty for a specific recall announcement.
<!-- para 6 -->
We employ salience theory (e.g., Bordalo et al., 2012, 2013a, b) to argue that the market penalty for a recall announcement is affected by the noticeability of previous recalls made by other firms in the industry.
The noticeability of the set of recalls that precede the focal recall is conceptualized as recall environment intensity.
Insights from salience theory, interviews, and a survey of investors are used to develop a measure of recall environment intensity.
Then, we theorize and test its impact on the stock price of a firm that announces a new recall.
<!-- para 7 -->
The market penalty from a negative event such as a product recall depends on the degree to which the announcement surprises investors.
We contend that when recall environment intensity is high, investors might find the new recall announcement less surprising.
Consequently, the market penalty for a newly announced recall in a high intensity recall environment could be lower than in a low intensity recall environment.
We further propose that the heterogeneity in this effect depends on product characteristics.
Particularly, the impact of high intensity recall environment in mitigating the market penalty for a new recall announcement will be stronger for newer products and products of stronger reputation for reliability.
These hypotheses are tested in the context of the U.S. automobile industry.
<!-- para 8 -->
We contribute to the product recall literature (see Table 1) by: (a) conceptualizing recall environment intensity, (b) developing a measure for recall environment intensity, (c) theorizing how recall environment intensity influences the stock penalty from recall announcements, (d) hypothesizing the heterogeneity in this effect, and (e) empirically demonstrating the role of recall environment intensity in the context of the U.S. automobile industry.
Our results provide a more nuanced view of the impact of a recall announcement on a firm's stock price and build on research from Borah and Tellis (2016) and Mukherjee et al., (2022).
Borah and Tellis (2016) show how recalls of a brand can affect sales of rival brands and firm value, but they do not account for the impact of the recall environment.
Mukherjee et al., (2022) show that recalls occur in clusters and the stock market penalty for a recall announcement depends on its position within a recall cluster.
In contrast, we focus on the characteristics of the recalls that precede the focal recall (and constitute recall environment intensity), not its position in the cluster.
Furthermore, we empirically show that recall environment intensity impacts stock market penalty even after accounting for the focal recall's position in a cluster.
Our research, thus, complements Mukherjee et al., (2022) by indicating why the stock market penalty to similar recall announcements could vary even if their positions in the recall cluster are identical.
<!-- para 9 -->
The rest of the article is organized as follows.
First, we present the conceptual framework and develop hypotheses.
Thereafter, in the research methodology section we explain the conceptualization of recall environment intensity, the empirical context, how the estimation sample was obtained, how the dependent and independent variables are measured, the empirical strategy to address the potentially endogenous nature of recall environment intensity, and the econometric model to estimate the effects of interest.
Next, we present the results, the theoretical and managerial implications, and directions for future research.

Decision contexts are often complex and uncertain.
Given the multiplicity of sources of information, decision makers may lack resources to scan for information and may not pay attention to all events equally (Ocasio, 1997).
Thus, the salience of events in the external environment influences decisions (Desai, 2014).
Salience is the phenomenon where decision makers' attention is selectively directed to one part of the information environment, which then influences the decision (Taylor & Thompson, 1982).
For example, risk propensity could vary with the salience of the payoff information in the decision context such that decision makers are likely to be risk seeking when gains are salient but risk averse when losses are salient (Bordalo et al., 2012).
Likewise, consumers are less likely to be price sensitive when the quality of a product is salient and more likely to be price sensitive when the price of the product is salient (Bordalo et al., 2013a).
In short, uncertainty and limited attention resources lead decision makers to rely on salient contextual information to make decisions (Levitt & March, 1988).
<!-- para 7 -->
From the perspective of investors, there is uncertainty regarding how product recalls affect a firm's cash flow (Liu et al., 2016).
Hence, relevant contextual information could play a role in influencing their response to events like product recalls.
As per salience theory, the noticeability or salience of such contextual information shapes the response to the focal event.
Thus, for investors, the salience of the recall environment (consisting of recalls by other firms in the industry) could become relevant in assessing and responding to new recall announcements.
We explain this next.
<!-- para 9 -->
According to salience theory, uncertainty and limited attention span may induce investors to focus on relevant contextual information from the industry as they evaluate and respond to firm specific events (Peng & Xiong, 2006; Ramos et al., 2020).
For instance, Cooper et al., (2001) found that during the internet bubble firms that changed to dotcom names earned significant abnormal returns when they announced their name changes even when they did not change their strategies.
Based on these arguments regarding reliance on industry-level information, we propose that investors' response to a recall announcement is likely to be shaped by the relevant industry context of recent recalls announced by other firms in the industry.
We call this context the recall environment.
Thus, we posit that recall environment intensity could influence investor response to recall announcements.
Investigating this impact is likely to improve our understanding of the stock penalty from a new recall announcement.
To pursue this investigation, we conceptualize and measure the recall environment intensity construct.

<!-- para 14 -->
Recall environment intensity refers to the salience of the recall environment.
Accordingly, we use insights from the literature on information salience to conceptualize the recall environment intensity construct.
Strategic actors' decisions in uncertain environments depend on the extent to which the context can draw their limited attention through its salience or noticeability (Peng & Xiong, 2020; Taylor & Thompson, 1982).
Thus, to delineate the recall environment intensity (salience) construct, we focus on identifying features of product recall announcements by other firms in the industry that make them more noticeable to investors.
<!-- para 15 -->
Epstein and Segal (2000) argue that social and political issues that receive the most media coverage are likely to be salient to political actors.
Likewise, Collins and Cooper (2012) posit that salience of judicial cases depends on their media coverage.
Therefore, we suggest that media coverage of a recall announcement could enhance its salience.
Veer and Rank (2012) demonstrate that mortality salience is likely to be effective in drawing consumers' attention.
Bordalo et al., (2013a) show that consumers are likely to be quality conscious in situations where product quality is salient.
Following these lines of reasoning, we posit that product recalls that involve major hazards (e.g., mortality or serious accidents) are likely to disproportionately draw investors' attention as consumer safety issues and lack of quality are salient in such recalls.
Furthermore, we argue that product recalls that affect large number of customers, i.e., recalls that impact numerous products (recall size) and multiple product categories (recall scope), are likely to draw investors' attention (Eilert et al., 2017).
We also argue that the salience of recall announcements will likely fade over time as other subsequent events draw investors' attention (Borah & Tellis, 2016).
Hence, recalls that are temporally proximate to and precede the focal recall could be more salient, with their salience waning over time.
<!-- para 16 -->
To summarize, we propose that recall environment intensity is constituted of five aspects of the recalls that make up the recall environment, i.e., media coverage or publicity, degree of hazard, recall size, recall scope, and recency.
We next discuss how we validated these dimensions of recall environment intensity.
<!-- para 18 -->
We validate the five dimensions of recall environment intensity in two ways: by conducting personal interviews of individuals who are actively involved in stock market investments and by conducting an MTurk survey of individuals involved in investing.
Apart from validating the dimensions of recall environment intensity, the interviews and survey also allowed us to assess whether the recall environment influences investors.
<!-- para 20 -->
We conducted the interviews as per the template provided in Web Appendix W1.
In total, we interviewed twenty individuals from a diverse set of companies who are actively involved in investing in the stock market.
The experience of respondents in our sample ranged from one year to more than five years.
On average, the respondents have three years or more of relevant experience.
The summary of investors' responses is provided in Table W1 of Web Appendix W1.
<!-- para 21 -->
All respondents were aware of product recalls and were able to provide examples of recent recalls that caught their attention, suggesting that recall environment as a decision context might influence investors' stock market investment decisions.
All of them considered news and social media as the major source of information about product recalls.
Recalls that are frequently discussed in the media are more likely to get their attention because of repeated exposure.
For example, a respondent noted that social media plays a major role in what people retain in their minds and recalls are no exception.
She further noted that repeated mention of a recall on different media is likely to make them more noticeable.
Another respondent mentioned that if a recall is shown on media repeatedly, she will pay attention to it even if it does not involve any serious issue.
These insights validate our conceptualization of media coverage as a potential dimension of the recall environment intensity construct.
<!-- para 22 -->
Respondents opined that they are more likely to notice higher magnitude recalls.
For example, one respondent noted that the size of the recall reflects its significance and will definitely play a role in getting her attention.
Another respondent stated that if the recall involves thousands of units, she will consider it a major recall, and it will draw her attention relatively more than a recall which involves far fewer units.
Yet another respondent mentioned that a news magazine she subscribes to covers major recalls.
This confirms our conceptualization of recall size and recall scope as potential dimensions of the recall environment intensity construct.
<!-- para 23 -->
Furthermore, all respondents indicated that recalls that involve significant safety issues would get their attention, which in turn confirms degree of hazard involved in a recall as another dimension of recall environment intensity.
For example, one respondent indicated that recalls that affect the well-being of consumers are likely to draw attention, while another respondent mentioned that recalls that involve health related issues or fatalities are likely to draw attention.
Since respondents consider hazard associated with recalls as important regardless of the underlying defects, the notion of the extent to which recalls are similar is likely to get reflected in our measure of recall environment intensity.
Finally, respondents agreed that they may give higher weightage to recent recalls, thereby validating recency as another dimension of recall environment intensity.
<!-- para 24 -->
Next, we asked respondents about their potential decision to invest in the stocks of a company that issues a recall announcement.
In general, respondents did appear reluctant to invest in the stocks of such a company.
However, some respondents said that they would investigate the recall before arriving at a decision, and if it did not appear to be a big issue, they may not desist from investing in the firm's stock.
This insight highlights the uncertainty faced by investors at the time a recall is announced.
When asked whether information about recent recalls by other firms in the same industry will affect their decision to invest in the stocks of the company that issued a recall, all respondents agreed that it will.
However, some respondents mentioned that they might withdraw their investment from the industry while a few mentioned that they would like to investigate the cause of recalls before making a decision.
<!-- para 25 -->
Financial analysts we interviewed had similar views as well.
They get information on recalls from media sources such as CNBC and research newsletters (which contain information from different sources including media) that they receive daily.
The scale of recall and hazard involved were considered significant factors in the noticeability of recalls.
Smaller recalls are likely to be ignored.
Furthermore, they indicated that recalls by other firms might influence how they assess a new recall announcement.
As such they suggested that if recalls are very frequent, any focal recall may get discounted when they consider investing or disinvesting in the stock.
In summary, our interviews suggest that the scale and scope of the recall, the level of hazard that motivates the recall, and the media publicity all influence their salience.
<!-- para 27 -->
To further validate recall environment intensity, we conducted a small survey study of investors to verify: (a) the five dimensions of product recall environment intensity and (b) whether investors are likely to pay attention to the recall environment and make their investment decisions using that information (see Web Appendix W2).
The study results support the validity of these dimensions and the relevance of the recall environment to investors as they assess investments in the stocks of a company that issues a recall announcement.
<!-- para 28 -->
In summary, the interviews and MTurk survey of investors support the conceptualization of the recall environment intensity construct.
Furthermore, the responses support the idea that investors pay attention to recent recalls in the same industry and the premise that these recalls influence their views regarding investment in the firm that announces a recall.
We next conceptualize how recall environment intensity influences investors' reactions to new recall announcements.
<!-- para 30 -->
The impact of an event on stock prices depends on whether it violates investors' expectations (e.g., Graffin et al., 2016; Sorescu et al., 2017).
Positive unexpected events typically boost stock prices while negative unexpected events like recalls extract a market penalty—they attenuate stock prices.
However, there is uncertainty associated with the extent of the impact of a product recall announcement on a firm's performance (Liu et al., 2016).
According to salience theory, investors could pay more attention to contextually relevant information to evaluate firm-level events when the impact of the event is uncertain (Peng & Xiong, 2006; Ramos et al., 2020).
In this regard, prior research discusses how relevant actions by other firms in the industry provide information that affects how the focal firm's actions are interpreted by investors (Bahadir et al., 2015; Chatterjee et al., 2005).
For example, when other firms have announced new products, a subsequent announcement by a firm about its new products is less likely to surprise investors (Warren & Sorescu, 2017).
Hence, information from prior recalls in the industry could condition the degree to which investors are surprised by a new recall announcement.
<!-- para 31 -->
As noted, as per salience theory this conditioning will vary with recall environment intensity.
When prior recall announcements in the industry are more salient, a new recall announcement might not surprise investors much.
In this situation, investors may conclude that recalls in the industry are commonplace, and hence, consumers are less likely to switch their loyalty from the products affected by the new recall announcement.
If so, the cash flow impact and the market penalty from the recall announcement will be attenuated.
Therefore, the intensity of the recall environment can have an expectation-adjustment effect where new recall announcements may be seen as less egregious, limiting their potential negative impact on the firm's stock price.
Hence, we propose:
<!-- para 32 -->
H1 High (vs. low) recall environment intensity attenuates the stock market penalty of product recall announcements.
<!-- para 34 -->
We next assess the boundary conditions of the impact of recall environment intensity on the stock market penalty for a new recall announcement—the recalled product's age and the reliability reputation of the recalled brand (hereafter, reliability reputation).
These two moderators were selected because they operate through the expectation-adjustment mechanism.
The key assumption under salience theory that explains the impact of recall environment intensity on stock penalty is that given limited attention span and uncertainty, salient information from industry-level recalls could become relevant in assessing a firm's new recall announcement.
But in the presence of directly relevant and more accessible product-related information that helps explain the recall and its outcomes, investors may be less likely to rely on relatively more distal industry-level information to adjust their expectancy of the recall and its outcomes, notwithstanding the salience of such information (Higgins, 1996).
However, if directly relevant product-related information does not justify or explain the recall, investors might rely on industry-level information more to explain the recall.
We explain the moderating effects in detail next.
<!-- para 36 -->
Product age is defined as the time difference between the product's introduction and its recall announcement.
We posit that product age could moderate the impact of recall environment intensity on stock penalty for a new recall announcement by changing its impact on expectation-adjustment.
<!-- para 37 -->
For a recall announcement to be issued, problems should be identified by customers, the firm, or public agencies, and this may take time.
Furthermore, some defects are likely to manifest later than sooner in the life of a product.
For example, design-related defects are likely to get discovered later than manufacturing-related defects (Muralidharan et al., 2022).
Sometimes it takes time to identify the root cause of the problem even if a problem is reported or recognized.
In short, identifying and reporting a product defect and determining the locus of the problem takes time, and hence investors are more likely to expect recall announcements for products that have been in the market longer.
Furthermore, it is likely that recalls of older products could lead investors to infer that the firm has an aging product line, and it needs to invest significant resources to launch newer products, thereby reducing uncertainty regarding recall outcomes.
<!-- para 38 -->
To summarize, due to uncertainty and limited attention, the impact of a firm's product recall announcement on its stock price could be evaluated against recall environment intensity.
However, contextual information plays a role depending on the availability of other relevant proximate information that helps resolve the uncertainty related to the event and its outcomes.
Thus, salient contextual information (recall environment intensity) may play less of a role in adjusting investor expectations in the presence of direct information like product age that allows adjustment.

<!-- para 42 -->
Reliability reputation is the subjective belief the market holds about the quality and dependability of the brand (Rhee & Haunschild, 2006).
We posit that reliability reputation could moderate the impact of recall environment intensity on stock penalty from a new recall announcement by changing its impact on expectation-adjustment.
That is, the reliability reputation of brands as more proximate information will influence the expectation regarding recalls.
Reputation influences expectations from a firm because it captures what a firm is known for among stakeholders (Lange et al., 2011).
Good reputation also builds a reservoir of goodwill and serves as a positive signal to buyers.
As a source of goodwill, reliability reputation affects investors' expectation of and sensitivity to product recalls (Rhee & Haunschild, 2006).
Brands with weak reliability reputation are likely to be seen as more susceptible to defects that lead to recalls and their recall announcements deemed less surprising compared to brands with strong reliability reputation.
Thus, when a product of low reliability reputation is recalled, the low reliability reputation serves as the relevant proximate information that justifies the recall (i.e., investors are not surprised), and therefore the contextual information (recall environment intensity) may not play a substantial role in adjusting investor expectations.
For instance, the suspect reliability reputation of Rivian (the company that manufactures electric vehicles) is important proximate information that may influence stock penalty when its recall is announced even when recalls in the auto industry are common (Bushey, 2022), and thus recall environment intensity presumably high.
In contrast, when the recalled product is of higher reliability reputation, recall environment intensity may play a greater role in adjusting investor expectations because proximate information about the product (reputation) does not justify the recall.
The reservoir of goodwill built up by past reputation of the brand also provides it with a stronger shield against blame when the blame is shared by other firms, which is the case when recall environment intensity increases.
These lines of reasoning imply that the attenuation of market penalty for recall announcements in high intensity recall environment increases when the reliability reputation of products increases.
Hence, we expect:
<!-- para 43 -->
H3 High (vs. low) recall environment intensity attenuates the stock market penalty of recall announcements more as the recalled product's reliability reputation increases.

## methods
<!-- para 2 -->
In this section, (1) we explain the empirical context, (2) develop the measure of recall environment intensity i.e., our key independent variable, (3) describe how we arrived at the estimation sample, (4) describe how the abnormal returns i.e., our dependent variable, were calculated (5) provide measures of moderator and control variables, (6) discuss endogeneity issues and the steps taken to identify the effects, and (7) present the empirical specification used to analyze the data.
<!-- para 4 -->
We collected product recall data for the automotive industry from the NHTSA database, and stock market data from the Center for Research in Security Prices (CRSP).
The automotive industry is relevant and conducive for our research as: (1) it is the largest manufacturing sector, responsible for 3% of America's GDP, and is the leading job multiplier in the United States (American Automotive Policy Council, 2018) and (2) NHTSA regularly collects vehicle safety recall information.
We obtained data on the six largest automakers (Toyota, Honda, Nissan, General Motors, Ford, and Chrysler) from January 2007 to December 2019, as they account for about 90% of the U.S. motor vehicle market and the highest number of recalls.
<!-- para 5 -->
Following prior research, we included a vehicle safety recall in the sample only if it was large in proportion to the size of the firm that announced the recall (Gao et al., 2015; Jarrell & Peltzman, 1985).
To illustrate, we considered recalls involving 50,000 or more vehicles for Toyota; 40,000 or more vehicles for General Motors, Ford, and Chrysler; 30,000 or more vehicles for Honda; or 20,000 or more vehicles for Nissan. [^3] Based on this criterion, our starting sample consisted of 526 vehicle recalls.
Following Gao et al., (2015), we identified the recall announcement date as the earliest date that the recall was reported on any online or printed media in the U.S. We dropped 29 recalls for which no exact announcement date could be identified.
This reduced our sample to 497 recall announcements, which we used to construct the recall environment intensity measure.
<!-- para 7 -->
To measure recall environment intensity for the focal recall, we consider recalls announced by other firms between the focal firm's current recall announcement and its immediate last recall announcement.
For example, to capture recall environment intensity for a firm's recall on May 15, 2021, we first obtain the date of the immediate last recall by the firm, say April 20, 2021.
We consider the period between the two consecutive recalls by the focal firm as the estimation period.
We did not consider recalls on or before the day of the immediate last recall announced by the focal firm to measure recall environment intensity as that information would already be captured in the stock price.
For instance, in our example above, the information from any recall on or before April 20, 2021, would be fully absorbed in a firm's stock price on that date and no longer new to investors.
<!-- para 8 -->
In our conceptualization, recall environment consists of five aspects that make recalls salient: size, scope, publicity, hazard, and recency of recalls.
Our measure of recall environment intensity combines the five aspects of recall environment into one metric in the spirit of cumulative measures of concentration index in the competition literature (Bikker & Haaf, 2002).
An advantage of such a measure is that it is sensitive to changes in the dimensions of the recall environment, and therefore, it allows us to assess the combined effect of such changes on stock prices.
For each day i in the estimation period, we measure Size $_{i}$ as the number of products recalled by other firms on that day, Segment $_{i}$ as the number of product segments involved in the recalls on that day, [^4] Publicity $_{i}$ as the number of U.S. newspaper citations of recalls on that day, and Hazard $_{i}$ as the number of recalls due to major hazards.
Since recent recalls could contribute more to recall environment intensity, we obtain the weighted sum of Size $_{i}$, Scope $_{i}$, Publicity $_{i}$, and Hazard $_{i}$ using recency (i.e., the time difference in days between day i and the focal firm's recall day) as the weighting factor.

Next, we explain how we arrived at the sample that we used to estimate the effects of interest.
We took the following two steps to arrive at our estimation sample.
First, we dropped 99 recalls issued by firms not listed on the US stock market during the time of the study. [^6] Second, consistent with existing research we recognize that observations or recalls consisting of confounding events or recalls by other firms in their vicinity must be excluded (Gao et al., 2015).
It is important to exclude observations with confounding events such as earnings surprises, product introductions, mergers and acquisitions, and changes in top management as these events impact returns.
Thus, if we do not exclude these observations then it is difficult to ascertain if the change in the abnormal returns for the focal recalls is due to these events or due to the recall environment (Chen et al., 2009).
In fact, because of information leakage concerns, similar argument applies to the confounding events that are present in the vicinity of the recalls but happen after the recalls are announced (McWilliams & Siegel, 1997).
<!-- para 20 -->
Additionally, it is important to exclude recalls by other firms in the industry announced very close to the focal recall announcement because existing research has demonstrated that recall announcements by one firm can change the stock prices of other firms in the industry through spillover effect (Borah & Tellis, 2016).
The spillover effect occurs because recalls generate negative chatter not only about the firm that issues a recall but about other firms in the industry and causes a negative effect on their returns as well (Borah & Tellis, 2016).
If we do not account for the negative spillover effect, it could confound the detection of the positive recall environment effect.
The most feasible way to do so would be to exclude observations with recalls very close in proximity to the focal recall.
Accordingly, we decided to exclude those observations that have recalls issued by other firms very close in time to the focal recall.
While this may raise concern that observations that face a stronger recall environment are removed, of note is that this would for the same reason constitute a stronger test of the theory while preventing the confounding because of the spillover effect.
Because of information leakage concerns, we also exclude observations that have recalls by other firms announced in close temporal proximity after the focal recall.
<!-- para 21 -->
To exclude observations with confounding events we need to identify observations with and without clean windows around them.
However, it is not readily apparent what the size of these windows should be as the literature does not provide clear direction in this regard.
For example, Hoffer et al., (1988) defined observations free of confounding events using a [-5,5] windows, whereas Bhagwat et al., (2020) used a [-7,7] window, Meznar et al., (1994) used a [-3,3] window, and Boyd et al. (2010) and Markides (1992) used a [-2,2] windows.
The difficulty of deciding on the right window in our case is further complicated due to the potential presence of spillover effects.
The spillover effect is negative, and the recall environment effect is positive.
Thus, the presence of spillover effect could make it difficult to detect the effects.
Therefore, if we reduce the size of the window, we may not be able to detect the effects of interest.
On the other hand, if we increase the size of the window, we may end up reducing the number of observations in our estimation sample which again may make it difficult to detect the effects.
Accordingly, we follow Boyd et al. (2010) and Markides (1992) and consider [-2,2] window around recalls.
However, we assess the robustness of our estimates using [-1,1], [-3,3] and [-4,4] windows in the robustness section.

Our final sample used to test the hypotheses consists of 148 automobile safety recalls and is comparable to those used in previous studies.
For example, Gao et al. (2015) had a sample of 110 recalls, Eilert et al., (2017) 73 recalls, Germann et al., (2014) 55 recalls, and Hoffer et al., (1988) 66 recalls.

We operationalized the recalled product's age (AGE) as the difference between the recall announcement year and the year the vehicle entered the market averaged across all model-years involved in a recall campaign.
Consistent with prior research, the reliability reputation of the brand that issued a recall (REPUT) was measured using a five-point scale of "trouble indexes" reported in Consumer Reports (Rhee & Haunschild, 2006).
Specifically, the mean of the "overall problem rate" scores of each model for the most recent three years of ownership was calculated. [^7] We then create an overall problem rate score for a brand in a year by averaging the overall problem rate scores of all car models of the brand in that year.
Since reputation is built up over the years before the recall happens, an average score over the three years prior to the year in which focal recall happens is used (Rhee & Haunschild, 2006).
Higher scores indicate a higher reliability reputation.
When multiple brands are recalled, we use the average reputation of brands recalled by a firm as the measure of reliability reputation.
<!-- para 36 -->
Based on prior research, we identified three sets of potential determinants of stock market response to a focal recall: (1) its direct costs, (2) its indirect costs (e.g., a damage to firm image, sales, and market share), and (3) firm characteristics that may affect the firm's ability to dampen the volatility of cash flow after a recall announcement (Chen et al., 2009; Eilert et al., 2017; Germann, et al., 2014; Jarrell & Peltzman, 1985).
<!-- para 37 -->
Direct costs associated with product recalls are likely to depend on recall size (RECSIZE) and problem severity (PROBSEV) (e.g., Jarrell & Peltzman, 1985; Rupp, 2004).
The higher the recall size, the higher could be the costs of repairing the affected vehicles, and the higher the negative stock market reactions (Jarrell & Peltzman, 1985).
Likewise, higher problem severity (defined as the seriousness of the consequences of the product defect) could increase the cost of repairing vehicles as owners may be more motivated to take their vehicles for repair (Hoffer et al., 1994; Rupp, 2004).
We measure the focal recall's size (RECSIZE) using the number of vehicles affected (Gao et al., 2015), and problem severity (PROBSEV) using reports of incidents, i.e., crash, fire, injury, or fatality (Eilert et al., 2017).
Specifically, we control for problem severity using a dummy variable that equals 1 if crashes, fires, injuries, and fatalities are reported in the recall announcement, and 0 otherwise.
<!-- para 38 -->
Indirect costs associated with product recalls are likely to depend on the firm's recall strategy (RECSTRA), the negative publicity around the recall (PUBLICITY), and the extent of brand diversification (DIVERSE).
A firm could adopt a proactive recall strategy (identify the defects and issue a voluntary recall without involvement of a government agency) or a passive recall strategy (delay the recall until compelled to issue a recall) (Chen et al., 2009).
A passive recall strategy could damage a firm's reputation for social responsibility and result in higher market penalty.
In contrast, a proactive recall strategy signals the firm's social responsibility to its stakeholders, which in turn could lead to lower market penalty (Dawar & Pillutla, 2000).
Following Chen et al., (2009), we measure recall strategy (RECSTRA) using a dummy variable which equals 1 if an investigation is opened by NHTSA before the recall announcement (i.e., passive recall strategy), and 0 otherwise (i.e., proactive recall strategy).
<!-- para 39 -->
Likewise, higher negative publicity around a recall can increase market penalties (Rhee & Haunschild, 2006) and make it difficult to repair the damaged reputation (Rhee & Valdez, 2009).
We measure the extent of negative publicity around a focal recall (PUBLICITY) as the count of recall announcement citations in U.S. newspapers (Borah & Tellis, 2016).
Highly diversified brands, consisting of a variety of products under the same brand, might have to incur higher indirect costs to respond to product recalls because of potential spillover of negative perceptions to products not affected by the recall (Van Heerde et al., 2007).
Consistent with prior research, brand diversification (DIVERSE) was measured using the principal component of the number of product lines and the range of engine capacities (the highest capacity minus the lowest capacity) of the car models produced by a brand in a year (Rhee & Haunschild, 2006). [^8]
<!-- para 40 -->
Firm-level factors that are likely to affect firms' ability to preserve a healthy cash flow after a recall announcement and therefore determine investors' stock market reactions include product recalls in the recent past, size, debt, and profitability.
The number and size of product recalls issued by a focal firm in the recent past (PASTREC) could enable firms to develop the managerial competence to deal with them.
However, recent recalls may also constrain the resources a firm has at its disposal to administer a recall (Eilert et al., 2017).
In either case, past recall magnitude could determine a firm's ability to respond to the focal recall in a timely and cost-efficient manner, and hence, to protect cash flow after a recall announcement.
We operationalize past recall magnitude (PASTREC) as the principal component of the total number of vehicles recalled and the total number of recalls issued by the focal firm in the preceding year (Eilert et al., 2017; Gao et al., 2015). [^9] In addition, we also account for the time interval between the focal firm's recall and the immediate last recall by the focal firm (TI). [^10] Furthermore, firm size, debt, and profitability are factors that may impact a firm's vulnerability to the negative financial consequences of a product recall, and hence, affect the firm's ability to preserve a healthy cash flow after the recall announcement.

As noted earlier, recall environment intensity has been conceptualized as consisting of five dimensions: recall size, scope/segments, publicity, hazard, and recency of prevailing recalls in the industry.
Among these dimensions, the recency dimension depends on when a focal firm announces its recall.
For example, the intensity of the recall environment will be higher if the recall announcement is closer in time to prior announcements than otherwise.
Past research suggests that firms may time their recall announcements based on recall-related or firm-related factors, and these factors can also be associated with investors' stock market reactions (Eilert et al., 2017; Hora et al., 2011).
Thus, to the extent the focal firm strategically times its recall, the recall environment intensity faced by a firm might be endogenous.
Hence, it is essential to account for factors which influence the timing of firms' recall announcements and their stock market performance so that recall announcements that face high intensity recall environments are comparable to recall announcements that encounter low intensity recall environments.
Otherwise, statistical tests comparing the stock market response of recall announcements in high-intensity recall environments with that of recall announcements in low-intensity recall environments could result in biased estimates.
<!-- para 46 -->
To mitigate this endogeneity concern, we use an empirical strategy motivated to mimic a randomized experiment.
Specifically, we employ Coarsened Exact Matching (CEM) to match recalls facing high recall environment intensity to those facing low recall environment intensity using matching covariates that are likely to correlate with firms' recall timing (and therefore the recall environment intensity they are likely to face) and are also likely to correlate with post-recall stock returns. [^11]  The endogeneity of recall environment intensity is mitigated under the assumption that conditional on the matching covariates, recalls are equally likely to face a low-intensity or a high-intensity recall environment.
<!-- para 47 -->
We identify the matching covariates for CEM based on prior research related to timing of product recall announcements.
According to Eilert et al., (2017), problem severity could determine a firm's ability and motivation to investigate a product defect, and therefore, managers' decision of when to announce a recall.
This relationship, in turn, is moderated by the reliability reputation and diversification of the recalled brand, along with the past recall magnitude of the firm issuing the recall.
Furthermore, a firm's decision to announce a recall is also likely to depend on its recall strategy, i.e., whether a recall is issued in a passive or proactive manner (Hora et al., 2011).
Thus, problem severity, reliability reputation, brand diversification, past recall magnitude, and recall strategy are likely to correlate with firms' timing of recall announcements, and therefore, with the intensity of the recall environment they likely encounter.
Moreover, as discussed earlier, these variables may also affect the stock market response to recalls. [^12] Thus, matching the recalls in the high-intensity environment to the recalls in the low-intensity environment based on their characteristics such as problem severity, reliability reputation, brand diversification, past recall magnitude, and recall strategy would ensure that the recalls in both environments are comparable in terms of factors that are likely to influence the timing of their announcements and rule out the endogeneity problem.

We calculate the abnormal stock returns accruing from a recall announcement through an event study.
Following existing research, we determine abnormal returns relevant for our analyses by considering different event windows and testing for the statistical significance of the corresponding abnormal returns (Gao et al., 2015).
The abnormal returns which are statistically significant are considered the dependent variable in our analyses (Eilert et al., 2017).
Particularly, we focused on four event windows: the event day (i.e., [0, 0]), the day before the event day (i.e., [-1, -1]), the day after the event day (i.e., [1, 1]), and finally both the event day and its following day (i.e., [0, 1]).
As shown in Table 5, the average abnormal return on the day before the event day is insignificant (CAR [-1, -1] = 0.0019, p > 0.10).
The average abnormal returns on the day after the event day (CAR [1, 1] = 0.0021, p > 0.10) and the cumulative abnormal return over the event day and the day after are also insignificant (CAR [0, 1] = 0.0002, p > 0.10).
However, the average abnormal return on the event day (CAR [0, 0] = -0.0020, p < 0.05) is negative and significant.
These results are consistent with prior findings which indicate the negative impact of product recalls on the stock prices of firms on the day the recalls are announced (e.g., Barber & Darrough, 1996; Jarrell & Peltzman, 1985).
Based on this analysis and prior research we chose CAR [0, 0] as the dependent variable in Eq. 8.

We conduct preliminary analyses to empirically support the assumptions that undergird our research (please see Appendix 3 for details).
First, we provide suggestive evidence that when recall environment intensity is high recalls that constitute the recall environment are likely to be salient.
To assess this, we obtain monthly-level aggregate search data for the word “recall” from Google search trends [^13] as a potential measure of the attention that the recalls (which constitute the recall environment) are likely to draw (Ramos et al., 2020).
We also calculate average recall environment intensity at the monthly level and classify it as high intensity if it is above its median; else we classify it as low intensity.
We then regress Google search trends on monthly recall environment intensity.
We find that when recall environment intensity is high, so is the Google search for the word recall.
A similar pattern emerges when we use different types of time fixed effects (see Table 13 in Appendix 3).
This pattern suggests that the theoretically motivated measure of recall environment intensity captures the salience of recalls that constitute the recall environment.
Hence, recall environment intensity is likely to serve as a decision context that informs investors’ investment decisions when new recall announcements are made.

Second, we devise an empirical test to support the use of information salience theory to explain recall environment intensity's ability to alter investor response to recalls.
The key assumption here is that given limited attention span and uncertainty, investors find the contextual information from industry-level recalls salient in judging the impact of a firm's new recall announcement.
But the decision environment's salience, and therefore, its ability to influence decisions, is likely to reduce or vanish on those occasions that draw relatively more attention (Higgins, 1996).
Thus, keeping everything else the same, a new recall announcement that involves incidents (i.e., crash, fire, injury, or fatality) is likely to draw more attention and make the recall environment intensity less salient to investors as they respond to new recall announcements.
Hence, we should either see no effect or a reduced effect of recall environment intensity on abnormal returns for recall announcements that involve incidents, while the effect of recall environment intensity should manifest for recall announcements that do not involve incidents.
Accordingly, we split our sample based on whether the recall announcements involve at least one incident or not.
As expected, we find the effect of recall environment intensity on abnormal returns to be insignificant (0.0027, p>0.10) when recall announcements involve at least one incident while the effect turns out to be positive and statistically significant (0.0075, p<0.01) when recall announcements do not involve any incident (see Table 14 in Appendix 3).

We then compare the average difference in the abnormal returns of recall announcements in the high intensity environment with that in the low intensity environment.
This difference turns out to be positive (i.e., 0.0061) and significant with $p < 0.01$ (see Table 15 in Appendix 3), which provides suggestive evidence that the market penalty to recall announcements is likely to be lower when recall environment intensity is higher than when recall environment intensity is lower.

In this section, we assess the extent to which key estimates are sensitive to different assumptions and empirical choices made in estimating Eq. 8.
First, it was assumed that recall environment intensity and abnormal stock returns are influenced by the factors that are observed in the data, and these factors are accounted for by creating a matched sample through Coarsened Exact Matching.
However, this assumption could be violated in the presence of unobserved factors that could correlate with recall environment intensity and abnormal stock returns.
To rule out this possibility the empirical specification (in Eq. 8) was augmented with firm fixed effects to eliminate all the time-invariant firm-level unobserved factors.
The results in Table 7, Model R1, confirm the positive effect of recall environment intensity, the negative moderation effect of the recalled product's age, and the positive moderation effect of reliability reputation.
<!-- para 19 -->
Second, since we observe firms on multiple occasions, there is a possibility that the observations pertaining to same firms might be correlated.
However, in estimating Eq. 8 we assumed the observations to be independent.
To ensure that our effects are not driven by this assumption, we estimate a random effects model which allows the observations pertaining to a firm to be correlated.
The results are presented in Table 7, Model R2, and the key effects continue to remain consistent.
<!-- para 20 -->
Third, based on Mukherjee et al. (2022), one may argue that the mitigating effect of recall environment intensity that we present is because of the position of a recall in the cluster.
To show that the impact of recall environment intensity holds even after accounting for the position of a recall, we estimate a model in which we account for the number of recalls that precede the focal recall (see Table 7, Model R3).
Our results continue to hold, and they demonstrate that regardless of the position of a recall announcement in the cluster, the information content of the recalls that precede them matters.
<!-- para 21 -->
Fourth, we tested the robustness of CEM employed to preprocess the data before estimating the econometric model.
The use of CEM is motivated by the idea that pre-processing the data using matching not only reduces reliance on the functional form assumption (like the assumption of linearity in OLS) to mitigate endogeneity of recall environment intensity but also reduces variance in the estimated effects (Ho et al., 2007).

## discussion
<!-- para 2 -->
In this research we conceptualize that investors' stock market reactions to a recall announcement depends on the salience of the recall environment which we term recall environment intensity.
Drawing from the literature on information salience, we conceptualize recall environment intensity, which we verify using managerial interviews accompanied by an MTurk survey.
Using data from the automobile industry, we develop a measure for recall environment intensity, show that recall environment intensity limits the market penalty from a recall announcement, and that this mitigating effect is likely higher for brands with stronger reliability reputation and lower for older recalled products.
Next, we discuss the theoretical contributions and practical implications of our study.
<!-- para 4 -->
Our research contributes to the product recall literature by conceptualizing the recall environment intensity construct, developing a way to measure it, theorizing how it could impact post-recall announcement market penalty, and proposing boundary conditions to provide a nuanced understanding of the effect of recall environment intensity on post-recall stock market returns corresponding to a recall announcement.
Prior research on product recalls (see Table 1 for details) has largely focused on how recall characteristics such as the degree of harm from the product defect, type of defective component in the recalled product, delay of recall, and recall strategy affect firm performance (e.g., Chen et al., 2009; Eilert et al., 2017).
This stream of research has not typically accounted for the influence of the recall environment though the findings seem to imply that investors pay attention to the recall environment.
For instance, Jarrell and Peltzman (1985) suggest that when a firm issues a new recall, investors evaluate the recall against the background of prior recalls by other firms.
However, the recall environment has not been explicitly conceptualized and its impact on investor response has not been tested in prior research.
<!-- para 5 -->
We address this by building on prior research from Borah and Tellis (2016) and Mukherjee et al., (2022).
Borah and Tellis (2016) show how recalls of a brand can affect sales of rival brands and their firm value.
However, their manuscript does not address the impact of the recall environment, the domain of our study.
Mukherjee et al., (2022) show that because of herding, recalls happen in clusters and that stock market reaction to a focal recall depends on its position within the cluster.
In contrast, we conceptualize and develop a measure for recall environment intensity.
To account for the recall environment, one could simply present the number of recalls in a relevant time-period with more recalls leading to a more intense recall environment.
However, such a measure does not account for the variance in the nature of the recalls.
Recall characteristics such as the degree of hazard, magnitude and publicity could be important in determining the intensity of the recall environment.
Two recall environments which are identical in the number of recalls or volume of products recalled could be quite different in their information content if other characteristics of the underlying recalls are different.
Recognizing this possibility, we proposed the recall environment intensity construct to reflect the salience of recent recalls in the industry.
We are not focused on the position of the recall in the cluster as much as in the information salience of the recalls from other firms in the industry that happen between the recalls of the focal firm.
We find that the effect of recall environment intensity holds even when the number of recalls prior to the focal recall is accounted for in the estimation.
The implication of our findings is that two recall announcements that are preceded by the same number of recalls (in the cluster or recall environment) may experience different stock market reactions as they may face different recall environment intensities.
Thus, our results add to the finding in Mukherjee et al., (2022).
<!-- para 6 -->
The finance literature on initial public offerings (IPOs) argues that IPOs happen in clusters, resulting in hot and cold market cycles, and the information from prior IPOs influence investor response to new IPOs (Ibbotson & Jaffe, 1975).
The marketing literature also has noted the potential impact of other firms' prior related events on how investors respond to a firm's event (Bahadir et al., 2015; Chatterjee et al., 2005).
For example, and as noted earlier, a new product announcement by a firm is likely to be viewed as less surprising when other firms have recently announced new products (Warren & Sorescu, 2017).
Thus, if in the case of IPOs investor sentiments are driven by recent prior IPOs and for new product introductions investor sentiments are driven by recent new product introductions by other firms, then the need to consider product recalls by other firms when assessing the impact of a new recall becomes apparent.
In this regard, we show that the information content of prior recalls by other firms influences the stock market response to a new recall after accounting for the position of the recall in the cluster.
Thus, we build on prior research and provide a more nuanced view of the impact of a recall announcement on abnormal stock returns.
Hence, an additional contribution of our research is that we highlight the need to assess the information content of prior similar events of other firms when investor response to a firm's event is assessed, especially when such events have a tendency to cluster.

Using Eq. 9 managers can identify conditions under which recall environment intensity can lessen or worsen the harmful impact of product recalls on stock returns.
Table 8 shows examples of the net effect of recall environment intensity in different conditions.
In particular, the mitigating impact of an intense recall environment is strengthened for firms that have stronger reputation for reliability.
Thus, in comparison to managers of brands with weaker reliability reputation, managers of brands that have stronger reliability reputation can afford to be less concerned about issuing a recall in an intense recall environment.
However, managers of brands with stronger reliability reputation also need to consider the age of the recalled product to understand the effect of recall environment on their stock returns.
For example, the mitigating effect of high intensity recall environment did not manifest even for Honda, a firm with a strong reputation for reliability, in 2016 when the age of recalled vehicles was 9 years or more.
On the other hand, brands with weak reputation for reliability are less protected by the intensity of the recall environment.
A recall announcement of a brand with weak reliability reputation is likely to see lower attenuation of the market penalty in a high intensity recall environment compared to a brand with strong reliability reputation.
<!-- para 13 -->
Overall, our research can help firms take a more nuanced view of how the recall environment can affect recall announcement outcomes.
Besides, firms can utilize the empirical approach we employ in this research to measure recall environment intensity to assess the information environment in which firms' actions are interpreted.
More broadly, our research highlights how to assess the information content of actions by firms in an industry when those actions tend to herd and provides insights to stakeholders on the relevance of such information as they consider significant actions.
<!-- para 15 -->
Since our results are from the automotive industry, we urge caution in generalizing our findings to other settings.
However, our conceptualization of the recall environment, the measure of recall environment intensity, and how it can affect stock market response towards recalls are portable to different recall contexts.
Likewise, our way of quantifying the effect of recall environment can also be applied to different contexts.
The automotive industry is extremely significant from an economic perspective, as it represents more than $3\%$ of the gross domestic product in the United States, and for that reason, worth examining.
Nevertheless, we encourage researchers to investigate the role of recall environments in other industries to understand industry-specific nuances and to generate empirical generalizations.
Besides, since we have considered recall environment at the industry level, future research may also investigate segment level recall environment and its consequences.
