# jeffers_2024_the_impact_of_restricting_labor_mobility_on_cor 句子库存

## introduction
<!-- para 1 -->
**JEL.** J24, J41, J62, E22, L26, K31
<!-- para 2 -->
---
<!-- para 3 -->
Recent research and policy proposals have renewed the debate over agreements that restrict workers from leaving their employers.
In January 2023, the Federal Trade Commission (FTC) proposed a rule to ban noncompete agreements (NCs), a specific type of contract that precludes employees from moving to a competitor after leaving their employer.
Proponents of the ban point to studies indicating mobility restrictions can dampen innovation and regional growth (Saxenian 1994; Gilson 1999; Marx, Strumsky, and Fleming 2009), and hurt workers (Johnson, Lavetti, and Lipsitz 2021; Starr, Prescott, and Bishara 2021).
Opponents argue these restrictive agreements are an important way to protect and encourage firms' investment (Barnett and Sichelman 2020).
This paper evaluates the latter claim by measuring the impact of NCs on investment rates, both at existing firms and in the form of new firm entry.
<!-- para 4 -->
Ex ante, the effect of NCs on investment is not obvious.
In a Coasian world with perfect labor markets and no informational frictions, wages should adjust to compensate workers for the restrictions, leaving employment and firms' optimal policies unchanged (Coase 1960).
In practice, several layers of frictions may cause firms to adjust their investment rate when labor conditions change, and the direction is ambiguous (Bai, Fairhurst, and Serfling 2020).
In the case of stronger labor mobility restrictions, if wages do not fully adjust, firms may decrease their investment rate as they shift to relatively cheaper labor.
NCs could also depress workers' incentives or productivity (Garmaise 2011; Fulghieri and Sevilir 2011), reducing the return to investment.
On the other hand, complementarity between skilled labor and capital may lead firms to increase their investment rate as they accumulate more human capital through greater worker retention.
NCs can also help firms make investments by reducing the possibility of an ex post holdup by workers (Acemoglu and Shimer 1999; Conti 2014).
Finally, if mobility restrictions slow the entry of new firms, incumbent firms may also benefit from a reduction in competitive pressure and increase investment as a result of lower constraints (Kang and Fleming 2020).
<!-- para 5 -->
I exploit changes in the enforceability of NCs to test the impact of labor mobility restrictions on investment rate and on firm entry.
These changes come mainly from state-level court rulings that overturn precedent and cause a sudden change in the enforceability of NC contracts.
Besides policy relevance, NCs provide a useful setting to test mobility restrictions because they are commonly used, including for rank and file employees.
I introduce detailed employer-employee matched histories from LinkedIn to verify these changes in NC enforceability affect labor mobility in my setting, and to understand which moves are most affected.
After matching to public companies with investment data, my sample covers 5 to 6 million individuals each year, across all levels of seniority, occupation types, and states across the US.
Later, I also use LinkedIn company pages (including of defunct firms) to study the entry of new firms in the economy.
<!-- para 6 -->
I find that increased NC enforceability leads departure rates to decline by 7% to 11% for workers in knowledge-intensive occupations, defined as occupations where a majority of workers have a bachelor's degree or higher.
In my setting there is no statistically significant effect on mobility in other occupations.
This pattern is consistent with work showing NCs primarily affect college-educated and skilled workers (Johnson, Lavetti, and Lipsitz 2021; Starr, Prescott, and Bishara 2021).
As one would expect for NCs, the greatest declines occur in moves to the same occupation type or industry sector.
<!-- para 7 -->
I then show that these declines in labor mobility lead to a higher investment rate in physical capital.
The increase in investment occurs specifically in firms that rely more on knowledge-intensive occupations.
The results support the idea that NCs help retain human capital that is complementary to physical capital, as in Autor, Levy, and Murnane (2003).
Additionally, more enforceable NCs reduce employees' ability to extract rents from the firm after costly investments have been made, which can also encourage more investment.
<!-- para 8 -->
To further understand the mechanisms behind the increase in investment, I devise additional cross-sectional tests.
First, firms with higher labor-to-capital ratios may be more susceptible to holdup by workers, all else equal. [^3] I show that the relationship between investment and NC enforceability is indeed stronger in these firms.
Second, the ability to retain existing workers should help firms to increase investment in their core business, as opposed to secondary areas which would more likely involve hiring new workers.
I show that for firms operating in multiple segments, increases in physical investment go towards the main sector of operation.
<!-- para 9 -->
Third, I split firms by the ex ante level of competition they face.
Competitive pressure can lead to lower investment by diminishing the firm's return on investment (Gilbert 2006) or inducing financial constraints (Fazzari, Hubbard, and Petersen 1988), and NCs may reduce competition for incumbent firms (Kang and Fleming 2020).
Stronger NCs could therefore lead to higher investment rates by alleviating competitive pressures, independent of the effect on the firm's own workers.
In line with this, I find that in the highest competition tercile of firms, increased NC enforceability leads to higher investment rates at all firms, not just at knowledge-intensive firms.
<!-- para 10 -->
Lastly, I explore the effect of NCs on other forms of investment.
Recent work on investment theory has rightly emphasized the increasingly important role of intangible investments (Peters and Taylor 2017; Crouzet and Eberly 2023).
However, empirical work finds that intangible investment responds in the opposite way from physical investment when it comes to other forms of labor frictions (Bai, Fairhurst, and Serfling 2020; Acharya, Baghai, and Subramanian 2014).
In my setting, I find no statistically significant change in R&D investment rate or patent applications following increased NC enforceability.
This may be explained by NCs adversely affecting employee incentives (Garmaise 2011), and employee effort being an especially important input for innovation (Fulghieri and Sevilir 2011).
<!-- para 11 -->
Similarly, investment in new firms may also work differently than investment in existing firms.
In addition to the motivation effects mentioned above, new firms are especially responsive to local investment opportunities (Adelino, Ma, and Robinson 2017) and to local labor availability (Nimier-David 2022).
It is therefore unclear whether we should expect NCs to affect new firms in the same way as existing firms.
To examine this question, I use firm entry as a proxy for the extensive margin decision to invest in a new firm.
I find that even as NCs lead to higher physical investment at existing knowledge-intensive firms, new firm entry declines in corresponding sectors.
Taken together, the results suggest that restrictions on workers play an important role in whether new projects are started within incumbent firms or new firms, that is, where the boundaries of the firm are drawn (Coase 1937).
<!-- para 12 -->
The results stand up to a range of robustness analyses.
Throughout, specifications include industry-year fixed effects to account for time-varying industry conditions and firm fixed effects to allow for different firm-specific baselines.
Results are robust to including state- and firm-level controls, and even state-year fixed effects, which account nonparametrically for potential time-varying local conditions.
Restricting firms to be more geographically concentrated, so that a greater fraction of employees are in the treated state, makes results stronger.
I also show that the sample composition is stable over time, including in the share of workers in knowledge-intensive occupations.
Additionally, a placebo test in control states shows zero differential trend for departure rates in knowledge-intensive occupations compared to other occupations, when NC enforceability does not change.
<!-- para 13 -->
This paper builds on a rich labor mobility literature studying the effect of NCs both on specific sets of individuals (e.g. inventors [Marx, Strumsky, and Fleming 2009], CEOs [Garmaise 2011] and new ventures (e.g. medical practices [Hausman and Lavetti 2021], banks [Bird and Knopf 2015]). [^4] This paper extends the results to millions of employment histories and adds cross-sectional results, showing that the decrease in departures is driven by workers in a range of knowledge-intensive occupations, and that new firm entry declines across knowledge-intensive sectors following a change in NC enforceability.
In doing so, it helps to establish that the impact of NCs is both deep and widespread, countering criticism that the impact of NCs is narrow and likely overestimated (Barnett and Sichelman 2020).
<!-- para 14 -->
This paper also relates to questions raised by Zingales (2000) about corporate finance in the context of firms' increasing dependence on human capital.
Garmaise (2011) considers the impact of NCs on CEOs' human capital investment and the skill-intensity of production, and finds that these decline when NCs are stronger.
This paper complements Garmaise (2011) by looking at firms' investment rates by unit of capital rather than unit of labor, to better align with most theories of investment (Bai, Fairhurst, and Serfling 2020), and by separating out the effect for different cross-sections of firms.
<!-- para 15 -->
In this sense, the paper is closer to those of Autor, Kerr, and Kugler (2007) and Bai, Fairhurst, and Serfling (2020), who study the impact of employment protections on firms' investment policy.
Following Bai, Fairhurst, and Serfling (2020), I focus on investment rate rather than level as the more relevant measure flowing from the q-theory model of investment.
Employment protections also induce lower turnover, but by making it more difficult for firms to lay off undesired workers; instead, NCs make it easier for firms to retain desired workers.
This difference is critical: while employment protections decrease firm investment rates, I find NCs increase them.
<!-- para 16 -->
Finally, this paper contributes to our understanding of business dynamism by investigating a factor that impedes human capital reallocation.
Firm entry has declined in recent decades, arguably because of a decline in allocative efficiency that has slowed productivity growth (Decker et al. 2014, 2017; Kang and Fleming 2020).
This paper shows NCs may contribute to slowing business dynamism by increasing existing firms' investment rates while depressing new firm entry.
<!-- para 18 -->
This section lays out relevant institutional details for the empirical setting, including the prevalence of NCs and the enforcement changes examined in this paper.
Further institutional details are provided in Appendix I.B.
<!-- para 20 -->
NCs are a useful setting to study labor mobility because of their relatively widespread use.
<!-- para 21 -->
In a survey of about 11,000 U.S. labor force participants, Starr, Prescott, and Bishara (2021) find that 38% of workers have at some time signed a NC, and that 18% are subject to one at the time of the survey.
NCs are more common for more knowledge-intensive positions: Starr, Prescott, and Bishara (2021) estimate that 39% of workers with professional degrees have NCs, and 35% of workers in architecture, engineering or computer and mathematical occupations are subject to a NC.
Table A.1 contains information from Starr, Prescott, and Bishara (2021) on NC incidence by occupation, alongside educational attainment statistics from the Bureau of Labor Statistics (BLS).
NCs are more common in occupations where a majority of employees have completed a bachelor's degree, but they are still present in less "skilled" positions.
Anecdotally, news organizations from the Wall Street Journal to the Washington Post and the Atlantic have reported on the general increased prevalence of NCs over the past 15 years. [^5]
<!-- para 22 -->
NCs are governed at the state level, like most employment law.
There is wide variation across states in the type of NCs that are permitted and how they are enforced.
At one extreme, California and North Dakota have banned the use of NCs; at the other extreme, Florida allows enforcement of NCs even for employees who are laid off.
Bishara (2011) identifies six broad dimensions of state NC enforcement, which together determine the strength of NC enforceability in a particular state.
I review these dimensions of enforceability in detail in Appendix I.B.
<!-- para 23 -->
The main reason that NCs affect mobility is deterrence: employees avoid moving to a new job because they fear the consequences of breaching their contracts.
After an increase in NC enforceability, employees who seek legal advice about a potential move are more likely to receive advice that discourages them from leaving their employer.
For example, following Texas' decision in Marsh v.
Cook (2011), attorneys counseled employees that the risk of litigation for a NC breach had increased. [^6] Employers also play a role in transmitting the effect of more enforceable NCs.
Firms can and do remind workers of their obligations when a worker expresses interest in leaving. [^7] Finally, in states that allow modification, employers have incentives to draft NCs that bar workers from a broader set of moves (Bishara, Martin, and Thomas 2015).
<!-- para 24 -->
Nevertheless, some factors can lessen the impact of NCs.
For instance, employers may selectively apply and enforce NCs for a specific group of employees, such as top executives or those with access to trade secrets (Barnett and Sichelman 2020).
NC-bound employees who wish to leave could also bargain with their employers, and reach a mutually beneficial agreement that still allows them to leave.
In these cases, we may not see meaningful changes in departure rates among rank-and-file employees.
Section 5.1 verifies that NC enforceability negatively affects labor mobility, driven by workers in knowledge-intensive occupations.

## theory
<!-- para 2 -->
Ex ante, the relationship between labor mobility and investment policy is not obvious.
To start with, NCs can be viewed as a transfer from employees to employers: under a NC, an employee provides a guarantee to her employer that she will not compete for a period of time after leaving, which is valuable to the employer and costly to herself.
In a Coasian world with no informational frictions, wages should adjust to compensate workers for the restrictions, leaving employment and firms' optimal policies unchanged (Coase 1960).
<!-- para 3 -->
In practice, evidence indicates wages fall rather than increase when NCs are stronger (Johnson, Lavetti, and Lipsitz 2021; Starr, Prescott, and Bishara 2021), suggesting that firms' optimal policies may also change in response to NCs.
Moreover, uncertainty and irreversibility on both the firms' and the workers' sides may create holdup problems that are affected by NCs.
The nature of these holdup problems depends on the type of investment, the firm's reliance on skilled labor, and other industry conditions, such as the level of competition.
<!-- para 4 -->
In the sections below, I discuss the possible effects on physical investment (e.g., capital expenditures), then on intangible investment (e.g., R&D).
I also review the possible effects on entrepreneurship.
I discuss the results in the context of other work on employment restrictions in Appendix I.D.
<!-- para 6 -->
The literature is indeterminate on the effect of mobility restrictions like NCs on physical capital investment.
<!-- para 7 -->
There are at least four forces to take into consideration, including whether labor and capital are complements, the potential holdup that workers exercise on the firm, workers' incentives, and competition.
<!-- para 8 -->
First, the effect of NCs on physical investment depends in part on whether physical and human capital are complements or substitutes.
If they are substitutes and NCs cause labor to become relatively cheaper, this could lead firms to shift away from capital-intensive assets in favor of relatively cheaper labor-intensive assets, causing lower investment rates in physical capital (Caballero and Hammour 1998).
However, if physical and human capital are complements, NCs could lead firms to increase physical investment in order to match the higher accumulation rate of human capital caused by NCs. [^11]
<!-- para 9 -->
Autor, Levy, and Murnane (2003) show that physical capital tends to complement workers performing nonroutine problem-solving and complex communication tasks, tasks which often show up in “skilled” occupations.
Indeed, Larrain (2014) shows that when it is easier for firms to accumulate physical capital, there is higher demand for skilled labor.
A symmetric relationship may hold with NCs, where easier accumulation of skilled labor leads to higher demand for physical capital.
In this case, investment rates should increase especially in firms with a greater fraction of skilled workers (“knowledge-intensive firms”).
<!-- para 10 -->
**Hypothesis 1 (Complementarity of Capital).** When human and physical capital are substitutes, NCs lead firms to shift away from capital-intensive assets and reduce investment in physical capital.
When physical and human capital are complements (e.g., in knowledge-intensive firms), NCs lead firms to increase investment in physical capital.
<!-- para 11 -->
A second consideration that may influence how NCs affect physical investment is the holdup problem that workers exercise on the firm.
Workers' ability to extract rents from the firm after an investment is made, whether by negotiating higher wages (Acemoglu and Shimer 1999) or transferring knowledge to a competitor (Conti 2014), reduces the firm's ex ante incentive to invest.
Stronger NCs reduce employees' ability to extract rents by threatening to leave.
This alleviates the holdup problem and can induce higher investment rates.
Since NCs occur more frequently among knowledge-intensive occupations, and knowledge transfer is a greater threat for these occupations, the effect should manifest most for knowledge-intensive firms.
<!-- para 12 -->
**Hypothesis 2 (Holdup Problem).** In the presence of a holdup problem (of workers over firms), an increase in NC enforceability leads to higher physical investment rates, especially for knowledge-intensive firms.
<!-- para 13 -->
A third channel moderates NCs' impact on physical investment: workers' incentives.
Belot, Boone, and Van Ours (2007) argue that employment protections can encourage firms to invest in firm-specific skills and enhance productivity.
Conversely, provisions that decrease employees' bargaining power, like NCs, could decrease employee incentives to invest in firm-specific skills and reduce their productivity.
Garmaise (2011) uses a similar argument to explain why stronger NCs could lower physical investment: in his framework, NCs lower managers' investment in their general human capital and lead firms to undertake less skill-intensive production, on a per-employee basis.
In both of these situations, the total effect on investment rate will depend on the extent to which increased retention offsets any declines in employee-level productivity, as well as on the complementarity of physical and human capital.
<!-- para 14 -->
**Hypothesis 3 (Employee Incentives).** If NCs reduce worker productivity more than they increase firm-level human capital, and physical and human capital are complements (e.g., in knowledge-intensive firms), then increases in NC enforceability decrease investment in physical capital.
Conversely, if NCs reduce worker productivity less than they increase firm-level human capital, and physical and human capital are complements, then increases in NC enforceability increase investment in physical capital.
<!-- para 15 -->
Lastly, NCs' effects on investment may vary with the competitive environment.
Kang and Fleming (2020) argue that NCs reduce competition by making it difficult for potential new competitors to hire workers.
(Indeed, Section 5.4 shows that increases in NC enforceability lead to lower firm entry and fewer moves to new firms in affected occupations.) Lower competition can improve returns to investment and reduce financial constraints, making investment more attractive (Gilbert 2006; Fazzari, Hubbard, and Petersen 1988; Grieser and Liu 2019).
Notably, this channel does not depend on the mobility of the firm's own workers.
Instead, it depends on the ex ante level of competition facing existing firms and the extent to which NCs reduce competition in their product markets.
<!-- para 16 -->
This channel is most relevant when firms face high ex ante competitive constraints.
Where competition is high, incumbent firms should benefit from a reduction in competition, even if their own workers are not directly targeted.
When firms face ex ante low competition, this channel is less relevant.
The additional slack afforded by reduced competition should matter much less, and other mechanisms driving higher investment in knowledge-intensive firms (e.g., H1 or H2) should dominate.
<!-- para 17 -->
**Hypothesis 4 (Competition).** In markets with high competition (ex ante), increases in NC enforceability alleviate competitive pressure and increase investment in physical capital across all firms.
In markets with already low competition, reduced competition is not a first-order concern, and the previous hypotheses hold.
<!-- para 19 -->
Recent work on investment theory has rightly emphasized the increasingly important role of intangible investments (Peters and Taylor 2017; Crouzet and Eberly 2023).
<!-- para 20 -->
At the macro level, papers like Peters and Taylor (2017) treat intangible investment (a combination of R&D and “organizational capital investment” measured by SG&A) in much the same way as tangible investment, though they do note that intangible capital is more costly to adjust and adjusts more slowly to changes in investment opportunities.
<!-- para 21 -->
Empirical work looking at labor frictions, however, has drawn different conclusions.
Bai, Fairhurst, and Serfling (2020) find that R&D increases with employment protection laws (EPLs), the opposite of what happens to the physical investment rate; this finding is consistent with that of Acharya, Baghai, and Subramanian (2014), who find that innovation increases with EPLs.
The logic is that EPLs reduce the firm's holdup over employees, which increases employee incentives to innovate, and (implicitly) that this channel dominates any firm disincentives to invest in innovation.
Following this logic, if NCs decrease employee incentives by increasing the potential holdup of firms over workers, then we should see less innovation after NCs become more enforceable (Acharya, Baghai, and Subramanian 2014; Fulghieri and Sevilir 2011).
<!-- para 22 -->
The different predictions for tangible and intangible investments stem from the relative importance of employee incentives in producing innovation.
In the case of innovation, both Acharya, Baghai, and Subramanian (2014) and Fulghieri and Sevilir (2011) emphasize the central role of employee discretion in producing innovation.
Using the framework from H3, the employee-level effect may dominate when it comes to producing innovation or making investments specific to the production of innovation, like R&D.
The firm-level effect may dominate when it comes to the production of physical outputs or making tangible investments to support the production of those physical outputs.
<!-- para 23 -->
**Hypothesis 5 (Intangible Investments).** If NCs reduce workers' innovation productivity more than they increase firm-level human capital, then increases in NC enforceability decrease investment in intangible capital.
Conversely, if NCs reduce workers' innovation productivity less than they increase firm-level human capital, then increases in NC enforceability increase investment in intangible capital.
<!-- para 24 -->
Outside of the theory, it is worth noting that intangible investment has specific measurement challenges.
The majority of R&D expenses are wages (Hall and Lerner 2010), and the possibility that wages change in response to labor frictions (Starr, Prescott, and Bishara 2021; Johnson, Lavetti, and Lipsitz 2021) makes it difficult to disentangle the quantity of intangible investment from the price of human capital.
Recent measures of intangible investment, like those from Peters and Taylor (2017), still largely rely on R&D.
Patents and patent citations, meanwhile, have their own limitations in measuring innovation for two reasons: not all firms use patents to protect innovation, and patent citations are tricky to forecast for recent innovations.
Additional caution therefore applies in interpreting the results of these analyses.
<!-- para 26 -->
Another distinction that warrants discussion is investment in new firms, which may have different sensitivities than investment in existing firms.
<!-- para 27 -->
Multiple studies indicate that young firms are more sensitive to financial constraints when it comes to investment (Gonzales-Uribe and Paravisini 2019; Ma, Murfin, and Pratt 2022).
Adelino, Ma, and Robinson (2017) also show that new firms are more responsive to local investment opportunities.
In addition, new firms are highly sensitive to the availability of human capital (Nimier-David 2022).
They also may stand to benefit the most from investment protections (Barnett and Sichelman 2020).
It is therefore unclear whether we should expect NCs to affect new firms in the same way as existing firms.
<!-- para 28 -->
The question of how NCs affect entrepreneurial activity is not a new one, and in fact has long been a subject of debate in both academic and policy circles (Saxenian 1994; Gilson 1999; Fallick, Fleischman, and Rebitzer 2006; Starr, Balasubramanian, and Sakakibara 2018; Barnett and Sichelman 2020; White House Report 2016).
Given the importance of new firms for economic growth and innovation (Decker et al. 2017), I review the theory relevant to this special case below.
Since it is difficult to distinguish discrete investment decisions at new firms from the decision to launch a business, I generally refer to firm entry below as the principal new firm investment decision.
<!-- para 29 -->
On the positive side, an increased ability to retain human capital through NCs could benefit new firms by reducing turnover, especially in knowledge-intensive sectors (H1).
New firms could also benefit from a reduction in holdup problems, again especially in knowledge-intensive sectors (H2).
If these forces dominate, then more enforceable NCs could in fact lead to an increase in new firm investment and entry.
<!-- para 30 -->
On the negative side, at least three forces combine to make NCs potentially more costly for new firms.
First, while NCs may enhance new firms' ability to retain human capital, they may also hinder the acquisition of suitable talent in the first place.
Several models highlight the role of NCs in preventing potential founders from launching their projects (Franco and Mitchell 2008; Fulghieri and Sevilir 2011).
Additionally, new firms are particularly sensitive to labor availability for new hires in early stages (Nimier-David 2022), and they typically have less bargaining power in the hiring process compared to established firms (Babina et al. 2019).
Therefore, from a labor acquisition perspective, NCs are likely to impose additional costs on new firms and dampen their entry, particularly in sectors that rely more on occupations bound by NCs.
<!-- para 31 -->
Second, similar to innovation, investment in new firms requires a high level of employee discretion and motivation (Fulghieri and Sevilir 2011).
Moreover, since new firms are typically small, a decline in worker-level productivity may be more difficult to offset through an increase in firm-level human capital.
Both attributes increase the likelihood of new firm investment behaving as in the first part of H3 and H5, that is, with an increase in NC enforceability causing a decrease in firm entry.
<!-- para 32 -->
Third, Adelino, Ma, and Robinson (2017) show that new firms are sensitive to investment opportunities.
If incumbent firms engage in more investment, that additional activity may crowd out new firm entry.
This scenario is similar to the one described in Saxenian (1994), who argues that NCs lead to a slowing down of creative destruction.
<!-- para 33 -->
The factors described above are not mutually exclusive.
For example, the displacement of firm entry by incumbent activity may be exacerbated by difficulties with labor acquisition and incentives.
Alternatively, the benefit of increased investment protection could compensate for diminished investment opportunities.
Whether NC enforceability increases or decreases aggregate firm entry depends on the net effect of these combined factors.
<!-- para 34 -->
**Hypothesis 6 (Firm Entry).** If concerns about retention and holdup problems dominate for new firms, then increases in NC enforceability increase firm entry.
If instead issues of labor availability, incentives, or investment opportunities dominate for new firms, then increases in NC enforceability decrease firm entry.
<!-- para 35 -->
Different forces may dominate in different sectors.
A corollary of H6 is that if NCs decrease the availability of some occupations more than others, or reduce investment opportunities in some sectors more than others, entry is relatively more likely to decrease for new firms that rely on these occupations or belong in these sectors.

## methods
<!-- para 2 -->
I look for discrete, plausibly exogenous changes to NC enforceability to see how varying labor mobility restrictions affect investment and new firm entry.
<!-- para 3 -->
NCs are governed by both statute and precedent, meaning that NC enforcement can change at the state level two ways: either the legislature changes the statute or the state's high court makes a decision that sets a new precedent that lower courts must follow.
Table 1 outlines the NC enforcement changes that I use for my empirical setting: seven state Supreme Court rulings, one Appellate Court ruling, and one change from the state legislature, occurring from 2009 to 2013. [^8] If the change took place in the last 3 months of the calendar year, I assign the following year as the relevant first year of change.

<!-- para 7 -->
I first identify court rulings by combing through practitioner blogs and verify all cases using Westlaw, a leading provider of data for legal professionals.
I focus on rulings from states' highest courts because these represent the “final word” for state law and apply to the entire state.
Court decisions are retroactive, meaning that they affect the enforceability of all NCs, including NCs entered into before the decision was issued.
<!-- para 8 -->
To intuitively check the unexpectedness and importance of each court decision, I search for what local attorneys wrote about each decision at the time and confirm the expected impact is meaningful.
In Appendix I.B, I further investigate whether judges faced upcoming reelections.
Only three of the states feature contested elections for judges, two feature uncontested retention elections and two use appointment by the general assembly.
On average, judges were 6 to 7 years from reelection or reappointment at the time of their decisions, making it unlikely that decisions were affected by immediate political concerns.
<!-- para 9 -->
Two court decisions in my sample merit special notes.
In Illinois, the state supreme court's decision to expand the scope of business interests in Reliable Fire Equipment v.
Arredondo et al. was followed the next year by an appellate court decision, which the state supreme court declined to revisit, that decreased the enforceability of NCs.
To reflect this, I code Illinois as an increase in 2012 but no longer an increase in 2013. [^9] In Montana, the decision concerns the applicability of NCs to terminated employees and is therefore a more narrow change.
I verify that my results are robust to excluding Montana in Appendix I.A.
<!-- para 10 -->
Second, in addition to these court rulings, I note that Georgia passed a law making NCs more enforceable in 2011.
With law changes, we may be more concerned about anticipation or lobbying than court decisions.
In Georgia's case, there was in fact substantial uncertainty leading up to the change (Ewens and Marx 2017). [^10] The statute change was not retroactive: employers had to secure new NCs with their employees in order to benefit from modification.
Since the Georgia change occurs during my sample period, I include it in my main specifications, but also verify in Appendix I.A that my results are robust to removing the state.
<!-- para 11 -->
The “treated” states constitute a geographically and economically diverse group of states.
In Section 4 and Appendix I.A, I show that these states are similar to the control states in terms of political and economic characteristics, as well as levels of NC enforceability in 2009.
I also show that treated and control states follow similar trends in terms of macroeconomic conditions such GDP per capita, GDP growth, population growth and unemployment rate.
This suggests the changes in enforceability are not simply a response to a relatively better or worse environment in the treated states.

## results
<!-- para 2 -->
This section reports the findings of my analysis.
I first verify the impact of NCs on labor mobility, then turn to investment and entrepreneurship following the theoretical motivation from Section 2.
<!-- para 4 -->

One open question is the extent to which competition modulates the effect of NCs on investment.
Kang and Fleming (2020) show that NCs reduce competition by slowing new firm entry and alleviating pressure on incumbents.
Does this affect investment?
<!-- para 75 -->
As described in Section 2, while NCs have a direct effect on knowledge firms through the impact on knowledge worker mobility, they may also have a spillover effect on other firms through NCs' effect on competition.
The benefit firms get from lower competition does not depend on their own workers being subject to NCs, or on labor-capital complementarity or the existence of holdup problems.
What matters for this channel is the ex ante level of competition faced by firms.
However, where competition was low, we would not expect this channel to matter as much (H4).
<!-- para 76 -->

Table 8 shows results for each tercile of competition.
In panel A, the analysis includes all firms, while panel B restricts the sample to firms with 40% or more employees in the state of assignment.
All regressions include industry-year fixed effects, to allow for within-industry comparisons.

The results show that in the highest competition tercile, physical investment increases across all firms, at the rate of about 28,000 per million in assets.
The investment rate also increases in knowledge firms, though by less: the total increase is about 5,000 per million in assets for knowledge firms in the high competition tercile (still a 16% increase relative to the sample mean for that group).
One possible explanation for this pattern is that all firms in high-competition markets benefit from reduced competition but, within industry, knowledge firms disproportionately bear the enforcement and hiring costs associated with stricter NCs. [^18] In the lowest competition tercile, the effect is reversed.
Nonknowledge firms do not benefit from lower competition, and their investment rate actually decreases.
In contrast, knowledge firms increase their investment rate significantly.

The results indicate that NCs do have spillover effects on nonknowledge firms via their effect on competition, consistent with H4.
They also add nuance to the findings in Table 4, by showing that knowledge firms drive the results in low competition environments, but do not benefit as much in high competition environments.

In this section I turn to the effect of NCs on new firm entry, both as a way of exploring the effect on competition through new entrants as well as understanding the investment decision for new firms.
<!-- para 89 -->
I cannot observe investment at new firms on the intensive margin (e.g., the investment rate within newly launched firms), but consider firm entry itself to be the main extensive margin outcome of new firm investment.
Although this is a rough proxy, it helps to show the change in dynamics after NCs increase: while investment at established firms increases, as shown above, new firm entry declines, pointing to a trade-off in the effect of NCs on firms.
<!-- para 90 -->
Existing work provides evidence that NCs hold individuals back from starting their own ventures (Hausman and Lavetti 2021; Starr, Balasubramanian, and Sakakibara 2018), but there is surprisingly little direct causal evidence that changes in NC enforceability decrease new firm entry in the aggregate, especially across a number of industries.
As described in Section 2.3, the effect of NCs on firm entry depends on the net effect of multiple factors.
Even if NCs impede the ability of some workers to leave and found new firms, if other workers (or new labor force participants) step up to replace them, new firm entry may not change.
More generally, as stated in H6, if concerns about retention and holdup problems dominate for new firms, then increases in NC enforceability should increase firm entry.
If instead issues of labor availability, incentives, or investment opportunities dominate for new firms, then increases in NC enforceability should decrease firm entry.
<!-- para 91 -->
To investigate the impact of NCs on new firms, I collect information on firm entry using LinkedIn business pages, which include information on year founded, state, and a LinkedIn industry category.
I convert this into a panel at the industry-state-year level, where my main outcome variable is the number of new firms started in each cell.
To account for the fact that some states are much larger than others, I scale this outcome by state population, and include industry-state as well as industry-year fixed effects.
Section 3.1.3 provides more detail on this data source and set-up.
<!-- para 92 -->
Since the results so far show a differential effect for knowledge workers and incumbent knowledge firms, I split out LinkedIn industries that fall into the most closely related sectors: technology and professional, scientific & technical services.
These sectors together contain 32 industries, and I refer to them collectively as the “knowledge sector.”
<!-- para 94 -->
One concern with panel A may be that the numbers are driven by solo projects, such as consulting, which could be more susceptible to relabeling.
In panel B, I exclude firms classified as “myself only” in LinkedIn.
The results are similar, with estimates pointing to a decline of 9% relative to the sample mean in non-solo knowledge-sector firms.
NCs do not have an impact on non-knowledge-sector firm entry.

A different concern may be that the numbers are actually driven by large company spin-offs, instead of the entry of truly new firms.
In panel C, I limit firm entry to those firms with a size of 50 or fewer employees.
The results are again very close to those in panel A, with estimates indicating a 6% decline in firm entry in knowledge sector firms, but effectively no change in non-knowledge-sector firms.

## discussion
<!-- para 2 -->
Recent research and policy proposals have renewed the debate over labor mobility restrictions.
In particular, covenants not to compete (NCs) have received a lot of attention, mostly for their potentially negative effects on knowledge spillovers and entrepreneurship (Gilson 1999; Samila and Sorenson 2011).
However, firms may view NC enforcement as a tool to safeguard capital investments.
<!-- para 3 -->
In this paper, I examine how labor mobility frictions, such as NCs, affect investment decisions.
I review the theoretical literature to generate a series of hypotheses for the conditions under which NCs should affect investment policy, yielding separate hypotheses for investment in physical capital, in intangibles, and in new firms.
Another important distinction that emerges centers on workers' occupation type.
The relationship between physical and human capital differs across occupations, and firms more reliant on knowledge-intensive occupations are predicted to respond more strongly to NCs.
<!-- para 4 -->
To test these hypotheses empirically, I identify a series of recent legal changes to the enforceability of NCs in various states, coming mostly from court rulings.
I combine these with detailed data on employee movements from LinkedIn's database of employment histories, deidentified and aggregated to the employer level.
I show increases in NC enforceability substantially reduce the mobility of employees in knowledge-intensive occupations.
The effect is particularly pronounced for moves to the same occupation, same sector, and when more of the firm's employees are located in the treated state.
<!-- para 5 -->
These changes in NC enforceability lead established firms to increase their investment rate in physical capital.
This effect holds specifically for firms that heavily rely on knowledge-intensive occupations, have a higher potential for employee holdup problems, and face high ex ante competition.
<!-- para 6 -->
At the same time, investment in intangibles does not increase.
This confirms the pattern documented in other contexts (Bai, Fairhurst, and Serfling 2020; Acharya, Baghai, and Subramanian 2014) that intangible and physical investment respond differently to labor frictions.
Finally, the study demonstrates that stronger NCs result in fewer new firms entering the economy, specifically in knowledge sectors where existing firms increase investment.
<!-- para 7 -->
These results point to an important trade-off of labor mobility restrictions, between promoting investment at existing firms on the one hand and dampening new firm entry on the other hand.
A limitation of this study is that it cannot quantify the value of the marginal investment or of the new firms foregone, and a welfare analysis is beyond the scope of this paper.
Nonetheless, this paper contributes to our understanding of the real effects of labor restrictions by showing how important NCs can be for investment decisions, both in terms of the physical investment rate at established firms and the decision to enter for new firms.
Future research may build on this foundation to investigate additional consequences of NCs, and to refine our understanding of the trade-offs involved.
