# jeon_2026_safety_defects_and_fuel_efficiency_in_the_automoti 句子库存

## introduction
<!-- para 2 -->
Sustainability, Product Safety, Econometric Analysis, Automotive Industry
<!-- para 3 -->
---
<!-- para 5 -->
Since the 1970s, when the environmental movement began to take hold in the United States and when the oil crisis was at its peak, automotive manufacturers have been under incessant pressure to improve the environmental sustainability of their vehicles.
This pressure has originated from the federal government, via regulations such as Corporate Average Fuel Economy (CAFE) standards, as well as from consumers, via demand pattern changes that push for more fuel-efficient vehicles (Greene et al., 2018, 2013).
In response to these sources of pressure, manufacturers have been encouraged to channel their efforts towards enhancing the environmental performance of internal combustion engine (ICE) vehicles (Chen and Zhang, 2012) or introducing a wider range of hybrid and battery electric vehicles (BEVs) to their fleets (Automotive Fleet, 2023).
However, owing to somewhat sluggish BEV demand and sales (WSJ, 2024, 2025, 2026), ICE fuel efficiency continues to be an important priority for manufacturers.
<!-- para 6 -->
The key measure of fuel efficiency in ICE vehicles is miles per gallon (MPG).
The MPG of a vehicle is a design and manufacturing constraint that is significantly influenced by federally mandated fuel efficiency standards, and it is achieved through the adoption of sleeker aerodynamics, lighter-weight materials, and more innovative, ground-breaking engine and transmission technologies (Leach et al., 2020).
As a result, the average MPG has been steadily increasing year after year, moving from 19 MPG in model-year 2006 to over 25 MPG in model-year 2023, the years that bookend our analysis.
Unless someone is intentionally purchasing a gas-guzzling pick-up truck, customers today expect traditional ICE vehicles to operate at an MPG that would have been unthinkable not too long ago.
<!-- para 7 -->
One reason for manufacturers' tendency to focus on increasing MPG of ICE vehicles instead of fully embracing electric vehicle technology is that a complete transition to BEVs necessitates drastic changes in automotive manufacturing processes and supply chains (Engineering.com, 2022).
Another reason, which motivates our study, is that there are few perceived downsides to continuing to squeeze every last drop of fuel efficiency out of ICE vehicles.
In fact, there is a dearth of rigorous academic research that has explored possible downsides, or hidden costs, to continuing to improve the MPG in ICE vehicles.
Identifying such an unrecognized cost may provide valuable insights for NHTSA, which has the dual mandate of regulating both vehicle sustainability as well as vehicle safety (NHTSA, 2024a).
It may also serve as a wake-up call for manufacturers that ICE MPG improvements may come with a poorly understood safety cost.
<!-- para 8 -->
The key measure of vehicle safety that we examine is consumer-reported safety defects. [^1] On the surface, there is not an intuitive connection between MPG and defects related to vehicle safety.
However, when carefully considering what is required to obtain such previously unthinkably high MPG in today's ICE vehicles, one emerges.
Firms often take multiple steps to enhance MPG, incorporating several MPG-enhancing technologies simultaneously.
For instance, firms may employ integrated exhaust manifolds, variable valve lift, variable geometry turbochargers, cylinder deactivation, variable compression ratio, and start/stop, among others (Leach et al., 2020) to drive up their MPG.
Each of these individually, but perhaps even more so collectively, can generate uncertainty as well as technology integration challenges (e.g., Fleming, 2001; Fleming and Sorenson, 2001; Gokpinar et al., 2010; McNerney et al., 2011; Sosa et al., 2004; Vogelsang, 2020).
If shown empirically, this would indicate that there may be a heretofore unrecognized safety cost to increasing MPG in ICE vehicles.
Thus, our research question is: Do increases in MPG lead to increases in safety defects?
<!-- para 9 -->
The primary means by which NHTSA collects, analyzes and trends quality defects that affect vehicle safety is through their safety defect database (NHTSA, 2024b).
These data serve not only as a measure of defects, but precisely those defects that make vehicles less safe for consumers.
Using 18 years of MPG data, 19,785 safety defects, and fixed-effects models, we find that increasing MPG within a model-engine-year leads to a significant increase in safety defects for that same model-engine-year.
Specifically, each additional MPG is associated with a 17.5% increase in safety defects on the vehicle.
Results are confirmed when using fuel prices as an instrument for MPG.
In moderation analyses, we additionally examine whether a manufacturer's fleet-wide average MPG, which serves as a proxy for the pressure a manufacturer experiences to achieve higher MPG requirements, impacts the relationship between MPG and safety defects.
We do not find a significant moderation effect, indicating a potentially equivalent level of pressure on manufacturers to make MPG ever-higher in ICE vehicles.
<!-- para 10 -->
In post-hoc analyses, we examine if or how a 2011 CAFE policy change that increased the MPG target for vehicles shaped automakers' operational responses and technology decisions.
We find that the 2011 policy change stimulated the adoption of a bundle of MPG-enhancing engine technologies, and that these policy-induced technologies not only improved MPG but also led to more safety defects.
We also examine a spline regression model that enables a more precise examination of when exactly MPG increases begin to hamper quality and safety most significantly.
We find that the quality and safety costs of improving MPG are most prominent when firms improve MPG in vehicles that are operating at relatively lower MPG levels to begin with ( $\sim$18–22 MPG) in comparison to when firms take steps to improve MPG in a vehicle that is already operating at relatively higher MPG ( $\sim$25 MPG or greater).
<!-- para 11 -->
In addition, we present the results of eight robustness checks that examine alternative measures, models, and explanations.
For example, as safety defects are consumer-reported and may be affected by consumer selection bias, we examine firm-reported safety recalls as an alternative measure of vehicle safety. [^2] We find that increasing MPG is also associated with an increase in safety recalls, and that safety defects serve to mediate the relationship between MPG and safety recalls.
Nonetheless, as firms often initiate recalls in response to consumer-reported defects, selection bias could continue to persist with firm-reported safety recalls.
Thus, we further examine selection bias using a separate vehicle quality and safety reporting channel: JD Power Quality & Reliability ratings.
We find consistent results: higher MPG is associated with lower JD Power Quality & Reliability ratings.
We further explore evidence for an alternative explanation, that higher MPG enables customers to drive more miles, and that this greater vehicle wear-and-tear may explain an increase in defects.
The opposite is found to be true: increases in MPG are associated with a faster time to a defect (e.g., lower mileage at the time of the defect).
Thus, increases in MPG lead to both more and faster safety defects.
<!-- para 12 -->
We contribute to operations management (OM) theory by building upon and connecting two related but distinct literature domains: studies that examine firm responses to regulatory pressure (e.g., Aghion et al., 2016; Jaffe and Palmer, 1997; Popp, 2002; Porter and Linde, 1995), and studies that explore the operational consequences of technology bundling (e.g., Fleming, 2001; Fleming and Sorenson, 2001; Gokpinar et al., 2010; McNerney et al., 2011; Sosa et al., 2004; Vogelsang, 2020; Vogelsang and Fuhrmann, 2013).
The former has shown that environmental regulation and economic incentives can redirect firms' innovation and product-design choices, while the latter indicates that combining multiple technologies or features can create uncertainty and coordination challenges.
However, few studies, to our knowledge, have amalgamated these two streams of literature as we do herein, pinpointing specific product safety downsides to technology bundles, implemented in response to regulatory pressure that comes with financial penalties (e.g., CAFE standards).
Our study also contributes to a rich body of work that explores automotive sustainability (e.g., Ba et al., 2013; Greene et al., 2013; Leach et al., 2020; Rothenberg et al., 2001) as well as product quality and safety (e.g., Haunschild and Rhee, 2004; MacDuffie et al., 1996; Ramdas and Randall, 2008; Shah et al., 2017).
<!-- para 13 -->
These theoretical contributions lead to implications for automakers and for the National Highway Traffic Safety Administration (NHTSA).
For automakers, a key implication is awareness of the product quality and safety consequences of MPG improvements in ICE vehicles—a relationship that, from our interactions with practitioners and regulators, is poorly understood.
Our post-hoc spline regression model refines this implication to a more precise level by providing actionable steps for manufacturers related to which MPG improvements should raise the most significant concerns for vehicle safety.
Such an implication comes at an important time.
In particular, although the transition toward electric vehicles continues, recent headwinds in BEV sales growth (WSJ, 2025, 2026) suggest that ICE vehicles will remain an important part of the U.S. automotive market for years to come.
<!-- para 14 -->
For NHTSA, our findings indicate a juxtaposition of their two main objectives: sustainability and safety.
While NHTSA has spent decades regulating vehicle sustainability (NHTSA, 2020), they were originally founded via the Highway Safety Act of 1970 (CFR, 1970) to protect consumers from unsafe vehicles.
These two objectives may not work in conjunction with one another as well as previously understood, at least as it pertains to ICEs.
Thus, a heightened level of safety defect monitoring may be required for vehicles undergoing MPG improvements, particularly those that are starting out at lower MPG levels in comparison to those that are already operating at a relatively higher MPG.
We had the privilege to present our main findings to NHTSA leadership, and we expand upon these interactions in our discussion.

## theory
<!-- para 3 -->
We theorize on the relationship between MPG and safety defects by building upon and bridging two related but distinct bodies of research: firm responses to regulatory pressure (e.g., Aghion et al., 2016; Jaffe and Palmer, 1997; Popp, 2002; Porter and Linde, 1995) and operational consequences of technology bundles (e.g., Fleming, 2001; Fleming and Sorenson, 2001; Gokpinar et al., 2010; McNerney et al., 2011; Sosa et al., 2004; Vogelsang, 2020; Vogelsang and Fuhrmann, 2013).
We employ the lens of firm responses to regulatory pressure because MPG improvements are implemented not only due to firm responses to consumer demand, but also in response to CAFE standards that come with financial penalties (NHTSA, 2026).
Similarly, we employ the lens of technology bundling because improving MPG in ICE vehicles is rarely achieved through a single isolated technological improvement.
Instead, it often requires automakers to adopt and combine multiple technologies simultaneously (National Research Council, 2015).
These technologies may incrementally improve MPG individually, but when introduced together, they have the ability to substantially improve MPG.
However, such substantial improvement emanating from potential MPG-related technology bundling may also create a more complex, defect-ridden vehicle system.
<!-- para 4 -->
Prior research on environmental regulation and economic incentives shows that external regulation and pressures can reshape firms' innovation decisions and redirect technological efforts (Aghion et al., 2016; Jaffe and Palmer, 1997; Popp, 2002; Porter and Linde, 1995).
Such regulatory pressure can shape not only the amount of innovation firms undertake, but also the direction of that innovation.
For example, Porter and Linde (1995) argue that environmental regulation can induce firms to revisit inefficient routines and redesign products or processes in ways that improve environmental performance.
Related to our context, Aghion et al. (2016) show that fuel-price and tax incentives shift automakers' innovation toward cleaner vehicle technologies.
Together, environmental regulation, such as CAFE standards, does more than impose an external constraint; it can redirect automakers' engineering attention toward MPG-enhancing product designs.
In ICE vehicles, this redirection requires concrete operational choices about which fuel-economy technologies to adopt, how many to introduce, and how to integrate them within the vehicle system.
<!-- para 5 -->
The technology-bundling literature explains why these MPG-oriented design choices may create unintended operational risks.
Innovation often occurs through recombining existing and new technologies, but such recombination is uncertain because component performance depends on how components interact within the broader system (Fleming,
<!-- para 6 -->
2001; Fleming and Sorenson, 2001).
Related work on complex systems argues that when components are highly interdependent, improvement in one part of the system can be constrained by bottlenecks elsewhere (McNerney et al., 2011).
Moreover, studies on complex product development show that technical interdependencies across components require corresponding coordination and integration effort (Sosa et al., 2004).
When organizational coordination does not match product-architecture interdependencies, product quality can suffer (Gokpinar et al., 2010).
In the automotive context, this concern is particularly salient because vehicle features and software-controlled functions are highly interdependent; feature interactions can generate erroneous or deficient behavior, and developers may not fully recognize these dependencies during development (Vogelsang, 2020; Vogelsang and Fuhrmann, 2013).
In fact, such a concern was shared with us in conducting this study.
We discussed the potential relationship between MPG and safety defects with an automotive engineer from a global manufacturer.
The engineer stated that: “… there is the issue of integration of fuel efficiency enablers and communication between functional areas and risk analysis … For example, at Firm X, we installed more than 8 enablers per family of cars (improvement of engine friction, tires, bearings, weight reduction, new transmission, aerodynamic enablers, etc.) … the enablers were studied and calculated independently, with their interactions not as well understood.”
<!-- para 7 -->
Taken together, when we consider the literature that examines firm responses to regulatory pressure, as well as research that explores consequences of technology bundles, augmented by this engineer's reflection, we would expect the following to hold:
<!-- para 8 -->
> [!hypothesis]
> Hypothesis 1.
Increases in MPG will be positively associated with safety defects.
<!-- para 9 -->
However, not all auto manufacturers may experience similar pressures to push the envelope in MPG-increasing technologies.
That is, each manufacturer may view fuel efficiency regulations differently, depending on how far they have to go to hit fuel economy targets; for example, CAFE standards.
CAFE standards are the minimum fuel efficiency levels required of each manufacturer; for example, fleet-wide average MPG.
This is essentially a production-weighted MPG average of all vehicles the manufacturer sells (we provide the precise calculation in our measures section).
It is calculated by the federal government annually and enforced with financial penalties when it is not met. [^3]
<!-- para 10 -->
We propose that automakers with higher fleet-wide average MPG, in theory, should experience less external pressure to adopt riskier, defect-causing fuel-saving technologies (Whitefoot and Skerlos, 2012), allowing instead for a more cautious approach.
For example, some automakers have regularly exceeded CAFE standards, due to their investments and dominance in high-volume hybrid models, which can help firms achieve fleet-wide MPGs well above the required standards for passenger vehicles (EPA, 2017).
This may give these types of firms leeway to introduce less drastic and more incremental technological improvements to increase MPG in their ICEs (Itsuki, 2021).
<!-- para 11 -->
In contrast, when a manufacturer has a lower fleet-wide average MPG, it may instead experience enhanced pressure to take greater risks on MPG-related technological innovations.
These types of firms that have had a history of low fleet-wide average MPG (EPA, 2017) may have to aggressively chase riskier fuel-saving technologies, which can lead to greater mechanical complexity (Suciu et al., 2024), or unnaturally lightweight vehicles to meet the stringent regulatory targets.
This forms the basis for Hypothesis 2.
<!-- para 12 -->
> [!hypothesis]
> Hypothesis 2.
The positive association between increases in MPG and safety defects will be accentuated for manufacturers that have a lower fleet-wide average MPG.

## methods
<!-- para 3 -->
We examine the relationship between model-engine-year level MPG and safety defects for all automobiles manufactured between 2006 and 2023 using four key data sources: the U.S. Environmental Protection Agency (EPA), NHTSA, Ward's Intelligence, and Autoevolution.
<!-- para 4 -->
The EPA provides an annualized model-engine level Green Vehicle Guide that contains the average MPG for all automobiles sold in North America (Liu and Shankar, 2015).
NHTSA maintains a comprehensive database that reports all safety defects for all model-years.
This database is the primary means by which NHTSA collects, analyzes and trends quality problems that relate directly to vehicle safety (NHTSA, 2024a).
The data includes manufacturer, model, Vehicle Identification Number (VIN), year, date, vehicle mileage, the affected component, and a detailed description of the defect (Mao et al., 2021).
The data source for our control variables, such as sales volume, manufacturer's suggested retail price (MSRP), and the number of factory-installed mechanical and electronic/electric options, which are presented below, is Ward's Intelligence.
We collect data for years since model launch (in the case of brand new models) and major model redesign (in the case of established models) from Autoevolution.com.
<!-- para 5 -->
A data-centered innovation to our study design is that we conduct our analysis at the model-engine-year level, in lieu of model-year level, which is what has been traditionally used in similar automotive research (Guajardo et al., 2016; Shah et al., 2017).
When we refer to the engine level of analysis, we refer to engine displacement, which is the combined swept volume of the pistons inside the cylinders of an engine, in liters.
This is a key determinant of MPG and changes across different engine configurations for the same model-year (Hu and Chen, 2016).
An example helps to illustrate.
A 2014 Nissan Altima is a model-year contained in our data.
However, the 2014 Nissan Altima has two different engines, each with a different MPG: the 2.5-liter displacement engine has a 31 MPG, while the 3.5-liter displacement engine has a 25 MPG.
If we analyzed our regressions at the model-year level of analysis, we would need to average these relatively disparate MPGs across engines for a given model-year.
However, a key challenge here is that unlike the EPA, which reports the MPG data at the model-engine-year level, NHTSA reports safety defects at the model-year level.
We overcome this challenge by exploiting the VIN provided in NHTSA's safety defect database.
Using the VIN, we are able to extract the precise engine information for each defect reported on the safety defect database using NHTSA's VIN decoding tool. [^4] This tool provides the precise engine type for each VIN.
Our data spans 18 years (2006–2023), consists of 18 manufacturers (BMW, Chrysler, Ford, General Motors, Honda, Hyundai, Jaguar, Kia, Land Rover, Mazda, Mercedes-Benz, Mitsubishi, Nissan, Subaru, Suzuki, Toyota, Volkswagen, and Volvo), and is comprised of 643 unique model-engines, resulting in a sample size of 4,409 model-engine-years.
We provide a detailed explanation of our sample construction in E-Companion Table EC1. [^5]
<!-- para 7 -->
3.2.1 Dependent variable: Safety defects.
We use NHTSA's safety defect database to create our safety defect outcome measure.
This database, used in prior studies (Astvansh et al., 2022; Bray et al., 2019), has several advantages.
The data-entry process, as we describe in the paragraph below, is relatively rigorous and error-proofed.
In addition, NHTSA depends upon this database to identify important safety trends (Data.gov, 2024).
In our discussions with NHTSA, their leadership indicated without reservation that they depend upon this database for their safety monitoring and oversight of manufacturers.
While this does not by itself indicate data integrity or reliability, when considered in parallel with our other reasons for using this data, this does build confidence in the trustworthiness of this data.
Our review of this data and prior research (e.g., Bray et al., 2019) indicate that the users are predominantly vehicle owners, with a small proportion coming from mechanics.
None of them, to our knowledge, are from the manufacturer.
Thus, safety defect reporting is not affected by firm self-reporting bias, one reason why NHTSA pays close attention to the data.
However, it may be affected by consumer selection bias, as owners are not required to enter defects into the database.
To address that concern, we employ two alternative outcome measures in robustness checks: safety recalls, which are firm-reported, and JD Power Quality & Reliability ratings, which emanate from a completely different reporting channel.
In both cases, we find consistent results.
<!-- para 8 -->
In what follows, we describe the process of entering a safety defect, in order to demonstrate the formalization of the data entry process.
Defect entry begins with the individual user, normally the vehicle owner, keying in a valid VIN.
Without a valid VIN, the defect cannot be entered.
After entering the VIN, the database automatically populates the make, model, and year of the vehicle.
Next, the incident information that describes the safety defect is collected.
The website requires the individual to select at least one vehicle component from the pre-populated list of components that are associated with the defect; only components related to safety (e.g., engine, fuel/propulsion system, speed control, suspension, steering, and brakes) are available for selection.
<!-- para 9 -->
To demonstrate the focus on safety, we include a quote that appears on the first data entry screen of this website that provides guidance on reporting the defect: “What is a safety-related defect?
Generally, a safety defect is a problem that poses a risk to motor vehicle safety … An example of a safety-related defect is a steering component that breaks, causing a driver to lose control.
However, an air conditioner that does not work properly isn’t considered a safety-related defect, because it does not pose a risk to motor vehicle safety.” (NHTSA, 2024a).
The website also collects the date of the incident and the mileage of the car at the time the defect occurred.
On the final screen, contact information, including name, address, and email, is entered, so that NHTSA can contact the individual, if necessary in the future, for future investigation.
In sum, the safety defect entry process is reasonably robust and error-proofed.
<!-- para 10 -->
Our dependent variable is the number of safety defects reported by NHTSA for a given model-engine-year, from the time of the model-engine-year launch until the end of the corresponding calendar year, which is ~18 months.
We use this 18-month time window as it ensures that all model-engine-years in our data have an equivalent amount of time (i.e., 18 months) to accumulate defects.
That is, our safety defect data end on 31 December 2023, and the most recent model-engine-year in our data is from the 2023 model-engine-year, which launched in the summer of 2022 (model-years launch in the summer before the year of the model).
If we did not standardize the time window in which a model-engine-year can experience defects, then the earliest model-engine-years that begin in 2006 in our data would have a much longer time to realize defects than the latest ones.
However, in a robustness check (Section 6.1.4), we demonstrate consistent results when we standardize the defect collection period to additional, longer windows of time.
We note that if there were no safety defects for a model-engine-year in a certain year, we included a zero for such an observation in our data.
In E-Companion Table EC3, we present a breakdown of all safety defects reported within the first 18 months post-launch for each model-engine-year, disaggregated by component type, component description, and safety defect count.
<!-- para 11 -->
3.2.2 Independent variable: MPG.
The EPA’s fuel economy guide provides city MPG, highway MPG, and combined MPG for each model-engine-year; we use combined MPG.
<!-- para 12 -->
3.2.3 Moderator variable: Fleet-wide MPG.
A manufacturer's fleet-wide MPG is calculated as the fleet-wide production-weighted harmonic average MPG, not a simple arithmetic mean.
Specifically, if a manufacturer produces four different models, A, B, C, and D, manufactured in numbers  $N_A$,  $N_B$,  $N_C$, and  $N_D$, with fuel efficiencies  $MPG_A$,  $MPG_B$,  $MPG_C$, and  $MPG_D$, the fleet-wide MPG would be:
<!-- para 13 -->
$$ \begin{aligned}Fleet-wide MPG&=\frac{Total production}{\sum_{models}\left(\frac{Production of each model}{MPG of each model}\right)}\\&=\frac{N_{A}+N_{B}+N_{C}+N_{D}}{\frac{N_{A}}{MPG_{A}}+\frac{N_{B}}{MPG_{B}}+\frac{N_{C}}{MPG_{C}}+\frac{N_{D}}{MPG_{D}}}\end{aligned} $$
<!-- para 14 -->
For example, a fleet of four models having MPG values of 17, 18, 23, and 70, manufactured in volume numbers 100, 150, 130, and 140, respectively, has a fleet-wide MPG of 23.8, while the simple arithmetic mean MPG of the fleet would be 32.
This measurement approach follows the definition of fleet-wide average MPG used in CAFE standards (U.S. Department of Energy, 2024).
We note that by default our primary MPG variable is a subset of the Fleet-wide MPG for that manufacturer in a given year.
To ensure this is not biasing our results, in a robustness check (Section 6.1.6), we recalculate the Fleet-wide MPG by excluding the focal model-engine-year MPG, and find consistent results.
<!-- para 15 -->
3.2.4 Control variables.
We also include the following control variables in our model:
<!-- para 16 -->
***Sales Volume.*** We use sales volume of a model-engine-year (Volume) as a key control because the more vehicles sold, the more opportunities for defects (Shah et al., 2017).
Vehicle Mileage.
Vehicle mileage is a key indicator of a vehicle's overall condition (Bray et al., 2019).
A vehicle that is driven more miles is more likely to suffer from faster wear and tear of moving parts, such as the engine, transmission, and chassis.
To control for this effect, we average the miles driven at the time of a reported safety defect across all those reported for that model-engine-year (Mileage).
We employ the average because the level of analysis for our regression is at the model-engine-year level, not the safety defect level.
<!-- para 17 -->
***Manufacturer Suggested Retail Price (MSRP).*** Higher-priced vehicles may reflect better production quality and more meticulous assembly.
They are also more likely to receive regular maintenance over their lifecycles.
Moreover, owners of higher-priced vehicles may be more attentive and vocal, increasing the chances that defects are identified and reported.
To account for these sources of heterogeneity, we include MSRP (MSRP) as a control variable (Cachon et al., 2019).
<!-- para 18 -->
***Years Since Model Launch or Redesign.*** To control for the influence of defects that occur for newly launched or significantly redesigned models, we include the number of years since the model was launched, in the case of brand new models, or the number of years since the model underwent a significant redesign, in the case of established models.
The vast majority of models in our data set are established, and thus this measure is predominantly capturing the number of years since a significant redesign.
Model redesigns, which happen on average every three years in our data, include updated aerodynamics, functions, and technologies.
To control for this effect and to extract the additional influence of increasing MPG while holding time since model launch or redesign constant, we include years since model launch or redesign as a control variable (YearsSince).
<!-- para 19 -->
***Number of Mechanical Options.*** We count the number of factory-installed mechanical options used on a given model-engine-year (Fisher and Ittner, 1999; Shah et al., 2017).
We do so because more mechanical options on a vehicle may create more chances for defects to arise, but they may also enhance or reduce MPG (M-Options).
These factory-installed mechanical options include items such as start-stop technology, turbochargers, power door locks, anti-theft devices, adjustable steering column, auto interior mirror, and rear defogger, as a few examples.
<!-- para 20 -->
***Number of Electronic/Electric Options.*** We consider the number of factory-installed electronic/electric options on a given model-engine-year, as the presence of these features increases product complexity, and may be associated with MPG-related technological improvements.
Therefore, we include the number of electronic/electric options as a control variable in our model (E-Options).
Factory-installed electronic/electric options include Advanced Driver Assist Systems (ADAS) such as cross traffic alert, active pedestrian detection, lane departure warning, blindspot alert, collision warning, and traffic sign recognition, as a few examples. [^6]
<!-- para 21 -->
***Curb Weight.*** We control for curb weight as it may affect both fuel efficiency and mechanical complexity (Curb-Weight).
<!-- para 22 -->
***Transmission Type.*** Automatic transmissions are generally more mechanically complex than manual transmissions, involving additional hydraulic and electronic components that may increase failure risk.
Manual transmissions tend to be mechanically simpler but may yield different MPG performance.
To account for these differences, we include an indicator for transmission type, using manual as the reference category (Automatic).
<!-- para 23 -->
***Drivetrain Type.*** Drivetrain configuration affects both fuel efficiency and mechanical complexity.
Vehicles equipped with all-wheel drive or four-wheel drive (AWD/4WD) systems require additional components that increase points of failure in comparison to front-wheel-drive or rear-wheel-drive (FWD/RWD) vehicles.
These systems also typically reduce MPG due to added weight and drivetrain losses.
To account for these differences, we include an indicator for drivetrain type, using AWD/4WD as the reference category (FWD/RWD).
<!-- para 24 -->
3.2.5 Descriptive statistics.
Summary statistics and correlations are displayed in E-Companion Table EC4 and EC5, respectively.
We note that volume, mileage, MSRP, years since model launch or redesign, both mechanical and electronic/electric options, and curb weight all demonstrate skewness; we thus use the natural log of these measures in all models for consistency.
We apply a  $\ln(x + 1)$ transformation to preserve zero-valued data.
We mean-center MPG and Fleet-wide MPG to minimize multicollinearity that may occur when including their interaction term in the regression model.
Regarding correlations, volume, mechanical options, electronic/electric options, and MPG are all positively correlated with safety defects, while MSRP, years since model launch or redesign, and curb weight are negatively correlated with them.
<!-- para 26 -->
As safety defects are overdispersed count data, we examine support for our hypotheses using the following fixed-effects negative binomial regression:
<!-- para 27 -->
$$ \begin{aligned}\mathbb{E}\left[\mathrm{SafetyDefects}_{jt}\mid\mathbf{X}_{jt}\right]\\=\exp\Big(\mathbf{X}_{jt}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\gamma}+\beta\mathrm{MPG}_{jt}+\varphi\mathrm{Fleet-wide}\mathrm{MPG}_{it}\\+\omega\big(\mathrm{MPG}_{jt}\times\mathrm{Fleet-wide}\mathrm{MPG}_{it}\big)+\mu_{j}+\lambda_{t}\Big)\end{aligned} $$
<!-- para 28 -->
(1)
<!-- para 29 -->
where $i$ is the index for manufacturer, $j$ for model-engine, and $t$ for year.
SafetyDefects$_{jt}$ is our dependent variable. $\mathbf{X}_{jt}$ includes the model-engine level control variables.
Our key independent variable is MPG$_{jt}$, the MPG value of model-engine $j$ in year $t$ for $t \in \{2006, \ldots, 2023\}$.
Fleet-wide MPG$_{it}$ denotes the fleet-wide average MPG for manufacturer $i$ in year $t$.
We include model-engine fixed effects $\mu_j$ to account for time-invariant model-engine characteristics that may be correlated with both MPG and safety defects.
For instance, a manufacturer may configure a particular model-engine with higher-quality, less defect-prone components, or alternatively, prioritize fuel economy over vehicle quality.$^7$ To account for unobserved time effects, we include year fixed effects $\lambda_t$ for $t \in \{2007, \ldots, 2023\}$.
We cluster the standard errors at the model-engine level, although our results are consistent when we cluster at the model level.

## results
<!-- para 2 -->
Estimation results are presented in Table 1.
In columns (1)–(4), we find that sales volume has a significant and positive association with safety defects, suggesting that the more vehicles sold, the more defects.
Mileage is also positively associated with safety defects.
As expected, the number of years since model launch or redesign is negatively associated with safety defects.
M-Options is positively associated with safety defects, while E-Options is negatively associated with safety defects (we elaborate on the influence of M-Options and E-Options in post-hoc analyses).

To meet exclusivity requirements, fuel prices should only affect safety defects through MPG changes on a vehicle.
While this is not statistically verifiable, it is quite reasonable to presume for a few reasons.
First, fuel prices are predominantly determined by global energy markets and the broader economy, conditions that are unlikely to directly impact the defect performance of any single vehicle.
As well, the safety defects we study are affected by design and manufacturing decisions that are internal to the firm and unlikely to be altered by fuel prices, other than through the changes in fuel economy on a vehicle.
Further, matching each vehicle to the lagged price of the specific fuel-type it uses strengthens the empirical connection between the IV and a particular model-engine-year.
This closer connection bolsters our exclusivity argument because it occurs prior to vehicle launch, while safety defects occur and are reported only after vehicle launch.

We employ a control function (CF) approach to identify and correct for any endogeneity that may exist in the relationship between MPG and safety defects, an approach that is more efficient in nonlinear models (Wooldridge, 2015).

In Section 2.1, we theorize that regulatory pressure may lead automakers to improve MPG by adopting fuel-economy technologies that may successfully raise the MPG, but may simultaneously and inadvertently create quality problems that present safety risks for consumers.
To examine this theorizing more precisely, we conduct two additional tests.
First, we use the 2011 CAFE policy change to assess whether regulatory pressure increased the adoption of MPG-enhancing technologies, and whether such technologies are associated with higher MPG as well as more safety defects.
Second, to make the MPG-defect relationship more salient and decision-relevant, we examine which MPG improvement levels are most closely and most egregiously associated with an increase in safety defects via a spline regression approach.

5.1.1 Model-free evidence.
After many years of stability, the federal government began to increase fuel efficiency standards in 2011.
As shown in Figure 1 (U.S. Department of Energy, 2024), after more than two decades at 27.5 MPG, the passenger-car standard rose beginning in model year 2011 and continued increasing thereafter.
We use this policy change to examine whether regulatory pressure is associated with the adoption of MPG-enhancing technology bundles.
We do so because, in essence, MPG is the consequence of numerous technological decisions made by a manufacturer to improve fuel efficiency.
By exploiting this 2011 policy change, we can unpack MPG into measurable technological decisions and, in turn, examine the impact of those decisions on safety defects.
<!-- para 23 -->
For this analysis, we revisit two control variables from the main analysis: M-Options and E-Options.
These two count variables comprise all the technological decisions manufacturers make on a model-engine-year that are available for collection in Ward's data.
We examine these technological decisions more granularly to determine which technologies are most plausibly associated with MPG improvement.
We begin with model-free evidence.
E-Companion Figures EC1 to EC5 demonstrate how each of these technologies aligns with MPG and with the 2011 policy change.
We present these figures in five mutually exclusive categories consistent with how NHTSA categorizes safety defects (similar to E-Companion Table EC3): engine, electronic/electric, chassis, body, and airbag.
In each plot, we present the volume-weighted adoption share for each of these technologies for every year in our data (red line using the left Y-axis) against the volume-weighted MPG for every year in our data (blue line using the right Y-axis).
The volume-weighted calculations that comprise the left and right Y-axes in these figures are discussed in detail in Section EC1 of the E-Companion.
<!-- para 24 -->
From a model-free perspective at least, only the engine category closely follows the upward trend in MPG as well as an apparent increase in adoption after 2011.
As shown in E-Companion Figure EC1, five separate engine-related technologies closely follow the upward MPG trend in the plots, and all of them have inherent MPG-improvement-related characteristics.
That is, turbocharging, variable valve lift, cylinder deactivation, start-stop systems, and continuously variable transmissions (CVTs) are all documented in published sustainability literature as providing substantial MPG gains in ICE vehicles (National Research Council, 2015).
Turbocharging allows smaller engines to maintain performance with lower fuel use.
Variable valve lift improves efficiency by adjusting valve operation to manage airflow across operating conditions.
Cylinder deactivation reduces fuel consumption under light loads by temporarily disabling cylinders.
Start-stop technology helps an engine perform at higher fuel efficiency by cutting idle losses via shutting the engine off at stops and then automatically restarting as needed.
Finally, CVTs enhance efficiency by varying gear ratios to keep the engine near its most efficient range.
In aggregate, this evidence motivates our focus on examining engine technologies as a bundle of MPG-related improvements.

6.1.1 Safety recalls.
While our main outcome variable, the number of safety defects reported to NHTSA for each model-engine-year, has been previously used as an indicator of safety-related defects in the automotive industry (e.g., Astvansh et al., 2022; Bray et al., 2019), it may create selection bias in our model.
This is because vehicle owners choose whether or not to report the defect.
In this test, we examine an alternative outcome measure: safety recalls.
Recalls are often initiated for safety defects, they are closely monitored by NHTSA, and they are reported by the manufacturer, not by the consumer.
While this alternative outcome may reduce selection bias, it may not fully eliminate it.
This is because firm-initiated recalls are frequently conducted in response to consumer-reported defects.
Thus, while this test takes an initial step to address consumer selection bias concerns, we go one step further in our subsequent test using an alternative outcome measure from a separate reporting channel: JD Power Quality & Reliability Ratings.
<!-- para 58 -->
***We first examine safety recalls.*** To do so, we collected all auto recalls that occurred during our study time period (2006–2023) from the NHTSA safety recall database. [^10] Recalls are a coarser measure than safety defects, because one recall can affect multiple models, multiple model-years, and even multiple model-engine-years.
Therefore, to align recalls to MPG at the model-engine-year level, we count a recall each time it occurs for a given model-engine-year (E-Companion Table EC10 includes a correlation matrix with recalls).
Note that all NHTSA recalls are related to safety concerns; thus, NHTSA defines them as safety recalls.

We observe that MPG is also a positive and significant predictor of safety recalls.

We next examine if safety defects mediate the relationship between MPG and safety recalls.
We find implicit support for mediation from columns (1) and (2) of E-Companion Table EC11.
That is, in column (1), the effect of MPG on safety recalls, without taking safety defects into account, is strongly positive and significant.
However, in column (2), the effect of MPG on safety recalls is weakened and less significant after accounting for the effect of safety defects.
While E-Companion Table EC11 provides implicit support for the mediating role of safety defects between MPG and safety recalls, we confirm this via a Monte Carlo simulation approach.
We simulated 1,000 draws of the indirect mediation effect.
This indirect effect is the product of two  $\beta$ coefficients: (i) the coefficient of the independent variable (MPG) explaining the mediator (safety defects) and (ii) the coefficient of the mediator (safety defects) explaining the outcome variable (safety recalls), conditional on the independent variable (MPG).
<!-- para 64 -->
The Monte Carlo simulation indirect effect results, as shown in E-Companion Table EC12, provide empirical evidence of mediation.
That is, as MPG increases, safety defects increase, and this increase leads to an increase in safety recalls.
This lends further support to the notion that increases in MPG can adversely affect vehicle safety.

6.1.2 JD Power vehicle survey ratings.
As mentioned, because safety defects are voluntarily submitted by consumers, and because firms often initiate recalls based on consumer-reported safety defects, the risk of consumer selection bias may permeate through from consumer-reported defects to firm-reported recalls.
Consequently, we examine an alternative outcome measure that is less driven by consumer choice: JD Power Quality & Reliability ratings.
These ratings are based on vehicle safety survey responses from verified vehicle owners and are less directly exposed to the selection bias concerns related to choosing to report, or not report, a safety defect to NHTSA.
We use the JD Power Quality & Reliability rating, as this rating closely aligns with our focus on safety and quality.
This measure captures the level of defects, malfunctions, and design flaws experienced by vehicle owners, covering the entire vehicle system; a higher rating means better vehicle quality and safety (JD Power, 2026).

6.1.3 Volume-scaled defect rate and offset approach.
In our main analysis, we account for the impact of volume on defects by including sales volume as a control variable.
While we take this approach because it is a relatively standard means to control for the influence of volume on complaints and/or recalls (e.g., Noh et al., 2025; Shah et al., 2017; Wowak et al., 2021), there are alternative means to do so.
We examine two of these possible alternatives next.
First, we construct a volume-scaled defect-rate outcome and estimate the model using fixed-effects OLS.
Specifically, we define this alternative outcome variable as follows:
<!-- para 72 -->
$$ DefectRate_{jt}=\log\left[\left(\frac{Defects_{jt}}{Volume_{ji}/k}\right)+1\right] $$
<!-- para 73 -->
(9)
<!-- para 74 -->
where  $k = 10,000$.
The scaling constant  $k$ is used to rescale the denominator because the raw defect rate,  $Defects_{ji}/Volume_{jt}$, is excessively small due to the extremely high vehicle volume in the denominator compared to the significantly low vehicle defects in the numerator.
We scaled down the denominator by  $k = 10,000$ in order to place the defect rate on a more interpretable scale. $^{[11]}$ The second alternative we employ is using the offset function.
We test a negative binomial regression model where the outcome variable remains the count of safety defects, but we allow the natural log of volume ( $Ln\ Volume$) to enter as an offset.
This means that the coefficient for  $Ln\ Volume$ is fixed at one.
By doing so, the model estimates defect incidence relative to the vehicles sold (see Choi et al. (2012) and Békés and Ottaviano (2025)).
The results of both approaches are shown in E-Companion Table EC14.
Column (1) reports the volume-scaled defect-rate specification results, and column (2) reports the negative binomial model results with  $Ln\ Volume$ as an offset.
We find consistent results across both specifications.

6.1.4 Time lags.
We have thus far measured the number of safety defects that occur on a specific model-engine-year from its launch until the end of the corresponding calendar year.
To examine if or how this decision influences our results, we conduct three additional tests that employ different time windows: (i) Three-year SafetyDefects, (ii) Four-year SafetyDefects, and (iii) Total SafetyDefects.
Three-year SafetyDefects is the number of defects that occur from when the model-engine was launched until the end of the corresponding calendar year, plus one additional year.
For instance, for model-engine-year 2016, which launched approximately in July of 2015, we include defects received in calendar years 2015, 2016, and 2017.
Four-year SafetyDefects is the number of safety defects that occur from when the model-engine was launched until the end of the corresponding calendar year, plus two additional years.
Finally, Total SafetyDefects are all defects that occur from when the model-engine was launched until the end of our panel, which is 31 December 2023.
As shown in columns (3)–(5) of E-Companion Table EC14, our results remain consistent.
E-Companion Table EC15 also demonstrates consistent results using alternative windows of time for our recall measure, and all results hold.

6.1.5 Outliers.
The distributions of safety defects and safety recalls are skewed, with a few model-engines experiencing very large numbers of defects and/or recalls.
To verify that our results are not driven by these outliers, we implement the following two robustness tests.
First, we exclude the top 1% and 5% of model-engines with the highest number of safety defects and safety recalls and re-estimate our main analysis.
The coefficients on MPG remain similar in magnitude and statistical significance to the baseline estimates.
Second, we winsorize the distributions of safety defects and safety recalls at the 99th and 95th percentiles and re-estimate the models.
We once again find consistent results, as shown in E-Companion Table EC16.

6.1.6 Moderator.
In our primary analyses, we use a manufacturer's fleet-wide MPG, calculated as its fleet-wide production-weighted average MPG, consistent with the definition used in the CAFE standards enforced by NHTSA (U.S. Department of Energy, 2024), as the moderator variable.
However, the MPG of a specific model-engine-year is inherently part of the manufacturer's fleet-wide MPG.
We thus remeasured fleet-wide MPG by excluding the focal model-engine-year from the fleet-wide MPG computation.
This adjustment reduces any possible inherent connection between the MPG measure and the fleet-wide MPG measure.
Results, shown in E-Companion Table EC17, indicate consistency.
<!-- para 79 -->
As there are a non-trivial number of model-engine-years that do not experience a safety defect in our data (45% of model-engine-year observations have zero safety defects), we examine our relationships using a fixed-effects logistic regression model.
The outcome indicator variable binary defect takes the value of one if the model-engine-year experienced at least one safety defect, and zero otherwise.
The results are presented in E-Companion Table EC18.
We find that a one-unit increase in MPG is associated with a 27.8% increase in the odds of observing at least one safety defect (i.e.,  $\exp(0.245)=1.278$) for a given model-engine, lending further support to our findings.
<!-- para 81 -->
An alternative explanation for our results is that vehicles with higher MPG might be driven more miles, and it is the greater mileage put on the vehicle, and the resulting additional wear and tear, that explains the significant relationship with defects.
We examine this alternative explanation by further exploiting an important measure that is included in the NHTSA safety defect database—the mileage of the vehicle at the time of the defect.
This analysis allows us to determine if increasing MPG is associated with a slower or faster time to defect (i.e., mileage at the time of the safety defect) in addition to more defects.
<!-- para 82 -->
We examine this in two ways: (i) using MPG to predict the mileage for the first reported safety defect for a model-engine-year, and (ii) using MPG to predict the average mileage across all safety defects for a model-engine-year.
In both cases, we employ OLS using the natural log of the mileage for the safety defect(s) as the outcome variable.
The results are shown in E-Companion Table EC19, where column (1) presents the results for the first reported safety defect, and column (2) presents the results for the average mileage across all safety defects.
In both cases, the MPG coefficient is negative and significant.
This indicates two important things.
First, the alternative explanation appears not to hold in our study; that is, it does not appear to be the case that increasing MPG in a vehicle explains an increase in safety defects simply because the vehicle is being driven more miles.
Second, not only does increasing MPG lead to more safety defects for a given model-engine-year, but the defects happen sooner.
This helps support the overall conclusions of our study regarding the vehicle safety consequences of increasing MPG, and highlights the pervasiveness of the effect we have uncovered.
Increasing MPG on ICE vehicles leads to both more and faster safety defects.

## discussion
<!-- para 2 -->
Sustainability in the automotive industry is frequently studied in academic research (e.g., Ba et al., 2013; Greene et al., 2013; Leach et al., 2020; Rothenberg et al., 2001), discussed in the popular press (e.g., Forbes, 2021), and debated in the legislative and executive branches of the U.S. government (e.g., Reuters, 2020).
Much of this attention has placed pressure on automakers to improve the fuel efficiency of ICE vehicles.
This pressure can shape automakers' operational responses, including the implementation of engine-specific technologies that improve fuel efficiency.
However, these same technological changes may also create quality problems that can make vehicles less safe.
<!-- para 3 -->
In this study, we uncover a heretofore unappreciated downside of increasing fuel efficiency on ICE vehicles: increasing safety defects.
Our results indicate that each unit increase in MPG results in at least a 17.5% increase in safety defects on the vehicle.
This relationship is strongest for vehicle systems most closely related to MPG, particularly engine-related technologies, and persists when examining vehicle mileage at the time of the defect.
We also find that the 2011 CAFE policy change is associated with greater adoption of MPG-enhancing engine technology bundles, and that these engine technologies are associated with both higher MPG and more safety defects.
These findings lead to two contributions to the literature and two implications for practice.
<!-- para 4 -->
Our first set of OM literature contributions is to two related but distinct bodies of research: studies that examine firm responses to regulatory pressure, and those that explore technology bundling.
Our study builds upon and bridges these two research streams by examining the vehicle quality and safety consequences that emanate from engine-related technology bundles enacted by firms in response to financially enforceable CAFE regulations.
While prior research has found several consequences of regulatory pressure (e.g., Aghion et al., 2016; Jaffe and Palmer, 1997; Popp, 2002; Porter and Linde, 1995), no studies of which we are aware have explored product quality and safety consequences of regulatory pressure to enhance vehicle MPG.
Similarly, while studies have examined several aspects of technology bundling (e.g., Fleming, 2001; Fleming and Sorenson, 2001; Gokpinar et al., 2010; McNerney et al., 2011; Sosa et al., 2004; Vogelsang, 2020; Vogelsang and Fuhrmann, 2013), none that we are aware of have found that bundles of engine-related changes may be impacted by regulatory rules, thereby creating quality and safety problems for consumers.
By connecting these two bodies of research, we demonstrate how regulatory rules that firms must meet, or pay a fine when they do not meet them, can lead firms to make technological changes that can have unintended consequences.
Ironically, these unintended consequences may harm the same consumers that these regulators are trying to protect.
<!-- para 5 -->
We also contribute to the broader sustainability literature.
Existing research provides empirical evidence of the benefits firms can obtain from environmentally and socially responsible practices, including operational and productivity improvements (e.g., Corbett and Klassen, 2006; Jacobs et al., 2016; King and Lenox, 2001; Klassen and Whybark, 1999), financial performance (e.g., Flammer, 2015; Klassen and McLaughlin, 1996), and risk mitigation (e.g., Albuquerque et al., 2019; Jacobs et al., 2016; Li et al., 2021).
Related work also links the adoption of quality management systems, such as lean and total quality management, to improved environmental performance (e.g., King and Lenox, 2002, 2001; Klassen, 2000), particularly in the automotive industry (Rothenberg et al., 2001).
However, an empirical connection between sustainability-oriented product design and safety-related product defects has not been established, to the best of our knowledge.
Importantly, we find a positive association between sustainability and safety defects in ICE vehicles.
We stress that our message is not that sustainable product design yields bad product quality or unsafe products in general—our findings are specific to the automotive industry, in the context of improving the fuel efficiency of ICE vehicles, which have come under intense regulatory pressure to increase vehicle MPG.
We note as well that the ICE vehicle is a product category that, although currently critical to society, is not environmentally sustainable to begin with.
<!-- para 6 -->
Our study leads to direct implications for automakers.
First, we provide a heretofore unrecognized level of awareness between ICE MPG and vehicle safety.
MPG is a clearly defined, regulator-enforced, manufacturer-touted statistic that is well-known by stakeholders.
However, individual safety defects are less publicized and can perhaps go unnoticed due to the complexity of the data collection efforts required to identify this sustainability-safety relationship at the model-engine-year level of analysis.
Our study thus shines a light on a relationship that we propose is underappreciated and valuable to consider as the auto industry moves into the future: vehicle safety trade-offs surface as MPG increases in ICE vehicles.
Thus, as automakers improve MPG, they can use our study findings to enhance their validation and stage-gate approval processes, especially when multiple MPG-enhancing technologies are introduced simultaneously.
This is because the safety concern does not arise only from the adoption of any single technology, but also from the interaction of multiple technologies within the same model-engine-year.
Accordingly, firms may need to organize governance around engine-related MPG-enhancing technology bundles.
Our spline analysis sharpens this implication by empirically demonstrating that the MPG-defect relationship is most pronounced when MPG increases for vehicles that are starting out in the relatively lower levels of MPG.
Consequently, we recommend that vehicle quality and safety reviews should be more intensive for these relatively lower MPG vehicles ( $\sim$18–22 MPG) undergoing MPG-enhancing modifications, in comparison to those already operating at relatively higher levels of MPG ( $\sim$25 or higher).
<!-- para 7 -->
Our findings also lead to implications for NHTSA.
This regulatory body, which was founded with the underlying premise of vehicle safety, now challenges auto manufacturers to also make their vehicles more sustainable.
We note that although that emphasis appears to be on the short-term decline in the new U.S. administration (White House, 2025), we expect this de-emphasis by NHTSA on fuel efficiency to be relatively short-term (NPR, 2025).
While both the safety and sustainability objectives are important and noble, our results indicate that they are not independent, that is, policies that incentivize higher MPG can also shape manufacturers' technology choices in ways that can elevate unintended safety risks.
We can confidently state that NHTSA, from our interactions with them, is unaware of how these two objectives juxtapose one another.
It was apparent to us that senior leadership at NHTSA had traditionally viewed fuel efficiency improvements and vehicle safety as two distinct, completely unrelated dimensions of vehicle performance.
Demonstrating that they are related, and in a way that NHTSA had not previously considered, signals the practical contribution that our study may be able to make to public policy.
While NHTSA did not commit to, nor did they make direct comments related to, policy changes that could come from this discussion, we believe our results were both unexpected for them and concerning.
It was apparent to us that the way in which they monitor defects going forward may be impacted by our study.
As a consequence of our meeting with NHTSA, they now have not only the inspiration and justification, but also the means by which they can carefully track MPG-enhancing technological changes that may elevate safety defects, and consequently lead to safety recalls.
<!-- para 9 -->
While we took extensive efforts to address endogeneity via a 2011 CAFE policy reform as well as control function approaches using fuel prices as an instrument, bias stemming from endogeneity can never be completely ruled out in a secondary data study such as ours.
In addition, our main safety-defect outcome is based on consumer-reported NHTSA complaints, which may be affected by selection bias.
Although we use firm-reported safety recalls and JD Power Quality & Reliability ratings as alternative outcome measures, we are not able to fully eliminate all concerns related to selection bias.
As well, while we were able to disaggregate increases in MPG into discrete engine-related technological decisions, which in turn led to an increase in safety defects, these may represent just a portion of the ways in which MPG increases can degrade vehicle safety, as Ward's data does not include all possible vehicle design decisions that may influence MPG and defects.
<!-- para 10 -->
Future research can extend this work in several ways.
First, it may be intriguing to study how other product design changes that are instituted as a result of a regulatory rule with financial penalties impact product quality or safety.
In addition, it would be interesting to examine how the very recent de-emphasis on fuel efficiency standards by NHTSA, and the resulting potential reduction in MPG in new ICEs, impact future vehicle safety measures such as safety defects and recalls.
Notwithstanding, our study explores a novel research question, identifying tangible federal policy and manufacturer implications, and lays the groundwork for new research in this domain.

As a first step to address this concern, we use firm-reported safety recalls as an alternative outcome.
However, even though firms report recalls while consumers report defects, firms often make recall decisions based upon consumer-reported defects, which would allow selection bias to propagate from consumer-reported defects into firm-reported recalls.
Accordingly, we also use JD Power Quality & Reliability ratings as a second alternative outcome measure in robustness checks.
For both recalls and JD Power ratings, we find a consistent pattern of results: increases in MPG are associated with lower quality that can harm consumer safety.
