# lee_2026_freedom_of_expression_protection_and_corporate_conc 句子库存

## introduction
<!-- para 2 -->
Protecting free expression is essential in ensuring that citizens can freely exchange and disseminate information within the community without fear of retaliation or legal sanctions.
Legal scholars, political scientists, and nongovernmental organizations highlight the importance of protecting free speech in promoting government accountability and transparency (e.g., Meiklejohn [1960], Lewis [1980], Schauer [2011], Cannataci et al. [2016], Ginsburg and Huq [2018]).
In this paper, we ask a related yet distinct question: Does the importance of free speech protection extend to corporate accountability and transparency?
To answer this, we exploit the staggered enactment of anti-strategic lawsuit against public participation (anti-SLAPP) laws across U.S. states that enhance free speech protection and examine whether the protection of free speech mitigates the corporate concealment of bad news.
We broadly define bad news as adverse financial or nonfinancial information about a firm’s operations that is relevant to the firm’s valuation.<sup>1</sup>
<!-- para 3 -->
Corporate managers often have incentives to withhold bad news but quickly reveal good news to the market (Verrecchia [2001], Kothari, Shu, and Wysocki [2009]).
The corporate governance literature has been focusing on the monitoring role of residual claimants (equity holders and debt holders, particularly institutional investors), their agents (e.g., directors, analysts, and auditors), and securities regulators (e.g., the U.S. Securities and Exchange Commission, or SEC) in shaping corporate disclosure.
However, a growing body of literature suggests that nontraditional external monitors, such as the media, current and former employees, short sellers, and the general public,<sup>2</sup> could play an equally if not more significant role in uncovering and publicly disseminating negative information about public firms (e.g., Subrahmanyam and Titman [1999], Miller [2006], Boehmer, Jones, and Zhang [2008], Dyck, Morse, and Zingales [2010], Hales, Moon, and Swenson [2018]).
Unlike traditional corporate governance actors, the monitoring incentives of these public stakeholders do not derive from their explicit claims specified in private contracts.
Furthermore, their monitoring responsibilities are generally not governed or protected by corporate bylaws and securities regulations.<sup>3</sup> Instead, their monitoring actions are primarily motivated by monetary benefits (net of potential costs) or nonmonetary benefits (e.g., reputation and social justice) stemming from their exercise of free speech rights to publicly disseminate information about public firms.
Nonetheless, anecdotal evidence suggests that corporate managers may take strategic actions, such as retaliatory and groundless lawsuits against these public stakeholders, to heighten the cost of information dissemination and suppress free speech.
The suppression of free speech could undermine the monitoring role of public stakeholders and diminish corporate transparency.
In this study, we examine whether strengthening the protection of free speech can enhance corporate transparency and reduce managerial concealment of negative news.
<!-- para 4 -->
We argue that safeguarding free speech incentivizes public stakeholders to uncover and openly share adverse information about companies by reducing the potential costs associated with information dissemination, such as fear of retaliation and legal repercussions.
This diminishes the ability of corporate managers to withhold bad news about their firms.
Specifically, public discussion and dissemination of negative information limit the scope of bad news that managers can potentially withhold in the first place because of reduced information asymmetry (Jung and Kwon [1988], Dye [1998], Verrecchia [2001]).
Furthermore, even if managers initially attempt to suppress certain bad news, they are unable to withhold it successfully for extended periods because public stakeholders can detect and disseminate the hidden information, including the fact that managers are concealing bad news (Dyck, Morse, and Zingales [2010]).
More importantly, the mere threat of ex post public revelation of negative information reduces managers’ ex ante incentives to withhold such information (Ball, Jayaraman, and Shivakumar [2012]).
While the diminished ability to hide bad news does not necessarily change managerial disclosure behaviors directly, the decreased incentives to conceal negative information are likely to manifest in altered disclosure or reporting practices, such as fewer earnings misstatements to mask bad performance and more proactive disclosures of bad news (e.g., Verrecchia [2001], Leuz, Nanda, and Wysocki [2003], Nagar, Nanda, and Wysocki [2003]).
Based on the above arguments, we predict a negative association between the strength of free speech protection and the managerial concealment of bad news.
<!-- para 5 -->
To test this prediction, we exploit the staggered enactment of anti-SLAPP laws across U.S. states as a shock to free speech protection.
Strategic lawsuits against public participation (SLAPPs) are designed to censor, intimidate, and silence critics by burdening them with significant litigation costs (in terms of time and legal fees) until they abandon their criticism.
Beginning in the early 1990s, states started enacting anti-SLAPP laws to protect individuals who exercise their First Amendment rights to speak out on matters of public concern.
While federal protections such as the First Amendment and landmark Supreme Court cases such as New York Times Co. v.
Sullivan [1964, Sullivan hereafter) establish foundational rights to free speech, state anti-SLAPP statutes offer critical procedural safeguards that federal laws do not, such as the early dismissal of frivolous lawsuits and attorney fee recovery for defendants (Aimonetti and Talley [2021]).
These mechanisms discourage the misuse of legal actions aimed at silencing individuals who speak on matters of public concern.<sup>4</sup> Therefore, the enactment of state anti-SLAPP laws increases the protection of individuals from frivolous SLAPPs and substantially enhances constitutional free speech.
<!-- para 6 -->
Political and social issues are traditionally viewed as matters of public concern.
However, both legal scholars and state courts recognize that the actions of public corporations (and, by extension, corporate managers) also constitute a matter of public concern (e.g., Lidsky [2000], ComputerXpress, Inc. v.
Jackson [2001], Jackson [2001], Desai [2013]).
For example, in Global Telemedia Intern., Inc. v.
Doe 1 [2001], the court argues that
<!-- para 7 -->
A publicly traded company with many thousands of investors is of public interest because its successes or failures will affect not only individual investors, but in the case of large companies, potentially market sectors or the markets as a whole.
This is particularly so when the company voluntarily trumpets its good news through the media in order to gain the attention of current and prospective investors.
<!-- para 8 -->
Anecdotally, investment professionals argue that anti-SLAPP “statutes have gone a long way into protecting free speech in the U.S., and by extension promoting a freer and more efficient market.”<sup>5</sup> However, it is worth noting that the primary purpose of anti-SLAPP laws is to protect citizens’ First Amendment rights—not necessarily to enhance corporate transparency.
<!-- para 9 -->
We use three measures of corporate disclosure to capture corporate concealment of bad news.
Managerial concealment of bad news up to a certain threshold is often associated with stock price crashes (Jin and Myers [2006], Hutton, Marcus, and Tehranian [2009], Kothari, Shu, and Wysocki [2009]).
Therefore, we use the incidence of stock price crashes as the first measure of bad news hoarding (e.g., Ali, Li, and Zhang [2019], Kim, Wang, and Zhang [2019b]).
This market-based metric has the potential to capture various channels of information concealment (Piotroski, Wong, and Zhang [2015]).
In addition, we use two measures of bad news concealment based on corporate reporting and disclosure practices.
First, we use the probability of accounting fraud, the F-score, as a financial reporting–based metric of bad news concealment by means of misstatement or outright fraud (Dechow et al. [2011]).
Second, we use the frequency offirm-initiated negative and impactful press releases as a disclosure-based metric to capture bad news concealment.
The logic is that firms hiding adverse information are likely to initiate fewer press releases that contain significant negative news.
As previously discussed, these reporting- and disclosure-based measures are more directly linked to managers’ ex ante incentives to conceal bad news.
<!-- para 10 -->
Before conducting our main analysis, we use a cohort-matched difference-in-differences (DiD) research design to validate the effectiveness of anti-SLAPP laws in enhancing free speech protection (Gormley and Matsa [2011], Cengiz et al. [2019]).
Consistent with the assumption that these laws provide meaningful safeguards against frivolous SLAPPs, we find that, following their enactment, both the number and duration of such cases decrease significantly in treatment states relative to other states.
Turning to our main analysis, the findings indicate that, relative to control firms in other states, firms headquartered in states that enacted anti-SLAPP laws (treated firms) experience a statistically and economically significant decline in the likelihood of stock price crashes and the probability of accounting fraud, as well as a significant increase in the frequency of firm-initiated negative press releases after the laws’ enactment.
These results are consistent with our prediction that strengthened free speech protection leads to a decrease in the corporate concealment of adverse information.
In addition, the negative effect of anti-SLAPP laws on adverse information concealment arises largely after the enactment of the laws and not before, suggesting that the documented effect of free speech protection is unlikely to be driven by differential trends of reporting and disclosure practices between treated and control firms.
<!-- para 11 -->
One potential concern with our analysis is that variations in free speech protections across U.S. states and over time may not create a sufficiently strong setting to examine their impact, given the overarching influence of federal laws such as the First Amendment.
Consequently, omitted factors correlated with the timing of anti-SLAPP law enactment could be driving our results.
Arguably, our validation test on the effectiveness of these state laws partially mitigates this concern.
To further alleviate the concern, we conduct two sets of analyses.
First, we consider potential determinants of anti-SLAPP law enactment and show that unobservable factors correlated with the timing ofthese laws are unlikely to be large enough to overturn our findings (Oster [2019]).
Second, we explore the variation in the strength of anti-SLAPP laws across states.
If our results are spurious, it is unlikely that the effect of anti-SLAPP laws on bad news concealment varies predictably with the strength of these laws.
Using the anti-SLAPP ratings from the Institute of Free Speech (IFS) to capture the breadth and strength of free speech protection, we find that the negative effect offree speech protection on the corporate concealment of adverse information is more pronounced for firms located in states with broader and stronger anti-SLAPP statutes.
Furthermore, we identify states where anti-SLAPP laws are likely to have greater influence and reestimate our main regressions.
In these states, we find a negative and statistically significant effect of free speech protections on bad news concealment across all three measures of bad news concealment.
Conversely, in states where anti-SLAPP laws are less likely to have a meaningful impact, their effect on bad news concealment is generally weaker and statistically nonsignificant.
<!-- para 12 -->
The causal link between free speech protection and the corporate concealment of bad news hinges on two key mechanisms: (1) free speech protection encourages various public stakeholders to uncover and disseminate negative information about firms and (2) the dissemination of such information reduces managers’ ability and incentive to withhold it.
Although data limitations prevent us from directly testing these mechanisms, we present indirect evidence to support our interpretation of the main findings.
To this end, we focus on the stock price crash metric, which captures the effects of various channels of bad news concealment and is available for a larger sample.
First, using equity issuance and merger and acquisition (M&A) activities as proxies for ex ante incentives to conceal bad news, we find that the strengthened protection of free speech has a stronger negative effect on bad news concealment for firms with greater incentives to withhold bad news.
Second, we explore whether the effect of free speech protection on bad news concealment is partially driven by enhanced monitoring from three key groups of public stakeholders identified in prior literature.
Our results show that the negative effect of free speech protection on bad news concealment is more pronounced for firms with potentially more knowledgeable employees and firms with greater short interest.
Moreover, we find that both the media and employees become more willing to uncover and disseminate negative news about firms following the enactment of anti-SLAPP laws.
Overall, these findings provide tentative evidence that the enhanced free speech protection of employees, short sellers, and the media likely contributes to the reduced tendency of firms to conceal bad news.
However, we acknowledge that our collective evidence remains largely indirect, and these parties are unlikely to be the only public stakeholders who benefit from the enhanced protection of free speech.
<!-- para 13 -->
Our final set of analyses extends our main findings by examining the impact of the enhanced free speech protection on various corporate reporting policies.
Our findings show that the enactment of anti-SLAPP laws is associated with a significant increase in timely loss recognition in earnings, a significant decrease in earnings management tojust meet or beat analyst forecasts, and a significant decrease in the frequency of reporting incomeinflating non-Generally Accepted Accounting Principles (non-GAAP) earnings.
Collectively, these findings provide further evidence that strengthened free speech protections reduce firms’ incentives to withhold adverse information, prompting managers to adjust their reporting policies in ways that enhance the quality of financial reporting.
<!-- para 14 -->
This study contributes to the corporate disclosure literature by providing the first evidence that free speech protection mitigates managerial concealment of bad news.
Information asymmetries and agency conflicts between capital providers and managers create incentives for managers to either disclose or withhold private information.
The bulk of the empirical disclosure literature examines how various disclosure costs and benefits shape managerial disclosure decisions (Healy and Palepu [2001], Beyer et al. [2010]).
Moreover, an important stream of disclosure literature examines how the institutional context in which firms operate (e.g., securities and shareholder litigation) impacts managers’ disclosure strategies (Beyer et al. [2010]).
While prior literature has predominantly focused on financial market institutions, our study broadens this perspective by examining the role of nonfinancial market institutions—specifically, the protection of free speech.
These insights are pivotal for disclosure regulation, since they underscore the importance of considering the potential impact of nonfinancial market institutions on corporate disclosure when assessing the marginal benefits of new disclosure mandates (Leuz and Wysocki [2016], Mahoney [2021]).
<!-- para 15 -->
This study is also related to the literature on the relation between corporate governance and financial reporting and disclosure.
Traditional research has primarily emphasized the monitoring role of investors and their agents in shaping corporate disclosure.
In contrast, a burgeoning body of studies highlights the influence of non-capital provider stakeholders, such as the media (e.g., Dyck and Zingales [2002], Miller [2006], Dyck, Volchkova, and Zingales [2008]), employees (e.g., Dyck, Morse, and Zingales [2010], Wilde [2017], Berger and Lee [2022]), short sellers (Karpoff and Lou [2010], Fang, Huang, and Karpoff [2016]), and other public stakeholders (Dyreng, Hoopes, and Wilde [2016]), in shaping the corporate information environment.<sup>6</sup> Rather than identifying specific public stakeholders that influence corporate disclosure, our study contributes to this line of research by demonstrating the importance of free speech protection as a critical mechanism that enables these stakeholders, collectively, to mitigate the corporate concealment of adverse news.
<!-- para 16 -->
Finally, our research contributes to the law and finance literature (e.g., La Porta et al. [1998]).
Prior studies on corporate reporting and disclosure have primarily examined the impact of laws that protect the property rights of capital providers (e.g., Leuz, Nanda, and Wysocki [2003], Li, Lin, and Zhang [2018]).
We extend this literature by investigating the role of free speech protection laws, which apply to a broader set of public stakeholders, in mitigating corporate suppression of negative information.
<!-- para 19 -->
Financial markets rely on both financial and nonfinancial information to evaluate the performance of public companies and their executives.
Although this information demand could incentivize corporate managers to disclose value-relevant information, it also creates incentives for managers to conceal negative news (e.g., Healy and Palepu [2001], Verrecchia [2001]).
For example, Kothari, Shu, and Wysocki [2009] find that, due to career concerns, managers strategically withhold bad news, hoping future good news will offset its impact.
Similarly, Leuz, Nanda, and Wysocki [2003] show that the incentives to protect private control benefits induce corporate managers to mask poor performance via various earnings management practices such as earnings smoothing and accrual manipulation.
<!-- para 20 -->
The traditional corporate governance literature emphasizes the role of corporate stakeholders with residual claims (e.g., institutional investors) and their agents (e.g., boards of directors, auditors, and financial analysts) in monitoring managers and mitigating their tendency to conceal bad news (e.g., Klein [2002], Kim, Lu, and Yu [2019a]).<sup>7</sup> However, a growing body of literature suggests that nontraditional players in the corporate governance arena, such as employees, consumers, the media, short sellers, nongovernmental organizations, and the general public, also have incentives to monitor public companies by discovering and disseminating value-relevant information, particularly negative information, about public companies.
<!-- para 21 -->
The incentives of public stakeholders to monitor firms could stem from their implicit claims with the firm, as well as monetary or nonmonetary rewards (e.g., Bowen, DuCharme, and Shores [1995], Dyck, Morse, and
<!-- para 22 -->
Zingales [2010]).
For example, employees have incentives to monitor because their working conditions, job security, and future prospects hinge on their firms’ actions and performance.
The media is motivated to uncover and disseminate newsworthy and often negative information on public firms to increase their circulation and readership.
In addition, financial journalists who uncover corporate fraudulent behaviors could enjoy increased reputation and career opportunities.
Short sellers have financial incentives to discover negative information about public firms before taking a short position and to publicly disseminate their negative information after establishing the short position.
Finally, the general public, such as consumer organizations, environmentalists, and firms’ neighborhoods, has incentives to monitor and publicize product safety, pollution, or other issues to protect public welfare.
<!-- para 23 -->
The empirical literature has provided mounting evidence on the role of public stakeholders in corporate monitoring and information discovery and dissemination.
For example, research has shown that the media (e.g., Miller [2006], Dyck, Morse, and Zingales [2010], Dai, Parwada, and Zhang [2015], Miller and Skinner [2015], Call et al. [2022]), employees (e.g., Dyck, Morse, and Zingales [2010], Wilde [2017], Hales, Moon, and Swenson [2018], Chemmanur, Rajaiya, and Sheng [2020], Huang, Li, and Markov [2020], Berger and Lee [2022]), and short sellers (e.g., Desai, Krishnamurthy, and Venkataraman [2006], Boehmer, Jones, and Zhang [2008], Engelberg, Reed, and Ringgenberg [2012], Fang, Huang, and Karpoff [2016], Partnoy [2019], Alternative Investment Management Association [2020], Boehmer et al. [2020]) can access or discover firms’ negative information, including earnings management and fraud, and disseminate it via various channels, such as news reports, social media, and short seller reports.
Moreover, the general public can uncover negative news, such as unsafe products, through “serendipitous information” gathering in their day-to-day activities and expose it via social media or online reviews (Subrahmanyam and Titman [1999], Huang [2018], Maharana et al. [2019]).<sup>8</sup>
<!-- para 25 -->
The collection and dissemination of negative information by public stakeholders reduces information asymmetry between corporate managers and financial market participants, making it more difficult for managers to conceal bad news within their firms, even if they have incentives to do so.
Moreover, the threat of negative exposure can dampen managerial ex ante incentives to hide bad news.
However, corporate managers could take strategic actions to heighten the cost of information dissemination and suppress free speech.
In recent decades, there has been an increase in SLAPPs brought by corporations against news media,<sup>9</sup> short sellers, and individuals, with the primary purpose of stifling the public dissemination and discussion of negative firm information (e.g., Coalition Against SLAPPs in Europe, [2023]).<sup>10</sup> Section 1.1 of the online appendix provides several examples of SLAPPs.
These SLAPPs, albeit frivolous and without legal merit, can deter public stakeholders from disseminating negative information about public firms, because defending against these lawsuits is extremely costly and time-consuming.<sup>11</sup> The costs include not only monetary costs (e.g., legal expenses, libel insurance premiums for media companies) but also compliance costs associated with discovery requests (e.g., work disruption, potential loss of access to informants and information for media companies).<sup>12</sup>
<!-- para 26 -->
Although the First Amendment and the landmark decision of Sullivan, along with its progeny, have made it more difficult to successfully sue individuals for discussing matters of public concern, these federal protections do not prevent SLAPPs.
In Sullivan, the court held that proof of actual malice is required for plaintiffs involved in matters of public concern to prevail in a libel lawsuit, creating a national judicial standard for whether speech qualifies as libel.
The “actual malice” test was intended to set a stringent standard, where plaintiffs could establish it as an issue of fact through discovery and subsequently prove it at trial (Aimonetti and Talley [2021]).
Yet, it turned out to be a double-edged sword, since it encouraged SLAPP plaintiffs to subject their comparably less affluent defendants to the hassle of discovery and a lengthy litigation process (Aimonetti and Talley [2021]).
Defendants thus incur significant legal expenses, likely unrecoverable because of the American Rule on attorney fees (Root [2005]).<sup>13</sup> As the American Civil Liberties Union of the District of Columbia noted in its comment letter in 2010, “Litigation itself is the plaintiff’s weapon of choice; a long and costly lawsuit is a victory for the plaintiff even if it ends in a formal victory for the defendant.” State anti-SLAPP laws mitigate the abuse of process that arose from Sullivan by allowing defendants to file a motion to halt the discovery process, quickly seek the dismissal of the SLAPP plaintiff’s complaint, and recover attorney fees.
Thus, anti-SLAPP laws provide protection against libel lawsuits for defendants that is substantially incremental to existing federal protections.
<!-- para 28 -->
In this study, we use the staggered enactment of anti-SLAPP laws across U.S. states as a shock to free speech protection and investigate how free speech protection influences the corporate concealment of negative information.
Beginning in the early 1990s, states started enacting anti-SLAPP laws to protect individuals exercising their First Amendment rights to speak out on matters of public concern.
These laws aim to discourage the filing of SLAPPs and protect individuals from significant litigation and time costs.
For example, the California anti-SLAPP law (enacted in 1992) allows a defendant to file a motion to strike the complaint that the court will hear within 30 days.
Most discovery activities are placed on hold from the time the motion is filed until the court has ruled on it.
If the court grants the motion to strike, the defendant is entitled to recover attorney fees and costs.
In Global Telemedia Intern., Inc. v.
Doe 1 [2001], Global Telemedia International, Inc., headquartered in California, sued Barry Kind and another Internet poster to suppress their critical discussion on the company’s financial performance.
In 2007, GTX Global Corp., headquartered in Nevada, sued short seller Andrew Left for publishing “at least five defamatory falsehoods concerning GTX” (GTX Global Corp v.
Left [2007]).
In both cases, the defendants successfully filed an anti-SLAPP motion to strike in response to SLAPPs, and they were not burdened with significant litigation and time costs.
The enactment of anti-SLAPP laws substantially enhances constitutional free speech and increases the protection of individuals from frivolous SLAPPs by reducing the marginal costs of publicly discussing or disseminating negative news about public firms.
<!-- para 29 -->
The impact of free speech protection is likely to be more relevant to public stakeholders than to investors and their agents.
The responsibilities and rights of monitoring and information dissemination by investors and their agents are already specified and protected by private contracts, corporate bylaws, and securities regulations, and they can therefore speak freely about their firms absent free speech protection.<sup>14</sup> In contrast, free speech protection is critical for public stakeholders because their information dissemination is not governed by private contracts and thus more likely to qualify as speech on matters of public concern.<sup>15</sup> Therefore, we argue that strengthened protection of free speech facilitates public dissemination of corporate information by public stakeholders and decreases the ability and incentive of managers to suppress negative information.
Accordingly, we predict that corporate concealment of bad news declines after the enactment of anti-SLAPP laws.

## methods
<!-- para 3 -->
During our sample period from 1987 to 2017,<sup>16</sup> there was no federal anti-SLAPP law, and 31 states and the District of Columbia had adopted anti-SLAPP laws.
The anti-SLAPP laws in Washington and Minnesota were deemed unconstitutional by the respective state supreme courts in 2015 and 2017, respectively.<sup>17</sup> We provide the states and corresponding years of anti-SLAPP law enactment in appendix A and the ratings of the strength of the laws in appendix B.
Our identification strategy comes from the assumption that state anti-SLAPP laws are enacted primarily with the general public in mind to prevent the curtailment of citizens’ First Amendment rights and are not specifically intended to improve firms’ transparency and disclosure.
Section 1.4 of the online appendix explores the factors explaining the timing of the enactment of anti-SLAPP laws in various U.S. states.
The findings suggest that the enactment of anti-SLAPP laws is intended to protect a person’s rights under the First Amendment rather than to increase corporate transparency or improve the information environment.
<!-- para 4 -->
To validate the strength of state anti-SLAPP laws in enhancing free speech protection, we examine the impact of anti-SLAPP laws on the incidence and duration of SLAPPs.
To identify SLAPPs, we follow the legal definition of SLAPP cases by Canan and Pring [1988], where plaintiffs file a lawsuit with no intention of winning the case (i.e., unfavorable legal outcomes) but to deter defendants from voicing out.<sup>18</sup> Unfavorable legal outcomes include lawsuits where the case is dismissed in favor of the defendant before the trial begins or ruled in favor of the defendant if the case goes to trial.
In particular, we define SLAPP cases as (1) cases that were dismissed due to legal or factual flaws and (2) cases whose judgment is favorable to the defendant.
In addition, the SLAPP case duration in days captures the prevalence and legal costs (monetary and opportunity costs) of SLAPP cases.
<!-- para 5 -->
We obtain district court libel case data from the FederalJudicial Center’s Integrated Database of civil court cases.
We focus on cases at the district court level because this is the only level at which anti-SLAPP laws are applied.
The summary statistics on the number of SLAPP cases (SLAPPCases) and the duration of SLAPP cases (SLAPPDur) for each state–year observation are presented in table 1, panel A.
The mean (median) number of SLAPP cases in a state–year is 5.93 (4.00), implying an average of more than 300 SLAPP cases per year in the United States.<sup>19</sup> The mean (median) duration of a SLAPP case in a state–year is 271.09 (250.95) days, which is less than the 36-month average duration of SLAPPs documented by Canan [1989].
This is because Canan [1989] includes the involvement of all court levels in the calculation of average duration, whereas our data include only the average duration at each district court level.<sup>20</sup> We also present and discuss the distribution of SLAPP cases across states and time in section 2 of the online appendix.
<!-- para 6 -->
Next, we compare the pre- and post-event numbers of SLAPP cases (SLAPPCases) and the duration of SLAPP cases (SLAPPDur) for the treatment and control states.<sup>21</sup> Table 1, panel B, shows a decrease in the number and duration of SLAPP cases in the post-event period for treatment states, but an increase in the number of SLAPP cases and a slight decrease in the duration of SLAPP cases in the post-event period for control states.

This table reports the results of a validity test examining the impact of anti-SLAPP laws on SLAPPs.
In panel A, we report the descriptive statistics of the variables used in the validity test.
In panel B, we report the pre- and post-event descriptive statistics for treatment and control states. +++ denotes significance at the 1% level, and + denotes significance at the 10% level (two-tailed) for a two-sample t-test using standard errors clustered at the state level to test the differences in means between the pre- and post-event periods.
In panel C, we report the results of estimating the effects of state anti-SLAPP laws on SLAPPs using a cohort-matched approach (Gormley and Matsa [2011]).
The unit of analysis is at the state–year level.
Our dependent variables are SLAPPCases and SLAPPDur in columns 1 and 2, respectively.
The variable SLAPPCases is defined as the natural logarithm of one plus the number of libel lawsuits filed in a state that are either dismissed or not favorable to the plaintiff in a given year, consistent with Canan and Pring’s [1988] definition of SLAPPs.
The variable SLAPPDur is defined as the natural logarithm of one plus the average number of days of the case duration of SLAPPs filed in a state in a given year.
Libel case data are obtained from the Federal Judicial Center’s Integrated Database of civil court cases for our sample period beginning in 1987 and ending in 2017.
We control for the natural logarithm of one plus the total number of lawsuits filed in a state in a given year (TotalCases) and lagged state socioeconomic variables (Real GSP Growth, Unemploy, ln(Pop)).
We include cohort–state and cohort–year fixed effects (FE).
The detailed definitions of the variables are provided in appendix C.
All t-statistics (reported in parentheses) are computed based on robust standard errors clustered at the state level. \*\*\*, \*\*, and \* denote significance at the 1%, 5%, and 10% levels, respectively (two-tailed) on the outcome variable to the passage of anti-SLAPP laws.
For each cohort, we identify states that enacted anti-SLAPP laws that year and compare them with states that did not enact anti-SLAPP laws in the 11-year window.
States can be chosen as matches in multiple cohorts (i.e., matching with replacement).
The regression specification for the cohort-matched DiD research design is as follows (appendix C includes the detailed definitions of all the variables):
<!-- para 16 -->
$$
\begin{array}{r} SLAPP Cases _ {cst} \text {or} SLAPP Dur _ {cst} = \beta_ {0} + \beta_ {1} Anti - SLAPP _ {cst} + \sum \beta_ {k} Controls _ {cst - 1} \\ + \rho_ {cs} + \delta_ {ct} + \varepsilon_ {cst}, \end{array} \tag {1}
$$
<!-- para 17 -->
where c indicates the cohort, s indicates the state, t indicates the year, Anti-SLAPP is the variable of interest, Controls is a vector of state-level control variables, and $\rho$ and δ are cohort–state and cohort–year fixed effects, respectively, to separately estimate the impact of state and year unobserved heterogeneities for each anti-SLAPP law cohort (Gormley and Matsa [2011]).
Note that the unit of analysis is at the state–year level.
<!-- para 18 -->
Our treatment variable, Anti-SLAPP, is an indicator variable that equals one if the state has anti-SLAPP laws in the current year and zero otherwise.
Because anti-SLAPP laws were ruled unconstitutional in Washington and Minnesota, we code Anti-SLAPP as zero if the state is Washington or Minnesota in the year of the ruling and subsequent years.
For example, for Washington, the Anti-SLAPP variable will be coded zero from 2005 to 2009, one from 2010 to 2014 (where 2010 is the year of enactment), and zero in 2015 (where 2015 is the year the law was deemed unconstitutional).
<!-- para 19 -->
We control for the natural logarithm of one plus the total number of lawsuits filed in a state in a given year (TotalCases), the state real gross domestic product growth rate (Real GSP Growth), the state unemployment rate (Unemploy), and the logarithm of the state total population $( l n ( Pop ) )$ to control for local socioeconomic conditions.
We cluster standard errors at the state level because our main variable of interest is measured at the state level (Bertrand, Duflo, and Mullainathan [2004], Imbens and Wooldridge [2009]).
The results are presented in table 1, panel C.
As reported, both the number of SLAPP cases (SLAPPCases, column 1) and the duration of SLAPP cases (SLAPPDur, column 2) significantly decline after the enactment of anti-SLAPP law.
This result suggests that anti-SLAPP laws are effective in enhancing free speech protection.
<!-- para 20 -->
We also examine the validity of the parallel trends assumption that underlies DiD analyses.
This assumption requires treatment and control states to follow parallel trends over time in the absence of state anti-SLAPP laws.
We therefore estimate dynamic treatment effects.
In particular, we replace the Anti-SLAPP treatment variable in equation (1) with indicator variables for each event year (t 5 to t 2 and t 0 to t 5) for the treatment states, with event year t 1 serving as the benchmark year.
Figure 1 plots the coefficient estimates from the dynamic analysis with 90% confidence intervals for the five-year periods before and after the enactment of state anti-SLAPP laws.
The plots suggest that the trends for the treatment and control states seem parallel in the pre-event period, and the number and duration of SLAPP cases significantly decrease in the post-event period.
Overall, the results suggest that anti-SLAPP laws are likely effective in enhancing free speech protection by deterring SLAPPs.

To measure firm-level variation in the concealment of bad news, we use three measures intended to capture different attributes and disclosure venues of bad news concealment.
Managerial concealment of bad news up to a certain threshold is often associated with stock price crashes (Jin and Myers [2006], Hutton, Marcus, and Tehranian [2009], Kothari, Shu, and Wysocki [2009]).
Therefore, the first measure is a market-based metric of bad news withholding, where we use the risk of firm-specific stock price crashes, defined as large negative stock price movements.
Following Chen, Hong, and Stein [2001] and Jin and Myers [2006], we use the negative skewness statistic of a firm’s weekly residual returns to capture the risk of stock price crashes.<sup>22</sup> In particular, the negative conditional return skewness metric NCSKEW is the negative of the third moment of firm-specific weekly returns, divided by the standard deviation of firm-specific weekly returns raised to the third power.
A detailed description of the calculation of NCSKEW is provided in appendix C.

The second measure is a financial reporting–based metric, where we use the probability of accounting fraud as a proxy for bad news concealment.
Specifically, we use the F-score developed by Dechow et al. [2011] to measure the ex ante likelihood of earnings management, misstatement, or fraud (e.g., Berger and Lee [2022]).
Beneish and Vorst [2022] evaluate five fraud prediction models and find that the F-score is the most effective in predicting financial statement fraud.
The indicator variable I(Fscore >1) equals one if the firm’s F-score is greater than 1 and zero otherwise.
An F-score greater than 1 indicates that the firm has a high probability of misstatement that is greater than the unconditional expectation and hence represents an “above normal risk” of misstatement (Dechow et al. [2011]).<sup>24</sup> A detailed description of the F-score’s calculation is provided in appendix C.
<!-- para 29 -->
The third measure is a disclosure-based metric, where we use the frequency of firm-initiated negative and impactful press releases as a proxy for bad news concealment.
Managers who conceal bad news are likely to initiate press releases that disclose negative news less frequently, particularly those that are impactful and elicit strong market reactions.
Following Billings, Cedergren, and Dube [2021], we identify firm-initiated press releases from RavenPack.
Specifically, we use RavenPack’s Event Sentiment Score (ESS) and News Impact Projections (NIP), respectively, to identify negative and impactful press releases.
We impose the impact condition to capture bad news that contains meaningful new information to market participants.<sup>25</sup> Our measure of the frequency of firm-initiated negative and impactful press releases (NegPR) is defined as the natural logarithm of one plus the number of negative and impactful press releases during the fiscal year.
A detailed description of this variable is provided in appendix C.

To conduct the main cohort-matched DiD analysis, we use the same cohorts as for our validation test (see section 3.1).
For each cohort, we identify firms headquartered in states that enacted anti-SLAPP laws that year and compare them with firms not headquartered in states that enacted anti-SLAPP laws in the 11-year window.
Firms are not required to be in the sample for the full 11-year window and can be chosen as matches in multiple cohorts.
The regression specification is as follows:
<!-- para 32 -->
$$
\begin{array}{l} B N _ {-} \text {CONCEAL} _ {cit} = \beta_ {0} + \beta_ {1} \text {Anti} - \text {SLAPP} _ {cst} + \sum \beta_ {k} \text {Controls} _ {cit - 1, cst - 1} + \gamma_ {ci} \tag {2} \\ + \rho_ {cs} + \delta_ {cjt} + \varepsilon_ {cit}, \\ \end{array}
$$
<!-- para 33 -->
where c indicates the cohort, i indicates the firm, s indicates the firm’s headquarters (HQ) state, j indicates the four-digit Standard Industrial Classification code, t indicates the year, BN\_CONCEAL is our measure of bad news concealment (NCSKEW, I(Fscore >1), or NegPR), Anti-SLAPP is the variable of interest, Controls is a vector of firm- and state-level control variables, and $\gamma , \rho ,$ and δ are cohort–firm, cohort–HQ state, and cohort–industry–year fixed effects, respectively, to separately estimate the impacts of firm, state, and industry–year unobserved heterogeneities for each anti-SLAPP law cohort (Gormley and Matsa [2011]).
In the regression, we lag our firm- and state-level controls by one year to avoid overcontrolling issues, because the control variables could also be affected by anti-SLAPP laws (Whited et al. [2022]).
We expect the enactment of anti-SLAPP laws to be associated with the reduced concealment of bad news, as captured by a lower incidence of price crashes and a lower fraud probability $( \mathrm { i . e . , } \ \beta _ { 1 } < 0 )$ and a higher frequency of firm-initiated negative and impactful press releases $( \mathrm { i } . \mathrm { e } . , \beta _ { 1 } >$ 0).
<!-- para 34 -->
Our treatment variable, Anti-SLAPP, is an indicator variable that equals one if the firm’s HQ state has an anti-SLAPP law in the current year and zero otherwise.
Our research design assumes that firms primarily litigate SLAPPs in their HQ state.
Corporations can sue defendants in the state where the defendants have personal jurisdiction, typically where they reside or maintain minimum contacts.
Public stakeholders in the HQ state are more likely to have access to the firm’s information and publicly disclose negative news, which increases the likelihood of SLAPPs being filed in the HQ state.
Furthermore, precedents such as Calder v.
Jones [1984] establish that out-of-state media outlets covering a firm may still be subject to jurisdiction in the firm’s HQ state.
<!-- para 35 -->
Moreover, some states (e.g., Florida and Ohio) have long-arm statutes that allow corporations to file libel lawsuits against out-of-state defendants (Internet Solutions Corp. v.
Marshall [2010], Kauffman Racing Equip., LLC v.
Roberts [2010]).
In states where jurisdiction is uncertain, firms may prefer to file SLAPPs in their HQ state to leverage home court advantages.
Additionally, unsettled jurisdictional interpretations of defamation law in some states could lead courts to adopt Florida’s and Ohio’s precedents, creating a momentum toward liability for out-of-state speakers (Reporters Committee for Freedom of the Press [2010]).
To the extent that some firms do not litigate SLAPPs in their HQ state, it should introduce noise into our empirical tests, biasing results against finding support for our prediction.

In the regression model in which the risk of stock price crashes (NCSKEW) is the dependent variable, we include standard controls for firm fundamentals, such as firm performance (ROA), leverage (Lev), the market-to-book ratio (MTB), and firm size (ln(AT)).
In addition, we include share turnover (DTURN), to control for differences of opinions among investors, and stock returns (Ret) and stock return volatility (Sigma), to capture the correlations between moments of stock returns (e.g., Chen, Hong, and Stein [2001], Kim et al. [2011a, 2011b]).
<!-- para 37 -->
In the regression model in which fraud probability (I(Fscore >1)) is the dependent variable, we follow Berger and Lee [2022] and include controls for firm fundamentals that could affect F-scores, including leverage (Lev), the market-to-book ratio (MTB), firm size (ln(AT)), whether the firm is audited by a Big 4 auditor (Big4), sales volatility (SalesVol), industry growth (IndGrowth), whether the firm experienced a loss (Loss), and net financing activities (NetFin).
<!-- para 38 -->
Finally, in the regression model in which the frequency of firm-initiated negative and impactful press releases (NegPR) is the dependent variable, we include controls for firm fundamentals that could affect the frequency of firm-initiated press releases, such as firm performance (ROA), leverage (Lev), the market-to-book ratio (MTB), firm size (ln(AT)), whether the firm experienced a loss (Loss), the past 12 months of stock returns (12MRet), and return volatility (12MRetVol) (Billings, Cedergren, and Dube [2021], Rawson, Twedt, and Watkins [2023]).
In addition, we control for firm events that might prompt a firm-initiated press release, including capital expenditures (CAPEX), equity issuance (EqtyIssue), and merger and acquisition (M&A) (Li [2008], Ahern and Sosyura [2014], Huang, Teoh, and Zhang [2014]).
<!-- para 39 -->
In all regression models, we also include lagged state-level controls that could be correlated with both the enactment of state anti-SLAPP laws and bad news concealment.
Specifically, we control for the state’s real gross domestic product growth rate (Real GSP Growth), the state unemployment rate (Unemploy), and the logarithm of the state total population (ln(Pop)) to control for local socioeconomic conditions.
We use cohort–firm fixed effects to control for unobserved time-invariant firm characteristics, cohort–HQ state fixed effects to control for the unobservables of firms that relocate their HQ,<sup>26</sup> and cohort–industry–year fixed effects to control for unobservable time-varying industry shocks.
We cluster standard errors at the HQ state level because our main variable of interest is measured at the state level (Bertrand, Duflo, and Mullainathan [2004], Imbens and Wooldridge [2009]).

We obtain the enactment years and relevant state code sections of state anti-SLAPP laws from LexisNexis.
We collect all financial variables from Compustat and the Center for Research in Security Prices.
We supplement Compustat’s HQ data with data from Bill McDonald’s website,<sup>27</sup> because Compustat does not provide historical HQ locations.
We limit our sample to firm observations that are headquartered in the United States.
Following prior studies, we drop observations with (1) missing or negative assets and common equity, (2) missing sales, (3) fewer than 26 weeks of stock return data, (4) fiscal year-end prices below \$1, and (5) missing control variables.
As mentioned in section 3.1, our sample period starts in 1987 and ends in 2017.

Finally, we winsorize all continuous variables at the 1st and 99th percentiles for each cohort.

We acknowledge that the timing of the anti-SLAPP law enactment could still be influenced by unobservable factors.
To assess the robustness of our results, we calculate Oster’s [2019] δ, which builds on the approach of Altonji, Elder, and Taber [2005] to measure the relative importance of observed and unobserved variables in explaining the estimated effects.
This approach examines how the coefficient of Anti-SLAPP in a baseline model changes relative to R-squared changes as control variables are added.
To properly identify the DiD estimate (i.e., the coefficient of Anti-SLAPP), we include two variables, Post and Treatment.
Post is an indicator variable that equals one if the year is on or after the enactment of the anti-SLAPP law in the cohort and zero otherwise.
Treatment is an indicator that equals one if the state eventually enacts an anti-SLAPP law in the cohort and zero otherwise.
These variables are subsumed by our cohort– state and cohort–industry–year fixed effects in our fully controlled model (i.e., equation (2)).

For example, a δ of 2.00 indicates that unobserved factors would need to be twice as important as observed factors to overturn the findings.

5.1.1.
Managers’ incentives.
An extensive accounting literature highlights the importance of managerial reporting incentives in shaping managerial reporting and disclosure practices (e.g., Leuz, Nanda, and Wysocki [2003], Burgstahler, Hail, and Leuz [2006], Leuz and Wysocki [2016]).
If managers have greater ex ante incentives to obfuscate firm performance from outsiders, the incremental effect of free speech protection in reducing bad news concealment should be stronger.

5.1.4.
Discussion.
The cross-sectional results in sections 5.1.2 and 5.1.3 suggest that the effect of free speech protection on bad news concealment is at least partially driven by the enhanced monitoring roles of employees and short sellers.
However, these findings do not imply that these stakeholders are the only beneficiaries of strengthened free speech protections.
Our focus on employees and short sellers is primarily due to the availability of reasonable proxies to capture their influence.
In contrast, investigating the roles of other public stakeholders—such as consumers, corporate neighborhoods, nongovernmental organizations, or the general public—is more challenging due to data limitations.
Furthermore, when we repeat the crosssectional analyses using the accounting fraud and press release metrics of bad news concealment, we find no significant moderating effects.
This absence of effects could be due to the lack of test power, measurement errors in our cross-sectional variables, or the possibility that these stakeholders do not function via mechanisms of deterring fraud or encouraging more firm-initiated negative press releases.

## discussion
<!-- para 2 -->
This study examines whether free speech protection mitigates the corporate concealment of bad news.
Using the staggered enactment of anti-SLAPP laws across U.S. states as a shock that enhances free speech protection, we find that strengthened free speech protection reduces the corporate suppression of bad news, as reflected by the reduced incidence of stock price crashes and the probability of accounting fraud and the increased frequency of firm-initiated negative press releases.
In addition, the mitigating effect of free speech protection on bad news concealment is more pronounced when anti-SLAPP laws provide broader and stronger protection for free speech and in states where the anti-SLAPP laws are more likely to be influential in enhancing free speech.
<!-- para 3 -->
Our cross-sectional tests suggest that the negative effect of free speech protection on bad news concealment is more pronounced for firms with stronger ex ante incentives to hide bad news.
Moreover, our analyses suggest that free speech protection might enhance the role of employees, short sellers, and the media in constraining bad news concealment.
Finally, we find that strengthened protection of free speech is also associated with a significant increase in timely loss recognition in earnings, a significant decrease in the tendency to just meet or beat analyst forecasts, and a significant decrease in the frequency of providing income-inflating non-GAAP earnings.
Collectively, our evidence suggests that free speech protection enhances the monitoring role of public stakeholders, thereby mitigating the corporate concealment of bad news and improving financial reporting and disclosure quality.
<!-- para 4 -->
Our findings should be interpreted with several caveats in mind.
First, our identification strategy relies on the assumption that the enactment of state anti-SLAPP laws generates significant variation in the strength of free speech protections across U.S. states and over time.
If this assumption does not hold, our results could be spurious.
Future research could explore cross-country variations in free speech protections to further validate and generalize our findings.
Second, unknown factors correlated with the timing of anti-SLAPP law enactments could be driving our results.
While we have conducted robustness checks to address the omitted variable concern, we cannot entirely rule out their influence.
Third, data limitations prevent us from precisely identifying the mechanisms through which free speech protections affect corporate transparency.
We encourage future research to leverage innovative data sets to explore these mechanisms further.
Lastly, our measurement of bad news concealment is not exhaustive, which constrains the broader implications of our findings appendix a: u.s. states with anti-slapp laws

In 1999, Bloomberg News reported a series of negative news articles about a California-based Internet company Hitsgalore.com.
In one article, Bloomberg News reported that Hitsgalore had failed to disclose in regulatory filings that its Chief Technology Officer (CTO), who was also the principal shareholder, had been sued by the Federal Trade Commission for making false and deceptive promises to customers of an earlier Internet firm with which he was involved (Bloomberg 1999).
In another article, Bloomberg News reported that Hitsgalore's largest customer and investor, Life Foundation Trust, had defaulted on its payment obligations to Hitsgalore (Bloomberg 1999).
This series of news articles uncovered potential negative news about Hitsgalore's financial performance and corporate governance, adversely affecting investor confidence in the company and causing the stock price to decline as a result.
In retaliation, Hitsgalore sued Bloomberg News for libel (Bloomberg 2000a).
However, the court relied on California's anti-SLAPP statute and dismissed the case (Bloomberg 2000b).
Our anecdote also underscores the importance of anti-SLAPP laws in encouraging the media to publicly discuss or disclose negative news about a firm.

RavenPack determines NIP using machine learning by creating a training set for the news impact classifier using tick data for a test set of large cap companies.
RavenPack then gauges the news impact of each news flash by measuring the stock price volatility of the company relative to the average stock price volatility of other companies in the two hours following the news flash.
The classifier is trained to predict whether the news impact is high or low based on the language used by journalists in news flashes.
NIP is an ex-ante measure of the amount of stock return volatility that a given news item is likely to have on the market, and it has been used by recent studies to capture the news impact of press releases (Cziraki et al. 2021) and Federal Open Market Committee (FOMC) announcements (Dambra et al. 2024).
