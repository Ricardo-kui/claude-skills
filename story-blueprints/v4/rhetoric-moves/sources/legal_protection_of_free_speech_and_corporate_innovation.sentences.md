# legal_protection_of_free_speech_and_corporate_innovation 句子库存

## introduction
<!-- para 2 -->
Consequential negative information about firms commonly reaches investors from civil society actors such as journalists, NGOs, and employees.
The disclosure of this information, like environmental violations, labor safety concerns, or accounting fraud, imposes reputational costs on the firm by depressing valuations, provoking stakeholder responses, and pressuring managers to change behavior (Dyck et al., 2010; Konar and Cohen, 1997; Jiang and Kong, 2024; Campa, 2018; Hamilton, 1995).
Firms cannot directly control the discovery of negative information, but they can raise the cost of dissemination.
A common strategy is to suppress free expression of civil society actors using threats of defamation suits, or Strategic Lawsuits Against Public Participation (SLAPPs).
SLAPPs burden civil society actors with litigation costs and deter them from disseminating their findings (Canan and Pring, 1988).
<!-- para 3 -->
In this paper, I study how firms adapt when the cost of producing negative information for civil society actors is exogenously lowered.
I examine the staggered adoption of anti-SLAPP statutes across U.S. states and estimate its effect on firm-level innovation, including R&D intensity, patent applications, and new product announcements.
Even though anti-SLAPP statutes do not directly target corporate actions, they are aimed at protecting First Amendment rights.
In doing so, they provide procedural safeguards for the free expression of civil society critics, allowing meritless SLAPP suits to be dismissed early and allowing critics to recover their legal costs (Aimonetti and Talley, 2021).
Other channels of information, such as voluntary disclosures and compliance requirements, often reveal only selective content that suppresses bad news (Graham et al., 2005; Bloomfield, 2008; Loughran and McDonald, 2011; Huang et al., 2014).
Civil society critics have no concealment incentives, and anti-SLAPP statutes provide them with legal protection.
<!-- para 4 -->
Anti-SLAPP adoption changes the information environment surrounding technology choices.
Criticism of firms by civil society actors, where it rises to a matter of public interest, mostly concerns product and labor safety and the environmental impact of their operations (Pring and Canan, 1996).
These issues are intrinsically linked to the technologies that firms deploy.
Without protection, critics face prohibitive litigation risk when publicizing the social costs of an incumbent technology.
Its demerits cannot be credibly aired, and the adoption of a lower social-cost alternative cannot be independently evaluated.
Firms deploying the incumbent therefore face no reputational penalty, and the returns to developing alternatives are depressed, even for firms with high absorptive capacity.
Anti-SLAPP adoption changes this equilibrium.
It makes it difficult for firms to suppress negative information about incumbent technology, and increases the expected reputational cost of using it.
Firms with high absorptive capacity can transition to new technology, relying on civil society disclosure to make the transition signal credible.
They do so by patenting alternative technology early, in advance of an industry-wide transition that the new information environment makes likely.
Patenting secures licensing rents and a competitive advantage before rivals can respond.
<!-- para 5 -->
I estimate the effect of adoption on innovation using staggered difference-in-differences models that compare the evolution of innovation among firms headquartered in adopting states with that of firms headquartered in states that never adopted anti-SLAPP statutes, by adoption cohort (Sun and Abraham, 2021). [^1] The models include firm and year fixed effects, which absorb time-invariant state characteristics and common aggregate shocks.
<!-- para 6 -->
I present four sets of results.
First, I show that anti-SLAPP protection changes firm behavior that is consistent with reputation discipline.
This implies that adoption should reduce firms' use of litigation to suppress criticism and produce broader improvements in conduct, with treated firms being better governed and more accountable to stakeholders (Lee et al., 2025; Chen et al., 2026).
I test this with litigation records from the Federal Judicial Center Integrated Database together with Glassdoor ratings and RepRisk incident data.
I show that firms sue less to suppress criticism, and their conduct toward stakeholders improves.
Firms' use of SLAPP suits declines by 0.38 of a standard deviation or 56% of the mean.
In addition, I show that overall Glassdoor ratings improve, as do senior management reputation scores.
Firms' reputation risk related to local pollution and controversial products and services also declines after anti-SLAPP adoption.
These results validate the economic force underlying the main analyses.
<!-- para 7 -->
Second, the adoption of anti-SLAPP statutes causally affects innovation outcomes.
Firms headquartered in treated states file more patent applications compared to firms in the control group of never-adopting states.
The magnitude is economically meaningful, equivalent to 5.6% of a standard deviation (or 19% of the mean of all firms that ever report R&D expenditure).
In states with strong anti-SLAPP statutes, the effect is stronger.
Patent applications increase by 9.8% of a standard deviation (or 33% relative to the sample mean).
I use the Institute for Free Speech's classification of anti-SLAPP statutes (discussed later in the paper) to grade the extent of legal safeguards, designating those with grade A and B ratings as strong.
The innovation response is concentrated among firms that already possess innovative capabilities rather than inducing previously nonpatenting firms to begin patenting.
Adoption has no effect on the extensive margin of patenting and does not induce previously non-patenting firms to file for patents.
<!-- para 8 -->
If firms innovate preemptively for industry-wide technology transitions, the resulting patents should represent genuine technological advances rather than incremental extensions of existing portfolios.
I test this prediction along three dimensions: patent quality, technological scope, and technological direction.
On quality, I use forward citations and find that the increase in patenting is concentrated almost entirely among breakthrough patents. [^2] These post-adoption patents are also more valuable, with anti-SLAPP adoption associated with a 22.4% increase in the estimated private market value of the firm's patent portfolio.
The measure of scope is the share of a firm's patents filed in CPC classes new to its existing portfolio.
Treated firms patent across a broader set of technology classes after adoption, indicating movement into new technological domains rather than incremental deepening of existing expertise.
Finally, since reputational pressure mostly affects high social-cost technologies, adoption should shift innovation toward cleaner alternatives.
Consistent with this, treated firms file more green patents after adoption than firms in control states, an increase equivalent to 14.3% of the standard deviation of the sample distribution of green patents.
<!-- para 9 -->
The third set of results provides evidence on the mechanisms underlying the baseline effects.
The economic argument developed above points to a strategic preemption channel.
The rise in the expected reputational cost of incumbent technology is the operative force.
Firms that are more exposed to civil society scrutiny, because they are consumer-facing or have high environmental and reputational risk, face the largest increase in that cost.
Firms with greater absorptive capacity are best positioned to act on it, as they can identify, develop, and patent alternatives at lower cost.
Therefore, the strategic preemption channel predicts that the innovation response is concentrated among firms that have high reputational exposure and high absorptive capacity.
A financing channel could generate a similar aggregate increase in patenting through a different route.
By strengthening the credibility of outside monitoring, anti-SLAPP protection reduces information frictions between firms and external investors and lowers the cost of equity (Guernsey et al., 2024).
Since innovation is intangible, difficult to collateralize, and disproportionately reliant on external equity, the marginal value of a lower cost of capital is highest for financially constrained firms.
The financing channel therefore predicts that the innovation response is concentrated within that set of firms.
<!-- para 10 -->
The cross-sectional evidence separates the two channels.
The innovation response is con-
<!-- para 11 -->
centrated among firms with high absorptive capacity and reputational exposure, namely those in consumer-facing industries and with high asset intangibility, R&D intensity, and environmental and reputational risk.
This pattern is consistent with capable firms preemptively developing cleaner and technologically novel alternatives in anticipation of heightened reputational pressure.
Moreover, the effect is also concentrated among firms with low financing constraints, measured using the Hadlock and Pierce (2010), Kaplan and Zingales (1997), and Whited and Wu (2006) indices.
This latter result is the opposite of what the financing channel predicts.
In these cross-section analyses, I split firms on the 2000 distribution of each conditioning variable and consider only subsequent adoptions, keeping the partitions plausibly exogenous to treatment. [^3]
<!-- para 12 -->
In the fourth set of results, I use stock market valuations to quantify the economic consequences of anti-SLAPP adoption.
On average, market-to-book value decreases by 3.7% relative to the sample mean among treated firms after adoption.
This is consistent with market pricing reflecting the higher reputational cost of incumbent technology (or higher expected litigation costs).
However, the market-to-book ratios of R&D-intensive and high-intangibility firms increase by approximately 1.5%.
This likely reflects the market's valuation of the rents embedded in their expanded patent portfolios.
These results suggest that anti-SLAPP statutes reallocate firm value toward firms that respond to reputational pressure by innovating.
I find no differential valuation effects from analyst coverage, suggesting that these valuation effects operate through the credibility of civil society criticism rather than through changes in the expansion of formal financial intermediation.
<!-- para 13 -->
In additional results, I ask whether these heterogeneous firm-level responses reshape the distribution of innovative activity within industries.
Earlier results show that anti-SLAPP raises the returns to pre-existing capability.
Therefore, it can widen the innovation efficiency gap between firms.
I show that dispersion in innovation efficiency within industries, measured as revenue per patent, increases after adoption.
I then examine allocative dispersion in a Hsieh and Klenow (2009) framework to see whether the innovation response propagates into real factor allocation.
There is no statistically significant effect in the short run.
Factor markets adjust slowly, and a longer horizon may be needed to observe any effect.
These results indicate that anti-SLAPP protection can raise aggregate innovation without developing broad-based innovation capacity.
<!-- para 14 -->
I conduct a range of robustness checks to ensure the results are not artefacts of methodological choices or institutional details.
The primary threat to identification is a confounder that aligns with adoption timing.
Such a confounder would require an unobserved factor coinciding with each adoption cohort and pushing innovation in the same direction across all adopting state-years.
That is a high bar to clear for an idiosyncratic factor because adoption is staggered over thirty years.
Policy bundling is a possible threat, but I find no systematic clustering of innovation-relevant reforms like R&D tax credits, non-compete enforcement, and securities laws around anti-SLAPP adoptions.
Two features of the setting provide further reassurance.
Exploiting the invalidation of Washington's (2015) and Minnesota's (2017) statutes by their state supreme courts, I show that the results are qualitatively unchanged when these two states are excluded.
The baseline pattern also survives dropping all firms in the most innovation-intensive states (New York, California, Texas, and Massachusetts) and states where anti-SLAPP statutes were modified during the sample period.
This indicates that a handful of large adopters do not drive the effect.
Finally, the results are robust to specification and inference.
Placebo tests with randomly assigned adoption years have no effect, and the results using the Callaway and Sant'Anna (2021) estimator match those of the baseline.
<!-- para 15 -->
This paper makes four contributions.
First, it identifies a microeconomic channel through which protecting the speech of civil society actors fosters innovation.
By limiting firms' ability to suppress adverse information, speech protections raise the expected reputational cost of opaque or low-quality technologies and induce capable firms to shift preemptively toward better alternatives.
The channel operates through strategic preemption, whereby the lower cost of producing adverse information makes innovation-capable firms patent before reputation costs are realized.
This equilibrium-deterrence mechanism provides micro-level evidence for the established macro associations between freedom of expression, institutional quality, and innovation (Acemoglu and Robinson, 2012; Aghion and Howitt, 1992; Acemoglu et al., 2005; Aghion et al., 2013).
<!-- para 16 -->
Second, this paper contributes to the literature on how information about firms reaches markets by studying a channel of information provision that does not originate within the firm.
The disclosure literature focuses on two channels through which information comes from the firm itself, and neither induces the timely revelation of negative information.
Voluntary disclosures are selective by construction and defer bad news (Verrecchia, 1983; Dye, 1985; Jung and Kwon, 1988; Kothari et al., 2009).
Compliance-based disclosure removes managerial discretion, but firms comply with boilerplate content that erodes informational value (Christensen et al., 2021; Hope et al., 2016; Christensen, 2016).
Anti-SLAPP protection instead enables information provision by civil society actors who have no incentive to conceal, and whose willingness to produce adverse information depends on being safe from retaliatory litigation.
This connects to the literature on how outside monitors such as the media and whistleblowers detect misconduct and reduce information asymmetries (Dyck et al., 2008; Heese and Pérez-Cavazos, 2021).
This literature studies monitors as instruments of ex post detection, uncovering misconduct after it occurs.
This paper shows that legal protection of these actors can have ex-ante deterrence of high social-cost choices.
<!-- para 17 -->
Third, this paper contributes to the emerging literature on anti-SLAPP statutes by identifying a distinct economic mechanism.
Existing work interprets anti-SLAPP protection as constraining firms' litigation strategies and improving the information environment, with effects operating through capital market conditions, corporate disclosure, or governance choices (Lee et al., 2025; Guernsey et al., 2024; Chen et al., 2026; Boamah and Li, 2023; Chen et al., 2025).
I show that equilibrium innovation incentives arise through reputational discipline over technology choices.
The predictions are therefore different from those of financing and disclosure channels and have distinct distributional implications.
Anti-SLAPP enhances strategic patenting incentives for firms with pre-existing innovative capability and lower financing constraints.
Consequently, it increases aggregate innovation, but concentrates it among a subset of firms rather than broadening participation.
<!-- para 18 -->
Finally, these findings have implications for the design of innovation policy.
Much of the existing policy toolkit relies on direct financial incentives such as R&D subsidies and tax credits or on intellectual property regimes designed to strengthen appropriability (Bloom et al., 2020b).
While these instruments can stimulate innovation, they also impose costs across the board, crowd out private R&D, and channel resources toward lower-productivity firms (Wallsten, 2000; Shah, 2025).
In contrast, legal safeguards that protect the free flow of information can improve innovation efficiency without direct fiscal intervention.
By raising the reputational cost of continuing with incumbent technologies of high social cost, free speech protections can induce firms to preemptively patent.
This can potentially mitigate adverse selection of lower-productivity firms into innovation.
In this sense, legal institutions governing information transparency function as a form of market infrastructure that enhances innovation efficiency.

## theory
<!-- para 3 -->
Strategic lawsuits against public participation (SLAPPs) are civil claims filed with the intent to deter or punish speech on matters of public concern rather than to prevail on the merits.
As originally documented by Canan and Pring (1988), SLAPPs are commonly brought against individuals or groups who publicly criticize corporate, political, or governmental actions.
Typical claims include defamation, business torts, conspiracy, and violations of constitutional or civil rights (Pring and Canan, 1996).
Although SLAPP lawsuits are often unsuccessful in court, their effectiveness lies in imposing litigation costs, delays, and uncertainty that discourage defendants—and others similarly situated—from continuing to participate in public discourse (Barker, 1993).
The risk of counter-lawsuits has been shown to deter whistleblowers, journalists, and other information intermediaries from surfacing corporate misconduct or underperformance (Pring and Canan, 1996; Dyck et al., 2008).
<!-- para 4 -->
In the United States, anti-SLAPP statutes were enacted across states to introduce procedural mechanisms to mitigate the "chilling effects" on civil society participation.
These statutes typically include expedited dismissal procedures for meritless claims, mandatory stays on discovery pending resolution of the motion, and fee-shifting provisions that reallocate legal costs to unsuccessful plaintiffs (Aimonetti and Talley, 2021).
These provisions reduce the ex-ante legal risk for disclosing information on matters of public concern and the effectiveness of SLAPPs as a strategic tool against civil society criticism.
In that way, adoption of these statutes provides micro-level safeguards for free speech and reduces information asymmetry for investors, regulators, and consumers (Heese and Pérez-Cavazos, 2021).
<!-- para 5 -->
Firms can (and do) deploy litigation strategically to suppress public scrutiny of their products and technologies.
In Energy Transfer LP v.
Greenpeace, a pipeline company filed a defamation lawsuit in North Dakota against Greenpeace for criticizing its environmental conduct.
The jury awarded Energy Transfer over $660 million in damages, a verdict Amnesty International warned could bankrupt Greenpeace and discourage future advocacy.[^4]In Sharper Image Corp. v.
Consumers Union, by contrast, a U.S. District Court in San Francisco dismissed a suit against Consumer Reports for publishing critical product reviews, ruling it was an attempt by the company to intimidate free speech on a matter of public concern.
A crucial difference is that California adopted an anti-SLAPP statute in 1992, whereas North Dakota did not.
These examples illustrate that legal protections for free speech can deter firms from using litigation to silence civil society participation.
<!-- para 6 -->
There is considerable variation in the scope and strength of anti-SLAPP legislation across state jurisdictions.
For instance, California’s anti-SLAPP law applies broadly to “any act … in furtherance of the person’s right of petition or free speech under the United States Constitution or the California Constitution in connection with a public issue” (Cal.
Civ.
Proc.
Code § 425.16).
By contrast, Pennsylvania’s anti-SLAPP statute is significantly narrower, applying solely to individuals who submit petitions to the government concerning environmental matters (Lee et al., 2025).
The Institute for Free Speech (IFS) provides a general framework for the strength of anti-SLAPP statutes based on statutory scope and procedural protections.
Strong statutes (grades A and B) protect all speech on matters of public concern in any forum, with an immediate stay of discovery once an anti-SLAPP motion is filed, and require the losing plaintiff to pay the defendant’s attorney fees.
In contrast, weak statutes protect only speech made directly to a government body or regarding a government proceeding and impose no mandatory fee shifting.
More importantly, weak statutes do not automatically pause discovery, allowing financially stronger SLAPP plaintiffs to stretch the defendant’s resources through a lengthy legal process.
<!-- para 7 -->
Anti-SLAPP statutes limit the scope for forum shopping.
Anti-SLAPP statutes are triggered by where the speech occurred and where the harm is alleged, not simply by where the plaintiff chooses to file.
A firm headquartered in California cannot easily refile a suit in Texas simply because California has anti-SLAPP protections if the underlying speech occurred in California and the alleged harm is to a California-based plaintiff.

In table 1, I provide the years of anti-SLAPP adoption by U.S. states and their strength classifications.
<!-- para 10 -->
Legal and political institutions shape economic performance by reducing uncertainty, constraining opportunistic behavior, and facilitating investment (North and Weingast, 1989; Acemoglu et al., 2005).
The role of legal institutions is to reduce frictions in the flow of information.
When information about firm conduct, performance, and risk circulates freely among market participants, reputational mechanisms operate more effectively, agency problems are mitigated, and resources can be diverted away from low-quality projects and allocated more efficiently.
The macro literature shows that democracies and societies with stronger civil liberties exhibit higher long-run growth and innovation (Scully, 1988; Acemoglu et al., 2019).
Legal institutions influence innovation through financing frictions, labor regulation, taxation, and subsidies (Brown et al., 2013; Bloom et al., 2002, 2013; Akcigit et al., 2022).
<!-- para 11 -->
The microeconomic channels through which information suppression may affect firm behavior are more nuanced.
At the firm level, innovation is particularly sensitive to information frictions.
R&D investments are difficult to collateralize, characterized by long horizons and uncertain payoffs (Hall, 1993; Li, 2011).
As a result, external validation, disclosure, and monitoring play a crucial role in disciplining managerial decisions and shaping innovative activity.
A broad literature shows that stronger disclosure regimes, media scrutiny, and external monitoring reduce agency problems and increase innovation output (Verrecchia, 2001; Dyck et al., 2008; Brown and Martinsson, 2019; Gupta et al., 2020).
At the same time, firms may strategically withhold or delay information about innovative activities when disclosure increases competitive pressure or erodes private returns (Bhattacharya and Ritter, 1983; Anton and Yao, 2002).
Whether innovation is stimulated or distorted, therefore, depends on how institutions shape the incentives to reveal or suppress information.
<!-- para 12 -->
Innovation outcomes depend not only on the level of R&D investment but also on how effectively innovative resources are allocated across firms and projects.
Dispersion in firm-level productivity reflects distortions that allow inefficient firms or low-quality projects to persist, thereby lowering aggregate efficiency (Hsieh and Klenow, 2009).
Recent work shows that innovation-led growth is driven primarily by incumbent firms, and that policies are most effective when they improve the selection and discipline of innovative activity rather than merely increasing aggregate R&D spending (Akcigit and Kerr, 2018; Acemoglu et al., 2018).
Because information frictions play a central role in determining which projects are pursued and which firms innovate, legal institutions that affect information disclosure may influence innovation not only at the level but also in the distribution of productivity across firms.
<!-- para 13 -->
Taken together, this literature suggests that speech-protection laws, such as anti-SLAPP statutes, may boost innovation by reducing firms' ability to suppress public criticism.
The greater likelihood of disclosing negative social costs can discipline technology choice and lead to higher innovation outputs.
This leads to the following hypothesis:
<!-- para 14 -->
H1.
Anti-SLAPP statutes increase firms' innovative output, measured by the number of patent applications.

## methods
<!-- para 3 -->
The sample consists of all Compustat firms from 1990 to 2024.
I exclude all firms that have missing observations on balance sheet variables and firms headquartered outside the United States.
This leads to a sample of 164,464 firm-year observations from 15,958 unique firms.
I augment the Compustat data with patent information from the Kogan et al. (2017) database, which provides information on patents filed by U.S. corporations, including CPC classes, economic value, and forward citations.
I restrict the sample to firms that have ever reported R&D expenses.
This is the estimation sample with 72,354 firm-year observations across 7,796 firms.
I further augment information on new product announcements from RavenPack.
Information on analyst coverage comes from I/B/E/S.
Reputational-risk data come from RepRisk, and environmental-risk measures come from (Hassan et al., 2019).
<!-- para 4 -->
For industry-level data, I use output, labor inputs, productivity, and value-added data from the United States Bureau of Economic Analysis (BEA).
This information is available from 1997 onward.
Industry-level productivity data is obtained from Dispersion Statistics on Productivity (DiSP) provided by the United States Census Bureau.

I consider all anti-SLAPP statutes adopted between 1990 and 2019.
During this period, there was no federal anti-SLAPP statute, and 32 states and the District of Columbia progressively adopted these statutes. [^5] The strength of the anti-SLAPP laws is determined using data from the IFS, a non-governmental organization that evaluates the protection provided by each state’s anti-SLAPP statute.

I measure innovation across three distinct stages of the innovation pipeline, capturing input, output, and commercialization.
<!-- para 8 -->

It measures innovation inputs.
It has a mean (median) of 0.321 (0.026) and a standard deviation of 0.503.

Reduced effectiveness of litigation-based suppression may encourage firms to invest more in research and development.
At the same time, these laws may improve the productivity of existing innovation inputs, and firms increase innovation output without increased R&D expenditure.

Patent applications filed by a firm in year t measures innovation output.
It has a mean (median) of 44 (1) and a standard deviation of 149.
It is a standard innovation outcome that is salient in this context.
If anti-SLAPP affects innovation through the strategic preemption channel, firms will file more patent applications.

Beyond patent counts, I use four measures to assess the quality, direction, and technological scope of patent output.
The patent value is constructed following Kogan et al. (2017) as the natural log of the nominal value of patents filed by firm i up to year t.
This market-based measure captures the economic significance of a firm's patent portfolio beyond what forward citations alone can identify.
<!-- para 11 -->
Breakthrough patents are defined as those with forward citations exceeding the 75th percentile of the citation distribution within the same CPC class and filing-year cohort.
An effect concentrated among breakthrough patents suggests that the innovation response to anti-SLAPP adoption reflects paradigm-shifting technological advances rather than incremental extensions of existing portfolios.
<!-- para 12 -->
The new CPC class shares measure the fraction of a firm's patents in year t that fall into CPC technology classes in which the firm had no prior patents, capturing whether firms are broadening their technological domains rather than deepening existing areas of expertise.
<!-- para 13 -->

An increase in green patenting following anti-SLAPP adoption is consistent with the reputational discipline mechanism, as environmental technologies represent the most salient domain in which civil society criticism can alter firms' technology choices (Li et al., 2024).

All three sub-indices are continuous scores where higher values indicate greater reputational exposure.
These measures are particularly well suited to this setting because they are informed by public criticism from journalists, NGOs, and community groups that anti-SLAPP statutes are designed to protect.

To estimate the causal effect of free speech protection on corporate innovation, anti-SLAPP adoption must constitute a plausibly exogenous increase in speech protection that is not itself motivated by innovation policy objectives.
These statutes are typically adopted through lobbying by journalists, NGOs, and civil society groups — not by corporations seeking to enhance their innovative capacity.
They neither expand R&D incentives nor alter intellectual property rights or regulatory approval requirements.
Legislative motivation for anti-SLAPP is, therefore, orthogonal to firms' innovation decisions.
<!-- para 56 -->
The empirical design exploits the staggered adoption of anti-SLAPP statutes across U.S. states between 1992 and 2019.
I compare firms headquartered in states that adopted anti-SLAPP statutes during the sample period to firms headquartered in states that never adopted such statutes.
Traditional two-way fixed-effects (TWFE) DiD estimators can produce biased estimates when treatment is adopted at different times across units, because TWFE implicitly uses already-treated units as controls for later-treated units, contaminating the comparison when treatment effects are heterogeneous across cohorts or over time (Goodman-Bacon, 2021; Sun and Abraham, 2021).
<!-- para 57 -->
To address this, I implement the interaction-weighted estimator of Sun and Abraham (2021) on the original (unstacked) firm-year panel.
A treatment cohort is defined by the calendar year in which a firm's headquarters state first enacts an anti-SLAPP statute.
The estimator interacts relative-time indicators (leads and lags around adoption) with cohort membership dummies, recovering cohort-specific treatment effects for each relative period.
These cohort-period estimates are then aggregated into an overall average treatment effect on the treated (ATT) using cohort-share weights, ensuring that the estimated coefficients reflect comparisons exclusively between each treated cohort and the never-treated control group.
The analysis focuses on an 11-year window centered on each state's adoption year ( $[-5,+5]$).
All regressions include firm and year fixed effects.
Since treatment is assigned at the state level, standard errors are clustered by headquarters state (Imbens and Wooldridge, 2009).
I estimate the following staggered difference-in-differences model:
<!-- para 58 -->
$$ \#\mathrm{Patents}_{it}=\sum_{g\in\mathcal{G}}\sum_{\tau\neq0}\beta_{g,\tau}\cdot\mathbf{1}\{G_{i}=g\}\cdot\mathbf{1}\{t-g=\tau\}+\rho_{i}+\lambda_{t}+\varepsilon_{it} $$
<!-- para 59 -->
(7) where  $\#Patents_{it}$ is the number of patent applications filed by firm i for year  $t, G_i$ denotes the cohort (i.e., the first year in which the headquarters state of firm i adopts an anti-SLAPP statute), and  $\tau$ indexes the time of events relative to adoption.  $\rho_i$ and  $\lambda_t$ are firm and year fixed effects, respectively.
The coefficients  $\beta_{g,\tau}$ capture cohort-specific treatment effects at each relative time  $\tau$, with  $\tau = 0$ omitted as the reference period.
I use the Sun-Abraham estimator for the baseline tests and present the Callaway and Sant'Anna estimates as robustness (Callaway and Sant'Anna, 2021; Sun and Abraham, 2021)
<!-- para 60 -->
To examine whether anti-SLAPP legislation affects different dimensions of firm innovation, I estimate Equation (7) using the following dependent variables in turn: (i) R&D Intensity, (ii) Patent Applications, and (iii) New Product Announcements.
The specification, fixed effects, and standard error clustering remain identical across these models.
<!-- para 61 -->
Anti-SLAPP statutes are considered plausibly exogenous because their enactment is driven by legal and political debates over free speech rather than by the economic outcomes studied in this paper.
For example, firms have few incentives to lobby for treatment or to endogenously sort into it.
State-level time-invariant factors can simultaneously affect innovation outcomes and anti-SLAPP adoption.
I account for that using a within-firm estimator.
Therefore, the estimates from equation 7 can be interpreted as the average treatment effect on the treated (ATT).
Identification relies on the assumption that, absent anti-SLAPP adoption, treated and control states would have experienced parallel trends in innovation outcomes, conditional on state- and year-fixed effects.
The validity of this assumption is assessed directly when presenting the difference-in-differences estimates with evidence of parallel trends.

The strategic preemption channel predicts concentration among firms that can act on the higher reputational cost of incumbent technology, namely those with the absorptive capacity, higher reputational exposure, and lower financing constraints.
On the other hand, a financing channel, operating through a lower cost of equity, predicts the response among financially constrained firms, with no cross-sectional difference in reputational exposure.
In cross-section tests based on sample partitions, I provide evidence towards these mechanisms.
<!-- para 30 -->
The choice of a conditioning year involves a trade-off.
Conditioning on the year of anti-SLAPP adoption would be the natural approach, since it fixes firm characteristics immediately before treatment.
However, adoption years are staggered, and never-adopting states have no counterpart date.
Fixing a single calendar year for all firms allows comparability, but at the cost of measuring characteristics further from treatment for some cohorts.
Among candidate years, 1990 is pre-treatment for every adoption in the sample but it confines the partition to firms already public at the start of the period.
This would exclude much of the software, biotechnology, and internet-era entries that account for a substantial share of subsequent patenting.
Alternatively, the year 2000 is a pragmatic compromise.
It precedes 20 of the 33 adoptions in the sample, and it captures the broader cross-section of R&D-intensive firms that entered during the 1990s.
The trade-off is that cohorts adopting before 2000 and firms entering public markets after 2000 are excluded from the estimation sample. [^15]

A potential concern with any staggered difference-in-differences design is that unobserved state-level factors may simultaneously drive both the adoption of anti-SLAPP statutes and changes in innovation outcomes.
I address this concern through a combination of institutional evidence, policy-bundling analysis, and additional fixed-effects specifications.
<!-- para 83 -->
I begin by examining whether anti-SLAPP statutes were adopted with innovation or economic objectives in mind.
I review media reports, legislative histories, and policy debates around each state's adoption date, searching for references to entrepreneurship, financing, innovation, and venture activity.
I find no systematic patterns.
Anti-SLAPP statutes consistently emerged from free-speech advocacy groups comprising journalists, civil liberties organizations, and environmental activists, rather than from pro-business or innovation policy agendas.
This institutional evidence supports the interpretation that anti-SLAPP adoption is orthogonal to states' innovation policy environments.
<!-- para 84 -->
The baseline specification with firm- and year-fixed effects absorbs time-invariant state characteristics and common aggregate shocks.
However, it does not account for time-varying state-level confounders like contemporaneous changes in fiscal incentives, political composition, or other legal reforms.
Two features of the setting mitigate this concern.
First, the staggered adoption spans three decades and 32 states, so any omitted-variable explanation would require a confounder that systematically aligns with each adoption cohort's timing and generates effects in the same direction across all innovation outcomes.
Second, Table A2 provides direct evidence on policy bundling.
I document changes in R&D tax credits, non-compete agreements, and intrastate crowdfunding exemptions within a  $[-2, +2]$ window around each state's adoption of anti-SLAPP statutes.
There is no systematic clustering of these entrepreneurship-relevant policy changes around anti-SLAPP enactment dates, suggesting that the statutes were not adopted as part of broader pro-innovation legislative packages.
<!-- para 85 -->
Firms relocating their headquarters are unlikely to significantly confound the results.
There are two possibilities: more innovative firms moving into adopting states, and less innovative firms moving out.
In the first case, the estimates would be upward-biased only if firms systematically relocated to states in anticipation of the adoption of anti-SLAPP statutes.
Since these statutes arise from free-speech advocacy rather than innovation policy, such anticipatory sorting is unlikely to be systematic.
In the second case, if firms with weaker innovation capacity exit adopting states after treatment, it could inflate the ATT.
However, this would make it harder to detect patterns of heterogeneity.
A compositional upgrading would produce uniformly higher patenting across all firm types, but the results do not support this.
More generally, headquarters relocations are infrequent and primarily driven by considerations that are orthogonal to free speech protection laws, such as tax environment, labor market conditions, and proximity to business services (Pirinsky and Wang, 2006; Strauss-Kahn and Vives, 2009).
<!-- para 86 -->
Finally, I assign treatment based on the state of incorporation.
I find no statistically significant effect of anti-SLAPP adoptions on R&D intensity, patent applications, or new product announcements.
A large fraction of public firms are incorporated in Delaware, where they neither do business nor face monitoring by civil society.
The absence of incorporation-state effects suggests that the innovation response arises from exposure to civil society monitoring rather than from incorporation decisions.

## discussion
<!-- para 2 -->
I examine how legal protections for free speech influence corporate innovation.
I argue that stronger speech protections, implemented through anti-SLAPP statutes, alter the informational environment in which firms operate (Lee et al., 2025).
By limiting firms' ability to suppress criticism through strategic litigation, these statutes make it harder to externalize the social costs of incumbent technologies.
The resulting rise in the reputational and market costs of continuing to use such technologies induces firms with low adoption costs to innovate.
There is no significant effect on the extensive margin of patenting.
<!-- para 3 -->
Using the staggered enactment of anti-SLAPP statutes across U.S. states, I show that firms in adopting states file more patent applications, with effects stronger in states with stronger protection and among firms with higher asset intangibility, R&D intensity, and environmental risk.
On average, affected firms lose market value, while R&D-intensive and high-intangibility firms gain, reflecting the market's recognition of their comparative advantage in innovation.
At the industry level, the dispersion of innovation efficiency widens after adoption, as innovating firms gain over those that do not.
Free speech protection therefore operates through selection, encouraging more efficient innovation rather than broad-based participation.
<!-- para 4 -->
These results speak to a broader debate about how policy can foster innovation without direct intervention.
The central insight of this paper is that institutions that protect civic liberties can shape firm behavior in ways that conventional policy instruments cannot replicate.
While R&D subsidies and financial incentives are commonly deployed to promote innovation, these tools risk crowding out private investment and channeling resources toward low-productivity firms (Wallsten, 2000; Shah, 2025).
Free speech protections do not lower the cost of innovation for all firms indiscriminately.
Anti-SLAPP statutes raise the reputational cost of continuing with high social-cost technology, effectively tilting the competitive landscape toward more innovative firms.
The result is a market-driven selection process in which stronger innovators gain, and weaker firms are exposed to greater scrutiny.
Strengthening civil institutions can thus offer a less distortionary and more targeted complement to conventional innovation policy.
