# li_2026_the_impact_of_supply_base_geographical_distance_and_ 句子库存

## introduction
<!-- para 2 -->
Globalization, diversification of customer needs, cost pressures, and the pursuit of external knowledge have prompted firms to source from a large number of suppliers from around the world, which increases the geographical complexity of their supply chains (Ateş et al., 2022; Bode and Wagner, 2015; Choi and Krause, 2006).
In turn, supply chain geographical complexity has become a source of product quality risks across industries (Marucheck et al., 2011).
In the automobile industry, which has one of the most globalized supply chains, approximately 47% of recalls are associated with suppliers (Astvansh et al., 2022a).
In the food industry, the horsemeat scandal highlighted the risks of final products being contaminated by geographically dispersed supply chains (Taylor, 2019).
In this case, food manufacturers and retailers in the United Kingdom and Europe were unaware that the meat they used in their products (e.g., burgers, lasagna, sauces) was horsemeat sold to them as beef by their foreign suppliers.
Cases such as these have prompted academic research into how supply chain geographical complexity affects firm outcomes, including product quality risks (Bray et al., 2019; Noh et al., 2025; Steven and Britto, 2016).
<!-- para 3 -->
Prior research has produced valuable insights on the relationship between supply chain geographical complexity and firm outcomes (Attari et al., 2025; Lu and Shang, 2017; Sharma et al., 2020).
However, this stream of research has two limitations.
First, prior work does not adequately theorize geographical complexity and measures it using geographical distance and geographical dispersion interchangeably (Dong et al., 2020; Lu and Shang, 2017; Sharma et al., 2020).
We argue that these are conceptually distinct dimensions with potentially different effects on product quality.
Geographical distance reflects the spatial separation between a focal firm and its suppliers; it increases the challenges of managing suppliers due to factors related to access, communication, and transportation.
In contrast, geographical dispersion captures the diversity of supplier locations across multiple countries/regions; it exacerbates challenges in coordinating with suppliers due to heterogeneity in institutional environments and quality standards.
Recognizing this distinction is necessary to improve supply chain design for better firm outcomes.
<!-- para 4 -->
Second, prior empirical research on supply chain geographical complexity has mostly been conducted in research contexts that are Western, educated, industrialized, rich, and democratic (WEIRD) (Henrich et al., 2010).
Firms from WEIRD countries source down the institutional gradient and thus geographical distance increases risks for firms (Steven and Britto, 2016).
However, similar risks may not affect firms from non-WEIRD countries if they source up the institutional gradient or source from a combination of domestic and foreign suppliers from WEIRD countries.
By failing to consider differences in institutional contexts between WEIRD and non-WEIRD countries, research on supply chain geographical complexity risks generating erroneous implications for theory and practice.
<!-- para 5 -->
To address these two gaps, we distinguish between geographical distance and dispersion and empirically examine their effects on product quality risks for Chinese automobile manufacturers sourcing from domestic and foreign suppliers.
We hypothesize that geographical distance increases quality risks by heightening challenges in monitoring distant suppliers.
In contrast, geographical dispersion increases quality risks due to the difficulties in coordinating consistent quality standards across heterogeneous supplier locations.
We also argue that vertical integration attenuates these effects by enhancing a firm's control over its supply base, which mitigates the monitoring and coordination challenges associated with supply chain geographical complexity [^1].
We test our hypotheses using data from the Chinese automobile industry, in which both domestic sourcing (local suppliers account for approximately 85%) and foreign sourcing (about 15% are foreign suppliers) are prevalent.
Further, we ascertain the plausibility of the mechanisms we hypothesized through additional tests.
<!-- para 6 -->
Our findings indicate that the geographical distance of Chinese automakers from direct suppliers is not significantly related to automobile quality risks, but the geographical dispersion of the supply base is positively related to automobile quality risks.
Contrary to our expectations, we find that vertical integration strengthens the relationship between geographical dispersion and product quality risks.
Our post hoc analyses support our theoretical arguments.
Consistent with our argument that geographical distance increases supplier monitoring challenges, we find that the relationship between geographical distance and quality risks weakens when firms source from suppliers located in countries with strong regulatory quality, thereby reducing the need for supplier monitoring.
Furthermore, we find that the positive relationship between geographical dispersion and quality risks is weakened by supplier quality certifications, which align quality standards across supplier locations and reduce the coordination challenges.
<!-- para 7 -->
This study makes three contributions to supply chain management scholarship.
First, by disentangling the two dimensions of geographical complexity (i.e., distance and dispersion), we provide a nuanced view of the impact of supply chain geographical attributes on firm outcomes.
While prior studies emphasize the detrimental impact of distance on product quality (Bray et al., 2019), our findings show that in an emerging-market context, dispersion, rather than distance, is significantly related to product quality risk.
Our findings underscore the importance of disentangling distance and dispersion dimensions in geographical complexity and theorizing their distinct effects on supply chain risks.
<!-- para 8 -->
Second, we contribute to the debate on the limited generalizability of theories developed in WEIRD contexts to non-WEIRD settings (Barkema et al., 2015; Pitesa and Gelfand, 2023; Wickert et al., 2024).
Prior research in supply chain management, largely based on developed-market manufacturers, has often treated geographical distance as a source of monitoring difficulties, coordination costs, and quality risks (Bray et al., 2019; Steven and Britto, 2016; Steven et al., 2014).
In contrast, we show that this prediction is not generalizable to emerging-market manufacturers.
In this context, geographical distance is not significantly associated with product quality risk, and supplier-
<!-- para 9 -->
country regulatory quality mitigates the risks of geographical distance.
These findings suggest that distant suppliers can also provide access to stronger institutional environments, more mature quality systems, and external enforcement mechanisms.
Thus, our study echoes recent studies (i.e., Carter et al., 2024) in highlighting the need for supply chain scholars to treat empirical context as theoretically consequential and to validate supply chain management predictions in non-WEIRD settings, rather than assuming that findings from WEIRD contexts are universally applicable.
<!-- para 10 -->
Third, our findings advance the emerging research on supply chain antecedents of product quality risks.
Existing research has primarily examined product quality risk through the lens of supplier characteristics (Zou et al., 2023), sourcing strategies (Gray et al., 2011; Steven et al., 2014), supply network structure (Kalaignanam et al., 2017), and location choices (Noh et al., 2025; Steven and Britto, 2016).
We extend this research by showing that the geographic configuration of the supply base also matters for product quality risks.
Specifically, our study identifies geographical dispersion of the supply base as an important antecedent of product quality risk.
<!-- para 12 -->
The simplest supply chain consists of a buyer and a supplier who frequently exchange materials, capital, and information.
However, a buyer may have multiple direct suppliers, who in turn may have multiple lower-tier suppliers (Mena et al., 2013).
Thus, a simple supply chain can evolve into a complex network comprising multiple companies (nodes) and multiple sets of buyer-supplier dyads (ties) (Pathak et al., 2007).
The complexity of a firm's supply chain depends on the number of suppliers, their diversity, and their interconnectedness (Choi and Krause, 2006).
Suppliers that are geographically distant or dispersed add another layer of complexity (Ateş and Luzzini, 2024).
<!-- para 13 -->
Prior research has shown that sourcing from distant suppliers increases physical transportation costs and introduces lead-time uncertainty (Bode and Wagner, 2015).
Furthermore, when suppliers are located in different locations, supply chain collaboration may be adversely affected (Gray et al., 2011; Pillai and Bindroo, 2020).
Therefore, research has devoted considerable attention to the effect of supply chain geographical complexity on firm outcomes, as summarized in Table A1 of the E-Companion.
A review of these studies reveals two insights.
<!-- para 14 -->
First, prior research has not adequately distinguished geographical distance and geographical dispersion.
Prior research has predominantly examined geographical distance or dispersion in isolation; only three studies (out of 14 papers summarized in Table A1) have included both.
Further, studies have used geographical complexity constructs (e.g., spatial complexity) but have empirically tested them using distance or dispersion measures.
We contend that geographical distance and dispersion are theoretically distinct and may shape supply chain risk in different ways.
Therefore, treating them as such in future research can not only help improve conceptual and empirical rigor but also generate more useful insights for theory and practice.
<!-- para 15 -->
Second, the studies examining how geographical complexity affects supply chain risks have all been set in WEIRD contexts, except for one study set in Japan.
There is growing recognition that theories and findings from WEIRD countries need to be assessed in non-WEIRD countries to confirm their generalizability and usefulness to practice (Barkema et al., 2015; Pitesa and Gelfand, 2023; Wickert et al., 2024).
<!-- para 16 -->
In this study, we seek to advance supply chain management scholarship by theoretically distinguishing between geographical distance and geographical dispersion, and empirically testing our hypotheses in China, a non-WEIRD context.
<!-- para 18 -->
Although geographical distance and geographical dispersion are related, they capture different aspects of complexity.
Geographical distance refers to the physical distance between the buyer firm and its direct suppliers (Bray et al., 2019); geographical dispersion represents the extent to which suppliers are spread across different areas (Lorentz et al., 2012).
As Figure 1 illustrates, the average geographical distance between buyers and suppliers is the same for supply bases A and B (both equal to d).
However, suppliers are more concentrated in supply base A and more dispersed in supply base B.
As a result, the challenges of managing supply base A are different from those of managing supply base B.
Thus, theoretically distinguishing between geographical distance and geographical dispersion of a supply base and assessing their empirical effects are important.

<!-- para 24 -->
Previous studies have predominantly argued for and found a negative association between geographical distance and supply chain performance (Adhikary et al., 2020; Handley and Benton Jr, 2013; Sharma et al., 2020).
This is because geographical distance between buyers and suppliers increases information asymmetry and transaction uncertainty, leading to supplier opportunism and challenges related to monitoring (Attari et al., 2025; Chedid et al., 2021; Falcone et al., 2025).
For example, Handley and Gray (2013) found that distant suppliers increase transaction uncertainty and information-processing needs, thereby increasing the costs of managing and monitoring contracts.
Adhikary et al. (2020) found that the geographical distance between buyers and their direct suppliers increases monitoring challenges and transaction uncertainties, thereby reducing the environmental performance of buyer firms.
Similarly, geographical distance from suppliers reduces information sharing and thus is negatively related to buyer firms' innovation performance (Pillai and Bindroo, 2020; Sharma et al., 2020) and international business performance (Sharma et al., 2022).
In short, this research stream shows that geographical distance increases supply chain risk primarily due to challenges in monitoring distant suppliers.
<!-- para 25 -->
Previous studies have noted that geographically dispersed [^2] supply chains increase coordination challenges (Lorentz et al., 2012), elevate risks of inconsistent inputs due to differences in quality standards (Adhikary et al., 2020), and raise uncertainties related to foreign trade (Noh et al., 2025).
Furthermore, they increase the difficulties of integrating information from suppliers and thus intensify the uncertainties related to global operations (Pillai and Bindroo, 2020).
In short, the geographical dispersion of the supply base can increase risk by exacerbating the challenges of coordinating across institutional contexts with differing quality standards.
<!-- para 27 -->
Product quality risk is a type of supply chain risk and has attracted growing interest in recent years (Chen et al., 2022; Chen et al., 2025) because quality failures can trigger outcomes such as product recalls that incur high costs for both the recalling firm and society (Astvansh et al., 2022b; Ball et al., 2025; Chakravarty et al., 2022; Mayo et al., 2022).
As supply chain partners influence product quality, scholars have studied product quality risks from a supply chain perspective (Mukherjee and Sinha, 2018; Zou et al., 2023).
Collectively, these studies highlight supply chain characteristics as key antecedents of product quality risk.
For example, Kalaignanam et al. (2017) found that firms embedded in high-density, low-centrality supply networks are more likely to experience product recalls.
Zou et al. (2023) found that the relationship between supply base R&D intensity and the likelihood of product recalls is U-shaped.
<!-- para 28 -->
Building on research on the supply chain risks of geographical complexity (Choi and Krause, 2006), recent research has begun to examine the supply chain and outsourcing antecedents of product quality risks (as summarized in Table A2 of the E-companion).
For example, Bray et al. (2019) showed that the geographical distance from direct suppliers is positively related to automobile quality risks in the U.S. market.
In contrast, Steven and Britto (2016) found that outsourcing to emerging markets increases the likelihood of recalls.
In a similar vein, Noh et al. (2025) showed that generic drugs produced in India result in significantly more serious adverse events for U.S. pharmaceutical companies.
<!-- para 29 -->

<!-- para 30 -->
In parallel with research on the geographical complexity of the supply base, this stream of research has also used data from companies in WEIRD countries.
Applying findings from studies in WEIRD countries to non-WEIRD countries can be problematic because Western firms sourcing from distant suppliers in non-WEIRD countries may face greater monitoring and coordination challenges than non-WEIRD suppliers sourcing from WEIRD countries.
Therefore, we aim to shed more light on the effects of the geographical distance and dispersion of the supply base on product quality risks for buyer firms in emerging markets that source from both local and foreign suppliers.

## theory
<!-- para 2 -->
Consistent with previous studies (Choi and Krause, 2006; Dong et al., 2020; Zou et al., 2023), we focus on the supply base (direct suppliers) rather than the entire supply network (both direct and indirect suppliers) because firms actively manage and maintain direct control over their supply base.
Moreover, direct suppliers act as agents to monitor indirect and lower-tier suppliers (Wilhelm et al., 2016).
<!-- para 3 -->
We theorize that supply base geographical complexity is positively related to product quality risks because it heightens the challenges of monitoring and coordinating with suppliers.
However, we posit that geographical distance and geographical dispersion will trigger distinct aspects of these challenges.
Specifically, geographical distance increases information asymmetry.
As a result, a focal buyer faces increased difficulties in monitoring suppliers and becomes vulnerable to supplier opportunism.
In contrast, geographical dispersion exacerbates coordination challenges: a focal buyer must harmonize standards, synchronize adjustments, and resolve quality problems across a dispersed supply base.
Thus, geographical distance and dispersion increase product quality risks through different mechanisms.
Further, we suggest that vertical integration (i.e., sourcing from internal suppliers) can increase supply base transparency and mitigate coordination and monitoring challenges, thereby reducing product quality risks arising from the supply base's geographical distance and dispersion.
We present these relationships in Figure 2 and theoretically develop them in the following section.
<!-- para 6 -->
We posit that the geographical distance between a buyer firm and its direct suppliers increases product quality risks by raising the challenges of monitoring and safeguarding supplier quality (Bray et al., 2019; Handley and Gray, 2013).
According to transaction cost economics (TCE) (Coase, 1937; Ketokivi and Mahoney, 2020), the geographical distance between a firm and its suppliers increases dyadic behavioral uncertainty.
If suppliers are located in close proximity, buyer firms can inspect their plants more frequently, interact more closely with supplier personnel, and identify signals of potential product risks before defects propagate downstream (Ball et al., 2017).
As supplier locations become more distant, buyers face greater difficulty monitoring supplier operations, including conducting site visits, observing quality-related efforts, and providing timely technical assistance.
Therefore, geographical distance increases information asymmetry: suppliers possess superior knowledge of their production processes, quality deviations, and corrective actions, while buyers rely on less frequent audits of supplier quality.
Such information asymmetry might be exploited by suppliers to reduce input quality or to underinvest in quality when these actions are difficult for the buyer to observe (Steven and Britto, 2016).
As evidenced by the widespread recalls of generic medications such as Ranitidine, buyer firms in the West were unable to detect carcinogenic impurities in a timely manner during the production of drugs in India and China (Food and Drug Administration, 2020).
<!-- para 7 -->
Second, geographical distance introduces logistical complexities that heighten product quality risks.
From a TCE perspective, geographical distance from the supply base increases transaction uncertainty by exposing material exchanges to greater transportation, handling, and environmental contingencies (Bray et al., 2019).
During long-distance transportation, components and materials are more likely to pass through multiple logistics interfaces and face varying weather conditions, such as high heat and humidity.
In turn, these pose quality risks to final products, especially when components require specific conditions during transit.
Distance also makes it more difficult for buyer firms to verify whether the damage occurred before delivery, during transportation, or after receipt, thereby complicating the assignment of responsibility and the implementation of corrective action.
If damage to materials and components is not identified before assembly into the final product, risks to the final product may be considerable, especially when suppliers are located far away.
Thus, we hypothesize:
<!-- para 8 -->
**H1: Buyers’ geographical distance from their direct suppliers is positively related to product quality risks.**
<!-- para 10 -->
Although the geographical dispersion of the supply base may also pose supplier-monitoring challenges, we posit that it primarily creates product quality risks by increasing coordination costs across heterogeneous suppliers.
In TCE terms, dispersion increases the likelihood of maladaptation because disturbances cannot be resolved through simple bilateral adjustments; instead, the focal firm must coordinate multiple suppliers operating under heterogeneous local conditions.
This is associated with higher product quality risks for two reasons.
<!-- para 11 -->
First, geographical dispersion often entails significant cultural and institutional differences, which can create misalignments in quality management expectations.
The quality management literature has documented that cultural differences can influence quality practices and quality outcomes (Kull and Wacker, 2010; Naor et al., 2008).
When suppliers are located across diverse geographic regions, differences in communication styles, work norms, problem-solving routines, and attitudes toward quality control may make it difficult for the buyer to establish a common understanding of quality requirements.
For example, what constitutes an acceptable tolerance level, a reportable defect, or an urgent corrective action may vary across national or regional contexts (Luo, 2008).
These differences increase the cost of specifying, communicating, and enforcing quality expectations (Gupta and Gupta, 2019).
From a TCE perspective, such variation across contexts increases coordination costs because the buyer must devote greater managerial attention to aligning supplier interpretations of quality standards and ensuring that suppliers respond consistently to quality-related contingencies.
<!-- para 12 -->
Second, differences in regulatory regimes and quality standards across countries can further exacerbate quality risks.
Prior research on global sourcing and product recalls suggests that sourcing from regions with weaker quality benchmarks or less reliable enforcement can increase the likelihood that supplied components fail to meet required specifications (Steven and Britto, 2016; Steven et al., 2014).
Suppliers located in different regions may be subject to different certification systems, enforcement practices, inspection routines, and industry norms.
Advanced economies often have more stringent quality regulations and stronger enforcement systems, whereas some emerging market contexts may have less rigorous enforcement of quality standards (Noh et al., 2025).
When a firm sources components from suppliers operating under heterogeneous quality regimes, the buyer must reconcile inconsistent external standards with its own internal quality requirements.
Thus, inconsistent quality standards across supplier locations may lead to variation in input quality, increasing the likelihood that defective components enter the production process and eventually affect final product quality (Adhikary et al., 2020).
Thus, we hypothesize:
<!-- para 13 -->
**H2: Buyers’ supply base geographical dispersion is positively related to product quality risks.**
<!-- para 15 -->
Vertical integration refers to the degree to which a firm controls the value chain activities within its supply chain (Novak and Stern, 2009; Wu, 2026).
Previous studies have shown that vertical integration enables firms to (1) control more stages of the value chain to gain monopolistic earnings by avoiding competition within the supply chain (Hu et al., 2022), (2) promote knowledge transfer and information sharing (Liu et al., 2016; Zhou and Wan, 2017), and (3) facilitate real-time adaptation decisions (Fan and Xiao, 2023; Forbes and Lederman, 2010).
As a result, vertical integration is positively related to firm outcomes, such as innovation performance (Zhang and Tong, 2021), inventory performance (Zhou and Wan, 2017), and operational performance (Forbes and Lederman, 2010).
Consistent with this stream of research, we argue that vertical integration can mitigate the coordination and monitoring challenges caused by geographical distance and dispersion for two reasons.
<!-- para 16 -->
First, vertical integration reduces information asymmetry associated with a geographically distant supply base.
As an owner, the buyer firm can gain access to more information about its suppliers' internal operations (Fan and Xiao, 2023).
Such information advantage can enable the buyer to better monitor suppliers and reduce supplier opportunism.
A higher level of trust will also arise between the buyer and its direct suppliers, which can facilitate the exchange of information on product design, quality control, and consumer feedback (Flynn et al., 2010).
Therefore, frequent information exchange and communication can mitigate the monitoring challenges associated with a geographically distant and dispersed supply base.
<!-- para 17 -->
Second, sourcing from internal suppliers helps mitigate potential conflicts of interest that arise when coordinating with geographically dispersed supply bases.
Vertical integration can create an internal market that eases coordination between a buyer and its supply base (Novak and Eppinger, 2001).
By holding ownership stakes in a supplier firm, a buyer firm can exert stronger control over component design and manufacturing processes and mitigate potential supplier opportunism (Hu et al., 2022; Novak and Stern, 2009).
Furthermore, as a shareholder of the supplier firm, the buyer can influence the supplier's operational practices.
For example, if quality risks are identified in a supplier's internal operations, the buyer can leverage its ownership to improve the supplier's operational practices, reducing product quality risks.
Thus, even if the supply base is geographically distant and dispersed, such control can help mitigate product quality risks.
Thus, we hypothesize:
<!-- para 18 -->
**H3a: Vertical integration weakens the relationship between buyers' supply base geographical distance and product quality risks.**
<!-- para 19 -->
**H3b: Vertical integration weakens the relationship between buyers' supply base geographical dispersion and product quality risks.**

## methods
<!-- para 3 -->
We tested our hypotheses using data from the Chinese passenger car industry, which is well-suited to our purposes.
First, product quality issues have caused widespread concerns in China.
For example, in 2024 alone, 233 automobile recalls were announced, affecting 11.23 million cars.
Compared with the previous year, the number of recalls increased by 8.9%, and the number of cars recalled increased by 67.0% (State Administration for Market Regulation, 2025).
Therefore, understanding factors that pose product quality risks in China can help improve product quality and safety in the world's largest market for automobile production and sales (Sun et al., 2021).
Second, since joining the World Trade Organization in 2001, China has gradually opened its market to foreign automakers and suppliers, helping its supply chains become global in nature (Chen et al., 2020).
However, the geographical and structural characteristics of the automobile supply network in China differ from those in developed countries, which enables us to test the theoretical robustness and empirical generalizability of research on supply chain risks conducted predominantly in WEIRD contexts.
<!-- para 5 -->
We collected the main data for our analysis from the MarkLines Automobile Industry Portal, which provides information services for the automotive industry.
This information portal offers detailed data on the global automobile industry, including production, sales, original equipment manufacturer (OEM) plants, and suppliers.
Its supply base data contains supply information for roughly 300 automobile components globally.
The module is organized in a “who supplies what to whom in which model-year” data structure and is similar to the “who supplies whom” dataset used in previous studies (Bray et al., 2019).
Compared with other supply network databases, such as Bloomberg and FactSet Revere, MarkLines provides more nuanced data on the sourcing activities between automakers and component suppliers (Astvansh et al., 2022a).
Table A3 in the E-Companion presents an example of component supply information from MarkLines.
<!-- para 6 -->
In addition to MarkLines, we collected data from five other sources.
First, we collected automobile complaint data from 12365auto.com, which collects and publishes consumer complaints about passenger cars (see Table A3 in the E-Companion for an example).
The platform categorizes each consumer complaint into typical problem categories, which are combinations of components and problems, such as “clutch” slip, “engine” noise, and “brake system” jitter.
We used this information to categorize each consumer complaint into the following seven automobile component systems: engine, transmission, steering, brakes, suspension, body & electronics, and tires.
Second, to construct the vertical integration measure, we collected data from Bureau van Dijk’s Orbis databases, which provide firm ownership information (including on private firms).
Third, we transformed the plant and supplier addresses collected from MarkLines into geocode data using the API services of Google Maps (for addresses outside China) and Gaode Maps (for addresses in China).
Fourth, we collected country-level Logistic Performance Index scores from the World Bank.
This variable helped capture the logistical convenience of a supplier country in the supplier selection model.
Fifth, we manually collected high-speed railway data from the National Railway Administration of the People’s Republic of China and the Chinese Railway Yearbook to identify the year when a high-speed railway connection was established between the assembly plant and supplier cities.
We included this variable in the supplier selection model (see section 4.5.2 for more details).
<!-- para 7 -->
We combined data from these different sources by car model, model year, and component system (for details on data collection and data matching procedures, see Item 3 in the E-Companion).
The sample comprises the supply base and automobile complaint data of 797 car models from 2014 to 2019.
We chose this period because the component supply information from MarkLines was incomplete before 2014.
In addition, we allowed a four-year observation window (introduction year and three years after) to measure product quality risks; thus, the final car model year is 2019.
After we removed observations with missing values (i.e., 844 of 8,627 observations), the final number of observations included in the study is 7,783 (i.e., 2,415 suppliers of 731 car models).
Among all suppliers in the study sample, 85% are located in China, while the remaining 15% are foreign suppliers from countries such as Japan, Germany, the United States, and South Korea.
See Table A4 in the E-Companion for the supplier country distribution.
<!-- para 9 -->
Our unit of analysis is the “car model–model year–component system,” similar to other studies on quality risks in the automobile industry (Kalaignanam et al., 2017).
We selected this unit because quality risks and the supply base vary considerably across car models, model years, and component systems.
As we discuss in section 5.3.2, we ascertained the robustness of our results by conducting analyses at different units of analysis: car model–component system level (ignoring model year); car model–model year level (ignoring component systems); car model level (ignoring both model year and component systems); and plant–year level.
<!-- para 11 -->
**4.4.1 Dependent Variable.** Previous studies have measured quality risks by product recalls (Shah et al., 2016; Steven and Britto, 2016).
This measure is appropriate in contexts where the institutional environment is strong, and thus, firms will recall a product when there are quality risks.
However, in the Chinese automobile industry, where quality regulations are still developing, treating recalls as a proxy for product quality risks might underestimate the situation, as firms may not recall cars even when quality risks exist.
Similar to Bray et al. (2019), we measured product quality risks using consumer complaints, which provide a truer picture of these risks than product recalls.
To avoid bias arising from a ratio measurement (Certo et al., 2020), we used the absolute count of consumer complaints and controlled for the number of cars sold for each car model.
<!-- para 12 -->
We also customized Bray et al.'s (2019) measure of quality risks, which is based on the U.S. automobile market, for the Chinese automobile market by selecting an appropriate observation window for product quality risks.
Figure A1 in the E-Companion shows trends in consumer complaints in the United States and China.
Unlike in the United States, where consumer complaints peak in year  $t + 4$, consumer complaints in China peak in year  $t + 1$ (i.e., one year after the introduction of a car model) and then decrease quickly.
The first four years (the introduction year and three years after) account for approximately 80% of the complaints in China, compared with 40% in the United States.
Thus, we used a four-year window (i.e.,  $[t, t+3]$) for a fair comparison of car models introduced in early and later years.
For example, the quality risk of the Toyota Camry 2013 model engine system is calculated as the total number of complaints about the Toyota Camry 2013 model engine system from 2013 to 2016.
In robustness tests (detailed in section 5.3.3), we measured the dependent variable using shorter and longer windows (i.e., one year and five years after the introduction).
The results remained consistent.
<!-- para 13 -->
**4.4.2 Independent Variables.** We measured geographical distance as the average geographical distance between the automobile assembly plant and its suppliers (Bray et al., 2019; Sharma et al., 2020).
We calculated it using a two-step approach.
First, we converted the addresses of the automakers and suppliers into coordinates.
For automakers with multiple branches/plants, we cross-checked car production information with plant descriptions in MarkLines (i.e., models assembled at each plant) to ensure geocoding accuracy.
Second, we calculated the geographical distance using the geopy package in Python based on the Haversine formula, which approximates the great-circle distance between two coordinates.
For each “car model–model year–component system” combination, we calculated the average geodesic distance (in kilometers) between all suppliers and the assembly plant.
For models assembled in more than one plant (46 of 731 car models in our sample), we calculated the average distance from each plant to its supplier (the results are identical when we use the maximum distance instead).
To account for supplier heterogeneity, we used weighted distance as a robustness check.
The results remained consistent (as detailed in section 5.2.3).
<!-- para 14 -->
We measure geographical dispersion using the Herfindahl–Hirschman index, as follows:
<!-- para 15 -->
$$ GeoDispersion_{ijt}=1-\sum_{k=1}^{n}\left(\frac{C_{ijt,k}}{C_{ijt}}\right)^{2} $$
<!-- para 16 -->
where  $C_{ijt}$ is the total number of components for car model i, system j, and model year t.  $C_{ijt,k}$ is the number of components provided by suppliers in country k.
For additional analyses based on the Chinese supply base, we measure geographical dispersion by the number of supplier provinces.
<!-- para 17 -->
We measure vertical integration by the percentage of internal suppliers in the supply base.
Larger values indicate a larger number of internal suppliers in an automaker's supply base, suggesting greater vertical integration and control.
A supplier in which the automaker owns more than 50% is considered an internal supplier.
Similar to other studies (Novak and Eppinger, 2001; Novak and Stern, 2009), we measured vertical integration at the “car model–model year–component system” level.
This measure accounts for variations in car models and component systems across model years.
<!-- para 18 -->
**4.4.3 Control Variables.** We controlled for product-, supply base-, and brand-level factors that may affect hypothesized relationships.
First, for product-level characteristics, we included car sales, system complexity, first year of production, production experience, and competition.
We included car sales because models with larger consumer bases are likely to have more complaints.
We included system complexity as complex component systems (e.g., transmission and engine systems) are more prone to product quality risks.
We measured system complexity by the number of components in each “car model–model year–component system”.
We also controlled for whether a car model was in its first year of production (measured with a dummy variable equal to 1 for the first year and 0 otherwise), as newly introduced models are more prone to quality failures (Shah et al., 2016).
Furthermore, we controlled for production experience to account for the learning curve in quality control (Thirumalai and Sinha, 2011).
We measured production experience by the cumulative number of cars manufactured in the preceding five years (Shah et al., 2016).
We included product competition because intensive market competition might motivate firms to act aggressively and adopt new technologies that might expose products to quality risks (Ball et al., 2018).
We calculated product competition as 1 minus the Herfindahl–Hirschman index of product sales concentration, based on the market share of all car models in a single market segment.
<!-- para 19 -->
Second, we controlled for the supply base characteristics, including supplier bargaining power, number of suppliers, relationship strength, supplier internal complexity, supplier quality risk history, and supplier quality certifications.
As each “car model–model year–component system” combination may have multiple suppliers, we aggregated these variables within the supply base to the “car model–model year–component system” level using averages or percentages.
We measured supplier bargaining power by the percentage of the top 400 suppliers in the supply base.
MarkLines lists 400 major global component suppliers to automakers, such as Marelli Corporation, Continental AG, Aisin Corporation, and Aptiv PLC.
These suppliers have significantly more bargaining power than small suppliers.
We measured the number of suppliers by the total number of suppliers, standardized by the number of components for each “car model–model year–component system” combination.
We measured relationship strength as the number of components a supplier provided to the assembly plant.
We measured supplier quality risk history by the total number of consumer complaints for all car models a supplier supplies, supplier internal complexity by the number of components a supplier provides, and supplier quality certifications by the percentage of suppliers with quality certifications (e.g., ISO 9000, ISO 9001, ISO 9002, ISO/TS 16949, QS-9000) in an automaker’s supplier base for a given “car model–model year–component system” combination.
<!-- para 20 -->
Finally, we controlled for brand-level variables, including whether a car model is a luxury brand or a local brand.
We accounted for luxury brands because they tend to invest more in product development and use higher-quality materials and components; thus, they tend to have higher product quality.
In our sample, luxury brands (coded as 1 and others as 0) included Audi, BMW, Cadillac, Hongqi, Infiniti, Jaguar, Land Rover, Lincoln, Lotus, Mercedes-Benz, and Volvo (Zhou et al., 2019).
We also controlled for local brands (coded as 1; others as 0) because Chinese brands tend to have less accumulated experience in car design and manufacturing and thus face greater product quality risks (Li et al., 2015).

4.5.1 Baseline Model.
The dependent variable is the cumulative number of consumer complaints for each car model–model year–component system combination.
As the dependent variable is count data, we used Poisson models for estimation (the ppmlhdfe command in Stata).
The regression model includes model year dummies, car segment dummies, car system dummies, and auto group dummies to account for systematic differences across years, segments, systems, and auto groups:

**4.5.2 Addressing Endogeneity.** The baseline model in equation (1) might be prone to endogeneity due to unobserved supplier selection; that is, automakers might simultaneously consider both the geographical attributes and the quality risks of components supplied by the supplier.
As a result, the relationship between geographical attributes and quality risks may be driven by unobserved supplier selection.
Following prior studies addressing endogeneity concerns using a selection-correction model (Bray et al., 2019; Dahl, 2002; Madiedo et al., 2020; Salvador et al., 2021; Serpa and Krishnan, 2018), we used a two-step procedure to address this alternative explanation (Wu et al., 2017).
<!-- para 6 -->
In the first step, we estimated a supplier selection model that captures the process by which automakers select suppliers for a given component of a car model.
Specifically, we took all suppliers that produce the same component in the same year as the selection pool.
To account for heterogeneity in supplier selection across component systems (Bray et al., 2019), we estimated selection models for different component systems (i.e., engine, transmission, steering, brakes, suspension, body & electronics, and tires).
The results are also consistent when we included all systems in a single selection model.
For each automobile system, we estimated the following supplier selection model:
<!-- para 7 -->
$$ \begin{aligned}Selected_{ijt,n}&=\alpha_{0}+\sum_{k=1}^{10}\delta_{k}C_{ijt,k}+\alpha_{1}Distance_{ijt,n}+\alpha_{2}Foreign_{n}\\&\quad+\alpha_{3}Highspeed_{ijt,n}+\alpha_{4}PeerSelect_{ijt,n}+\varphi_{ijt,n}\end{aligned} $$
<!-- para 8 -->
(2) where  $Selected_{it,n}$ indicates whether supplier n was selected to supply a component under system j of car model i model year t (equal to 1 if a supplier is selected and 0 otherwise).  $C_{ijt,k}$ represents the control variables, detailed in Item 8 of the E-Companion.
In this selection model, we also included geographical distance measured by the distance between supplier n and the assembly plant of car model i model year t (i.e.,  $Distance_{ijt,n}$) and whether supplier n is a foreign supplier (i.e.,  $Foreign_n$).
These variables capture the impact of geographical attributes on supplier selection.
<!-- para 9 -->
In this selection model, we included high-speed railway connectivity (i.e.,  $Highspeed_{ijt,n}$) and peer selection (i.e.,  $PeerSelect_{ijt,n}$) to satisfy the exclusion restriction condition.
Prior work has treated high-speed rail connections as a proxy for inter-city economic connectivity (Zhang and Gu, 2021) and has used the opening of high-speed rail lines as an exogenous shock to geographical distance (Bernard et al., 2019).
In line with this literature, we treated high-speed railway connectivity as a source of exogenous variation in the effective distance/cost of interacting with potential suppliers, influencing supplier selection but not product quality directly.
In China, high-speed railways are predominantly for passenger transport, while automotive parts are shipped mainly by truck or conventional freight rail.
Thus, high-speed railway connectivity between buyer and supplier locations is unlikely to directly affect product quality outcomes through the transportation channel.
We also included whether the supplier is already selected by a peer car model (in the same market segment but produced by a different automaker) to supply the same component.
Being selected by a peer firm increases the likelihood that the supplier will be selected by the focal firm.
However, this selection does not directly affect the quality risks of the focal firm's final product.
We constructed a binary variable equal to 1 if a supplier provided components to a peer car model in the same segment in the previous year, and 0 otherwise.
While these two variables are theoretically exogenous, we conducted additional tests to assess this assumption.
We also conducted sensitivity analyses to estimate potential violations of the exogenous condition using the local-to-zero approach (Conley et al., 2012).
<!-- para 10 -->
Following Wu et al. (2017), we used conditional logit models for the estimation.
For each supplier selection model, we estimated the probability of a supplier being selected ( $Pr_{it,N}$).
We averaged the predicted probabilities from the first step to the “car model–model year–component system” level, since the selection model’s analysis level is “car model–model year–component system–supplier” (since a given system may have components from different suppliers).
We conducted additional tests for the robustness of this approach (detailed in section 5.3.2).
<!-- para 11 -->
In the second step, as shown in equation (3), we included the predicted selection-correction terms (i.e.,  $Pr_{it,N}$) in the main model as controls to address selection-related endogeneity.
We followed Wu et al. (2017) and specified a second-order polynomial expansion of  $Pr_{it,N}$ as our correction term (i.e.,  $Pr_{it,N}^2$).
This regression model controls for the likelihood of supplier selection, thereby mitigating selection bias.

## discussion
<!-- para 2 -->
This study examines the relationship between supply base geographical complexity (i.e., distance and dispersion) and quality risks.
It also explores how vertical integration moderates these relationships.
We tested the hypotheses using data from the Chinese automobile industry.
The findings indicate that the relationship between geographical distance and quality risk is not significant.
However, the geographical dispersion of the supply base is positively related to product quality risks.
We also found that vertical integration enhances the relationship between geographical dispersion and quality risks.
