# lii_2026_vertical_spillover_of_product_recalls_theorization_ 句子库存

## introduction
<!-- para 2 -->
Managing the negative impacts of crisis events has become a strategic issue for many organizations, as negative events such as chemical accidents (Diestre & Rajagopalan, 2014), customer information breaches (Modi et al., 2015), and product-harm crises (Cleeren et al., 2017) threaten the long-term prosperity of the stricken organizations.
The negative impact of such crises on an organization can also spread to its suppliers, even if they are not directly involved in the actions that precipitate the crisis.
For example, the Volkswagen emissions scandal resulted in its Tier-1 suppliers experiencing a 2.69% firm value loss (Jacobs & Singhal, 2020), and the 2018 US government ban on Chinese telecommunications firm ZTE caused a 3.33% abnormal firm value loss for US suppliers (Jacobs et al., 2022).
These impacts are “vertical spillovers,” defined as the losses a supplier firm incurs when its buyer firm experiences a negative event.
<!-- para 3 -->
Product recalls are crisis events that have caused widespread concern across many industries, including automobiles, consumer products, and medical devices (Ball et al., 2018; Hora et al., 2011; Ni et al., 2023; Wowak & Boone, 2015).
Prior research has documented spillover effects of product recalls across the automobile and toy industries (Borah & Tellis, 2016; Liu & Varki, 2021; Zavyalova et al., 2012).
However, these and other studies (e.g., Lei et al., 2008; Roehm & Tybout, 2006) have only examined horizontal spillovers (i.e., losses experienced by competitors of the recalling firm), leaving vertical spillovers of product recalls and the mechanisms through which they manifest largely under-researched.
<!-- para 4 -->
Examining vertical spillover effects has both theoretical and practical significance.
Theoretically, vertical spillovers are not only distinct from horizontal ones but also more complex.
Horizontal spillovers occur through shared industry categorization, in which reputational or trust-based risks arising from consumer perceptions spread across competitors in the same industry (Freedman et al., 2012).
In contrast, vertical spillovers directly disrupt specific supplier-buyer ties and create severe consequences for them.
These effects often take the form of cascading operational risks arising from shared processes and interdependencies between suppliers and buyers (Hendricks et al., 2020) and may, in some cases, exceed horizontal effects.
Importantly, the factors that amplify or mitigate risks in interdependent supply chain contexts are likely to differ from those that shape risks emerging from shared industry membership.
Thus, the underlying mechanisms of vertical spillovers warrant dedicated theoretical attention.
Practically, research shows that an increasing number of recalls are caused by supply chain factors (Steven & Britto, 2016; Steven et al., 2014; Zou et al., 2023).
Understanding the supply chain costs of product recalls can pave the way for supply chain partners to work collectively to avoid quality risks.
<!-- para 5 -->
This article focuses on two research questions.
First, is there a vertical spillover effect such that a buyer's recall causes firm value losses for its suppliers?
Second, if so, what factors affect the strength of the vertical spillover effect?
This research draws on social network theory (Podolny, 2001) to suggest that relationship ties between a recalling firm and its suppliers serve as "pipes" and "prisms" that transmit the cash flow effect and impression effect, respectively, of product recalls from the recalling firm to its suppliers.
Specifically, as pipes, buyer–supplier relationships transmit operational disruptions caused by product recalls, resulting in a reduction in the future cash flow of the supplier firm (i.e., cash flow effect).
As prisms, buyer–supplier relationships shape categorization such that the buyer and suppliers are grouped together, leading to negative impressions spreading from the recalling firm to suppliers through "guilt by association" (i.e., impression effect).
<!-- para 6 -->
Building on the work of Yu et al. (2008), this study theorizes that vertical spillover effects are shaped by the extended social network that consists of institutional intermediaries, including common business ties, common institutional ownership, and common analyst coverage between suppliers and recalling firms.
Common business ties are overlapping supply chain partners between suppliers and recalling firms (Chae et al., 2020; Yu & Lester, 2008).
Common institutional ownership captures overlapping institutional investors of suppliers and recalling firms (Cheung et al., 2020).
Common analyst coverage occurs when the same analyst covers both suppliers and the recalling firms (Israelsen, 2016; Zeng & Tang, 2023).
This study suggests that institutional intermediaries shape the strength of the cash flow and impression effects.
Hypotheses were tested using a custom panel dataset that merged seven different secondary data sources.
<!-- para 7 -->
This study adds three insights to the literature on the spillovers of crises and supply networks.
First, it distinguishes between vertical and horizontal spillover effects and uses a social network lens to examine the propagation of supply chain risk events, such as product recalls.
Previous studies on the spillover effects of crises have focused primarily on the impression effect (i.e., reputation losses due to categorization) (Borah & Tellis, 2016; Liu & Varki, 2021; Yu et al., 2008), whereas research on supply chain propagation of crises has primarily focused on the cash flow effect (i.e., the operational disruptions spread within the supply chain) (Jacobs & Singhal, 2020; Jacobs et al., 2022).
However, each research stream only explains a part of the phenomenon.
Drawing on social network theory (Podolny, 2001), this study integrates these two views to understand the vertical spillover effect of crises by theorizing relationship ties as pipes and prisms that cause the propagation of crises in the supply chain.
Together, the theorization of vertical spillover effects and the empirical evidence provide a comprehensive framework for understanding how buyers' crises affect their suppliers.
<!-- para 8 -->
Second, while prior research has demonstrated the merits of network connections, specifically focusing on the benefits of structural embeddedness (Bellamy et al., 2014), network centrality (Kim & Zhu, 2018; Nezami et al., 2025), and structural equivalence (Chae et al., 2022; Chae et al., 2020), the present research shows that network connections can become liabilities in adverse situations; that is, more common business ties enhance the vertical spillover effect.
This finding echoes previous studies on the vulnerabilities related to high network centrality (Luo et al., 2024) and supply base rationalization (Koufteros et al., 2007), underscoring the need to consider both the benefits and risks of structural equivalence.
<!-- para 9 -->
Third, this study extends social network theory by hypothesizing and empirically testing how third-party intermediaries (i.e., institutional investors and financial analysts) are embedded within interorganizational networks and how their shared presence across firms conditions spillover effects.
The findings indicate that both common institutional ownership and common analyst coverage between suppliers and recalling firms affect the vertical spillover effect.
Consistent with prior studies highlighting the benefits of common institutional ownership (Cheung et al., 2020; Freeman, 2023), the findings reveal that the vertical spillover effect is weaker when suppliers and recalling firms have high overlaps in institutional investors.
<!-- para 10 -->
In addition, the presence of common analysts weakens the vertical spillover effect, highlighting the under-examined stakeholders in supply chain studies—namely, financial analysts.
Thus, supply network research should broaden its scope to include extended network players.

## theory
<!-- para 3 -->
Social network theory posits that firms are influenced not only by their internal attributes but also by the social networks in which they are embedded (Carpenter et al., 2012; Granovetter, 1983).
Supply chain studies have used this view to investigate supply network structures (Borgatti & Li, 2009; Choi & Kim, 2008; Kim et al., 2011), conceptualizing the market as a network of the nodes (firms) and ties (supply chain relationships) between them.
This stream of research indicates that a firm's position in the network affects its outcomes, including financial performance (Lu & Shang, 2017), innovation (Bellamy et al., 2014; Kumar et al., 2020), and sustainability (Adhikary et al., 2020).
Despite extensive research, this stream of research has two limitations that warrant further study.
<!-- para 4 -->
First, this stream of research broadly conceptualizes supply networks as channels for the transmission of information and resources, while ignoring the role of network ties in reputation spillover.
In essence, supply network connections can enhance access to resources and knowledge.
As a result, firms with more connections with other firms (i.e., high network centrality and embeddedness) in the supply network tend to perform better (Nezami et al., 2025).
Furthermore, structural equivalence between supply chain partners enhances relationship outcomes (Chae et al., 2022; Chae et al., 2020).
However, supply chain relationships are not merely transactional; they also shape a firm's access to resources, its strategic behavior, and how others perceive it (Burt et al., 2013; Kilduff & Brass, 2010).
According to Podolny (2001), networks serve not only as pipes through which resources flow but also as prisms through which reputation and quality are inferred.
In the "networks as pipes" view, information, capital, and goods are transferred between firms through relationship ties.
The "networks as prisms" view posits that a relationship tie between two firms is also an informational cue that third-party observers use to infer the quality and identity of the connected firms.
Thus, further research that examines how network ties shape reputational connections between supply chain partners is warranted.
<!-- para 5 -->
Second, this stream of research traditionally conceptualizes supply networks as patterns of direct ties among focal firms through which information, resources, and shocks flow (Borgatti & Li, 2009; Kim et al., 2011).
From this perspective, third-party actors such as institutional investors and financial analysts are often treated as background conditions that shape the information environment surrounding firms rather than as integral components of the network itself.
As a result, their role is typically modeled as exogenous or controlled for rather than theorized as an active mechanism that influences network outcomes.
However, a growing body of research in finance and strategy suggests that institutional investors (Cheung et al., 2020; Shi et al., 2022) and financial analysts (Brauer & Wiersema, 2018; Busenbark et al., 2022) play a critical role in interpreting, framing, and disseminating firm-specific information, particularly during periods of heightened uncertainty.
These intermediaries influence how external audiences assess responsibility, risk, and future performance following adverse events (Li et al., 2024).
From a network perspective, such actors do not merely observe firm behavior; they also actively determine the informational linkages connecting firms with their stakeholder ecosystems.
Thus, research that explores how the extended social network of supply chain partners shapes the information and information flows is also warranted.
<!-- para 7 -->
Product recalls are a result of product-harm crises and product quality risks (Cleeren et al., 2017; Shah et al., 2016).
They impose not only direct recall costs (e.g., compensation to customers, legal costs, recall advertising campaigns) but also indirect costs, such as loss of firm value and reputation (Li et al., 2022).
For example, research in marketing has shown that product recalls are associated with reduced sales and marketing effectiveness (Liu & Shankar, 2015; Van Heerde et al., 2007), resulting in both short- and long-term losses of firm value (Liu et al., 2017).
<!-- para 8 -->
The consequences of product recalls are not confined to the recalling firm but often spill over to other firms in the industry.
Previous studies have shown that automobile recalls of one brand are associated with reduced customer evaluations of similar brands (Borah & Tellis,
<!-- para 9 -->
2016); that is, recalls in one category affect other categories, resulting in a categorization effect.
Similarly, toy recalls by one brand are associated with negative media coverage of other toy brands (Zavyalova et al., 2012).
Considering such effects, Liu and Varki (2021) found that when a firm with a strong reputation for product reliability issues a recall, a spillover effect occurs, leading to a firm value loss for its competitors as well.
Together, these studies provide evidence for horizontal spillover effects of product recalls, but vertical spillover effects and their mechanisms remain under-researched.
<!-- para 10 -->
Research shows that many recalls result not from direct production failures by recalling firms but from upstream suppliers delivering defective parts.
For example, in the automobile industry, approximately 47% of the recalls are associated with component suppliers (Astvansh et al., 2022).
Underscoring this issue, Steven et al. (2014) found that outsourcing and offshoring increase the likelihood of product recalls.
This effect is more pronounced when firms source from emerging markets (Steven & Britto, 2016).
In a similar vein, Bray et al. (2019) found that sourcing from geographically distant suppliers increases the likelihood of product quality risks.
Zou et al. (2023) found that supply base innovation has a U-shaped impact on the probability of product recalls.
In summary, supply chains have become an increasingly important determinant of product recalls, but the consequences of product recalls on suppliers have been under-researched.
Therefore, examining vertical spillover effects and their contingencies can provide the reasons for supply chain partners to work together to reduce product recalls.
<!-- para 12 -->
Recent studies on crisis management have shown that the impact of negative events extends beyond firm boundaries to other organizations that are not directly involved in the event (Diestre, 2018; Kashmiri et al., 2017).
Yu et al. (2008, p. 452) define this situation as “negative impacts resulting from a crisis that strikes one organization [and then spreads] to other organizations.” The direction of this spillover effect can be horizontal—that is, from the crisis-stricken organization to other firms in the industry selling the same product (Borah & Tellis, 2016; Liu & Varki, 2021; Zavyalova et al., 2012).
Spillover effects can also be vertical—that is, from the crisis-stricken organization to its supply chain partners (Jacobs & Singhal, 2020; Jacobs et al., 2022).
Table A1 in the Online Appendix summarizes literature on the spillover effect; it shows that previous research has focused mainly on horizontal spillovers of crises.
<!-- para 13 -->
The most widely discussed theoretical underpinning for the horizontal spillover effect is categorization, which refers to a social-cognitive process by which individuals group similar social actors into categories that serve as a schema to generalize the attributes and behaviors of one actor to other actors in the same category (Naumovska & Zajac, 2021; Yu et al., 2008).
After the occurrence of crises or negative events at one organization, stakeholders may penalize other organizations that are in the same category as the focal organization (Bachmann et al., 2023).
Previous studies have shown that the categorization effect is more potent when stakeholders perceive other firms to be highly related to the affected firm; for example, overlap is high in the input portfolio (Diestre & Rajagopalan, 2014), technological fields (Diestre, 2018), and the country of origin (Borah & Tellis, 2016).
Mateska et al. (2023) used this categorization rationale (i.e., similarity heuristic), combined with the affect heuristic, to examine environmental, social, and governance– (ESG-) related legitimacy spillover from supplier firms to buyer firms.
They found that supplier ESG violations are associated with buyer firm value losses.
<!-- para 14 -->
Despite its broad adoption in explaining horizontal spillover effects, the categorization mechanism alone cannot adequately explain vertical spillover effects within a supply chain context.
In a closely coupled supply network, a crisis at one firm may disrupt the operations in its supply network (Hendricks et al., 2020).
Thus, supplementing the categorization effect, this study integrates the supply chain risk propagation perspective that explains vertical spillover effects from buyer firms to their suppliers.
This perspective is grounded in the supply chain disruption literature, with a focus on the spread of disruptions within a supply chain network (Carvalho et al., 2021; Jacobs & Singhal, 2017).
For a supplier firm, a crisis experienced by its customers may affect its future cash flows (Qiu et al., 2024).
This effect is more pronounced when the supplier is highly dependent on the crisis-stricken buyer firm. [^1]
<!-- para 15 -->
Spillover effects are most commonly investigated in event studies that assess short-term losses in firm value following salient events.
This approach is based on the efficient market assumption that all existing information is factored into a firm's stock price.
Negative events, such as product recalls, violations of environmental regulations, and customer data breaches, are likely to lower investors' expectations of the future cash flow of the company and thereby reduce stock prices (Ding et al., 2018).
As such, the spillover effect can be captured by the abnormal returns during the event window.
<!-- para 16 -->
In summary, while prior research has used categorization and supply chain propagation to assess the spillover effects of crises, it has theorized them independently and focused predominantly on horizontal spillover effects.
In contrast, the present study aims to integrate these two streams to examine the vertical spillover effect of product recalls.
<!-- para 18 -->
Based on the rationale that supply chain network structures are key determinants of supply chain risk diffusion (Craighead et al., 2007; Kim et al., 2015), previous studies on supply chain propagation have shown that cash flow dependencies create pipes for cash flow propagation, while studies on categorization have shown that shared identities and reputations form prisms for impression spillovers.
<!-- para 19 -->
Network as Pipes.
Studies on supply chain propagation have focused on how disruptions in one firm spread to other players within the supply network.
This stream of research indicates that the stock price of a supply chain partner is negatively affected when its buyers or suppliers experience negative events, such as government sanctions (Jacobs et al., 2022), scandals (Jacobs & Singhal, 2020), accidents (Jacobs & Singhal, 2017), ESG violations (Lo et al., 2018; Mateska et al., 2023), or natural disasters (Carvalho et al., 2021; Hendricks et al., 2020).
The underlying rationale is that supply chain partners depend on each other for resources, and thus operational disruptions in one firm will spread to its supply chain partners (Qiu et al., 2024).
Consistent with this rationale, a product recall by a firm can create a negative cash flow effect for its downstream suppliers.
During a product recall, the recalling firm is investigated and scrutinized by regulators and other stakeholders, and such scrutiny disrupts its operations.
In recalls involving severe quality problems, production lines might be halted for auditing, and new product sales might be prohibited until quality concerns are resolved.
Thus, product recalls are likely to reduce sales of the recalling firm (Liu & Shankar, 2015).
In a closely coupled supply chain, such operational disruptions might spread to suppliers, leading to reduced demand for raw materials and components and, in turn, lower revenues for supplier firms (i.e., the cash flow effect).
<!-- para 20 -->
Network as Prisms.
In addition to operational dependencies, supply chain relationships serve as informational cues that determine reputational spillover in negative situations.
This effect is based on categorization, or stakeholders' tendency to extend their impression of one firm to other firms perceived to be similar (Diestre, 2018; Diestre & Rajagopalan, 2014).
Product recalls are indicators of quality risks that would induce a negative impression on stakeholders (Hora et al., 2011; Shah et al., 2016; Wowak & Boone, 2015).
Buyer-supplier relationships are strong ties that stakeholders may use as anchors to categorize their impression of a recalling firm and generalize it to its suppliers (Nichols et al., 2019).
Thus, a firm's product recalls may create a negative impression on its suppliers (i.e., the impression effect).
<!-- para 21 -->
Given cash flow and impression effects, investors may be uncertain about their returns from supplier firms following recall announcements.
Although a reduction in returns may not manifest in the short run, uncertainty may prompt investors to lower their evaluations of supplier firms, resulting in losses in firm value for those suppliers.
Stated formally:
<!-- para 22 -->
**H1: Product recalls have a vertical spillover effect such that a firm’s recall leads to firm value losses for its suppliers.**
<!-- para 24 -->
Social network theory suggests that the pipes and prisms shaping spillovers are influenced by network structure and tie strength (Yu & Lester, 2008).
Consistent with this view, prior research indicates that the magnitude of spillover is dependent on the perceived similarity between firms and the strength of their relationship (Diestre, 2018; Kashmiri et al., 2017).
Furthermore, the spillover effect is mediated by institutional intermediaries who interpret the crisis and shape stakeholder perceptions (Yu et al., 2008).
Thus, the present study focuses on the direct and extended social network that captures these dynamics through the lens of three key market stakeholders: business partners, investors, and financial analysts.
Specifically, this study examines the moderating effects of common business ties, common institutional ownership, and common analyst coverage.
These intermediaries form the direct and indirect social networks that act as additional pipes and prisms, shaping the strength of vertical spillover effects.

<!-- para 28 -->
When two nodes share common ties, they tend to be structurally equivalent and occupy similar positions in the network.
As a result, they tend to share a similar social environment and have similar roles, attitudes, behaviors, and performances (Borgatti & Grosser, 2015; Burt, 1978, 1987).
Chae et al. (2020) found that high structural equivalence (i.e., a high percentage of common ties) between an automaker and a supplier helped the supplier better understand the automaker's innovation needs, thereby increasing the supplier's innovation value to the automaker.
That study highlights the benefits of having more common business ties.
However, more common ties between buyer and supplier firms lead to greater business dependency and a stronger relationship.
Thus, common business ties may become liabilities in the context of negative events such as product recalls.
<!-- para 29 -->
First, from a supply chain risk propagation perspective, when buyers and suppliers share common suppliers or customers, negative shocks can flow through more pipes from buyers to suppliers.
When a key buyer firm experiences a product recall, the negative shock is not confined to a single dyadic link; instead, it likely creates a ripple effect, with a negative impact on its business partners (Dolgui & Ivanov, 2021; Li et al., 2021a).
This process may transform a localized disruption into a broader network-level event, as the instability of demand is transmitted through multiple pipes to a wider set of stakeholders.
Thus, sharing more common business ties means that suppliers will experience not only a first-order cash flow effect, due to the cancellation of orders by the recalling buyer firm, but also a second-order cash flow effect from common business partners.
<!-- para 30 -->
Second, common business ties can also enhance the perceived homophily between suppliers and recalling firms and the categorization effect.
When two firms share more business partners, they are perceived as having a strong relationship and share a common fate, which makes reputation spillover more likely (Yu & Lester, 2008).
This is mainly because sharing business ties means that the two firms are perceived as similar and comparable.
From a categorization perspective, stakeholders are more likely to view suppliers and recalling firms as belonging to the same category and, thus, extend their impression of one organization to other organizations (Naumovska & Zajac, 2021).
Given this rationale, common business ties enhance the size of the prism that strengthens the impression effect.
<!-- para 31 -->
In summary, when suppliers and automakers share business partners, their fates are more deeply interlinked.
These ties act as pipes and prisms, transmitting not just operational disruptions but also negative reputations.
Therefore, the vertical spillover effect of product recalls tends to be stronger for suppliers that have more common business ties with the recalling firm.
Stated formally:
<!-- para 32 -->
**H2: The vertical spillover effect of a product recall is strengthened by common business ties between the recalling firm and its suppliers.**
<!-- para 34 -->
Institutional ownership captures the percentage of equity held by institutional investors (i.e., equity holders managing more than $100 million in equity assets), which are major players in the US stock market (Connelly et al., 2019).
Unlike individual investors, institutional investors tend to have information advantages and a stronger impact on firm operations (Kochhar & David, 1996).
Institutional investors can affect various aspects of firm operations, such as shareholder proposals, voting on strategic decisions, and shareholder activism (e.g., hostile takeover) (Goranova & Ryan, 2014; He et al., 2019).
<!-- para 35 -->
Common institutional ownership occurs when the same institutional investor maintains equity stakes in multiple companies (e.g., buyers and suppliers).
To maximize the overall return on their investment portfolios, institutional investors tend to internalize externalities to reduce competition and conflicts within their portfolios (Azar et al., 2018; He & Huang, 2017; Lu et al., 2022).
Consistent with this rationale, Cheung et al. (2020) suggest that common institutional ownership in supply chain partners can increase collaboration, reduce information asymmetry, and curb opportunism among firms.
As a result, supplier firms with a high level of common institutional ownership with buyer firms tend to have a higher level of operating and market performance.
Similarly, Freeman (2023) found that common institutional ownership increases relationship duration and transaction size between buyers and suppliers.
<!-- para 36 -->
In the context of product recalls, common institutional owners represent shared governance ties that mitigate the information asymmetry and uncertainties associated with product recalls, which drive stock price changes after recall events (Chen et al., 2009).
This is because institutional owners are not passive observers; they often engage directly with firm management and have access to non-public and strategic information (DesJardine et al., 2023).
When common institutional owners hold significant stakes in both the recalling buyer and its supplier, they can gain a granular understanding of the root causes of the recall, the scope of the operational disruption, and the buyer's recovery plan.
This direct insight allows these institutional owners to more accurately assess the true threat to the supplier's future cash flows.
Furthermore, categorization is more likely when both information asymmetry and uncertainty about the causes and consequences of recalls are high (Yu & Lester, 2008).
Given that institutional investors can access more accurate information, they are less likely to rely on categorization to create an impression of guilt by association.
Thus, common institutional ownership serves as an additional "pipe" through which more accurate information flows, leading institutional owners to hold onto the supplier's stock and thereby dampening the direct financial propagation from recalling firms to suppliers.
Thus:
<!-- para 37 -->
**H3: The vertical spillover effect of a product recall is weakened by common institutional ownership between the recalling firm and its suppliers.**
<!-- para 39 -->
Financial analysts are institutional intermediaries whose primary role is to collect, process, and interpret firm-specific information for market participants (Brauer & Wiersema, 2018).
For publicly listed firms, financial analysts serve as institutional intermediaries who bridge the informational gap between management and markets.
Analysts reduce information asymmetry for investors by providing regular forecasting, monitoring managerial decisions, and disseminating corporate news (Zhang & Wang, 2023).
Therefore, they are key agents affecting stock market reactions.
Previous studies have documented the impact of analysts on firm operations, such as resource allocation (Busenbark et al., 2022), competitive actions (Chen et al., 2024), and earnings management (Andrei et al., 2024).
In the context of product recalls, Li et al. (2024) found that analysts specialized in product-related reviews serve as information intermediaries, reducing information asymmetry in product quality by turning difficult-to-observe quality attributes into credible signals for the capital market.
<!-- para 40 -->
Common analyst coverage occurs when the same analyst simultaneously covers two firms, such as a buyer and a supplier (Israelsen, 2016; Zeng & Tang, 2023).
Prior research shows that shared analysts enhance information flow between covered firms.
For example, Martens and Sextroh (2021) found that shared analyst coverage improves patent citations between firms, confirming the role of analysts in knowledge sharing.
Cortes and Marcet (2023) revealed that shared analyst coverage of firms undergoing mergers and acquisitions serves as a screening mechanism.
While shared coverage reduces the overall likelihood of deal completion, it ensures that consummated transactions are of higher quality, yield higher announcement returns, and deliver superior post-completion performance.
Furthermore, common analyst coverage increases cross-firm monitoring, thereby reducing uncertainty of cash flow in buyer-supplier ties.
For example, Zeng and Tang (2023) found that suppliers with high levels of common analyst coverage with their customers gain higher returns on investment, as they attain an information advantage on customers' business prospects through common analysts.
These studies demonstrate that financial analysts are important intermediaries of information and that shared analyst coverage enhances information flows and reduces information asymmetry.
<!-- para 41 -->
Building on this insight, this research suggests that common analyst coverage between the supplier and recalling firms acts as a filter for the pipes and prisms of the vertical spillover effect.
In the context of product recalls, common analysts have access to information from both the recalling and supplier firms and serve as influential sensemaking agents who frame the narrative around the recall, such as the source, scope, and implications of recall events (Li et al., 2024).
Furthermore, common analysts are uniquely positioned to triangulate information from both management teams, understand the contractual obligations between them, and evaluate the operational interdependence that governs their relationship (Zeng & Tang, 2023).
By synthesizing this integrated information, common analysts provide the market with a more reliable forecast of the recall's financial consequences for the supplier.
This information can serve as a powerful counter-narrative to the market's tendency to generalize the crisis and help prevent overreaction driven by uncertainty.
As a result, common analyst coverage can weaken the vertical spillover effect of product recalls.
Thus:
<!-- para 42 -->
**H4: The vertical spillover effect of a product recall is weakened by common analyst coverage of the recalling firm and its suppliers.**

## methods
<!-- para 3 -->
This study tested the hypotheses using product recall and supply network data from the US automobile industry.
It focused on the automobile industry for two reasons.
First, product recalls are common in the automobile industry and have become a serious concern to its stakeholders (Astvansh et al., 2022).
Second, compared with other industries, suppliers build long-term relationships with automakers and have a higher dependency on them.
As a result, buyer-supplier relationship information is more visible to investors.
<!-- para 4 -->
The data comprise passenger car recalls by automakers listed on the New York Stock Exchange and the NASDAQ, including General Motors, Ford, Fiat Chrysler, Toyota, Honda, and Tesla.
Together, these firms serve 66.92% of the entire passenger car market in the United States (MarkLines, 2020).
The panel data for this study included merged data from seven databases: (1) recall data collected from the National Highway Traffic Safety Administration (NHTSA) website and the RavenPack database; (2) automaker–supplier relationship data from the FactSet Revere database; (3) stock return data from the Center for Research in Security Prices; (4) confounding event data and media sentiment data from the RavenPack database; (5) quarterly financial ratio data of suppliers from Compustat; (6) institutional investor ownership data from the Thomson Reuters ownership database; and (7) analyst estimation data from I/B/E/S database.
Figure A1 in the Online Appendix lists detailed steps for matching different datasets (with observations dropped in each step).
<!-- para 5 -->
Similar to previous studies (Gao et al., 2015; Javadinia et al., 2023), this study included only significant automobile recalls when the recall volume is higher than a threshold (2% of cars sold in the previous year) because small-scale recalls are common in the auto industry and thus are not regarded as crises.
It specifically focused on media-reported automobile recalls, matching each automobile recall reported in the NHTSA with recall news reports (if any) captured by the RavenPack database.
The RavenPack database has wide coverage of major media (Bushman & Pinto, 2024; Yu et al., 2024) and thus is a reliable source to distinguish media-reported recalls from those not reported.
<!-- para 6 -->
For methodological considerations, observations were deleted when: (1) multiple buyers of a supplier firm recalled on the same day (in this case, the source of the spillover effect is unclear); (2) there were confounding events (e.g., quarterly financial reports, new product announcements, formation of strategic alliances, mergers and acquisitions) related to the automaker and supplier in the event window (which make the source of firm value loss ambiguous); (3) suppliers are from financial industries (as financial firms operate under different regulatory environments) or are OEM suppliers (in this case, the type of spillover is unclear); and (4) values in the variables of interest are missing.
<!-- para 7 -->
The period of study was from 2010 to 2019, to exclude the impacts of the global financial crisis (2008–2009) and the COVID-19 outbreak (in 2020) on the automobile industry.
The final panel dataset contained 7,931 observations based on 752 buyer–supplier dyads (6 automakers and 348 suppliers) of 154 media-reported major automobile recalls.
<!-- para 9 -->
An event study approach helps capture the negative spillover effect.
That is, in event studies, abnormal returns measure the change in the firm's stock price associated with an event.
Abnormal returns are calculated by subtracting the expected return for a given day from the actual return for that day:
<!-- para 10 -->
$$ A R_{i t}=R_{i t}-E R_{i t}, $$
<!-- para 11 -->
(1) where  $AR_{it}$ represents the abnormal return for firm i on day t,  $R_{it}$ is the actual return for firm i on day t, and  $ER_{it}$ refers to the expected return for firm i on day t.
<!-- para 12 -->
Consistent with previous studies (Arora et al., 2020; Eilert et al., 2017), the four-factor model (i.e., Fama–French three factors plus Carhart’s momentum factor) was used to estimate the expected returns.
Based on this model, the expected return is a linear function of the four factors over a given period, including market, size, book-to-market, and momentum (Carhart, 1997; Fama & French, 1996).
<!-- para 13 -->
$$ E R_{i t}=\alpha_{i}+\beta_{1}\big(R_{m t}-R_{f t}\big)+\beta_{2}\left(S M B_{t}\right)+\beta_{3}\left(H M L_{t}\right)+\beta_{4}\left(U M D_{t}\right), $$
<!-- para 14 -->
(2) where  $R_{ft}$ is the risk-free return on the same day  $t$;  $R_{mt}$ is the risk factor, which captures the average return of all stocks in the market on a given day  $t$;  $SMB_t$ is the size factor, which captures the difference in returns between small- and large-market stock portfolios on the same day  $t$;  $HML_t$ is the book-to-market factor, which reflects the difference in returns between portfolios with high and low book-to-market ratios on the same day  $t$; and  $UMD_t$ is the momentum factor, which measures the difference between portfolios with strong past performance and those with weaker past performance on day  $t$.
Coefficients  $\beta_{1-4}$ are estimated with an ordinary least squares (OLS) regression.
Following prior event studies on the automobile industry (Eilert et al., 2017; Javadinia et al., 2023), the present study estimated the OLS model over 250 trading days (from day  $t-270$ to  $t-20$) and only included observations with at least 100 days of return data during the estimation period.
<!-- para 15 -->
To capture the vertical spillover effect, this study tested whether suppliers experienced a significant, negative cumulative abnormal return (CAR) during the event window.
A negative CAR indicates a vertical spillover effect, such that suppliers experienced firm value losses due to automobile recalls.
The CAR is calculated as
<!-- para 16 -->
$$ CAR_{it}=\sum_{k=t-2}^{t+5}AR_{ik}, $$
<!-- para 17 -->
(3) where  $CAR_{it}$ is the CAR for firm i on recall event t and  $AR_{ik}$ refers to the abnormal return based on Equations (1) and (2) during the event window  $[-2, +5]$.
<!-- para 20 -->
In this analysis, the dependent variable is suppliers' firm value losses following an automobile recall, operationalized as the CAR of a recalling automaker's suppliers during the event window.
To capture suppliers' firm value losses, the CAR was multiplied by -100, with higher values indicating more firm value losses (in percentage).
The eight-day event window spanned from two trading days before the recall announcement to five trading days after (i.e.,  $[-2, +5]$).
In this event window, Day 0 refers to the recall announcement date, which is the first date of the news report about the automobile recall (Gao et al., 2015).
The reason for the selection of two days before the event was that some investors may have an information advantage and sell their stock before the event, which may lead to a reduced stock price before the media reports the same (Eilert et al., 2017).
Selection of five days afterward is appropriate because some investors may become aware of the recall announcement only after it has been widely reported.
Robustness tests using alternative event windows showed similar results (see section 5.3).
<!-- para 22 -->
Common business ties captured the total number of customers and suppliers that are connected to both the supplier and the recalling automaker in the previous financial year.
Robustness tests measured common business ties using structural equivalence (i.e., the Jaccard index) (Chae et al., 2020) and found similar results.
<!-- para 23 -->
Consistent with previous studies (Freeman, 2023), common institutional ownership was measured as the ratio of the total market value of shares owned by common institutional investors to the total market value of the buyer and supplier.
In line with Kini et al. (2024), institutional owners were those with more than 1% ownership in both the supplier and the recalling automaker.
Robustness tests measured common institutional ownership using the number of common institutional owners and found similar results.
<!-- para 24 -->
Common Analyst Coverage was measured by the number of financial analysts who covered both the recalling automaker and its suppliers in the previous financial quarter.
Robustness tests measured common analyst coverage by calculating the ratio of common analysts to the total number of analysts who covered both suppliers and automakers.
The results remained consistent.
<!-- para 26 -->
This study controlled for recall and firm characteristics that may covary with the independent variables and affect investors' reactions to negative events, including recall, supplier firm, and automaker-supplier relationship characteristics (summarized in Table 2).
First, recall characteristics are key determinants of investors' perception of the recall impact (Chen et al., 2009; Eilert et al., 2017).
This study included three recall characteristics: (1) recall volume, measured by the total number of cars recalled; (2) the source of the recall, or whether the recall was caused by suppliers; and (3) severity of the recall, or OEM firm value losses after the recall.

Regarding the effect size, the results indicate that suppliers experienced $-0.222\%$ CAR during the event window $[-2, +5]$.
This corresponds to approximately $40\%$ of the effect size of automobile recalls' direct impact on OEMs (i.e., $-0.552\%$ CAR during the event window $[-2, +5]$).
While $-0.222\%$ may appear small, in market-value terms, it translates into economically meaningful losses.
Given that the average market value for the supplier sample was $25.88 billion in 2018, this effect implies an additional loss of roughly$57.45 million for suppliers.
Thus, the results support H1.
<!-- para 11 -->
Prior studies have reported abnormal returns of a similar magnitude.
For automobile recalls, Javadinia et al. (2023) reported a -0.20% CAR for automakers on the recall event day, and Liu et al. (2017) found a -0.69% CAR for automakers over a [-2, +2] event window.
Within supply chain studies, Jacobs and Singhal (2020) found that Volkswagen itself experienced a -31.95% CAR over a [0, +5] window due to the diesel-emissions scandal.
This caused a mean CAR of -2.69% for its cost of goods sold suppliers, -0.35% for its selling, general, and administrative expenses suppliers, and -0.17% for its capital expenditure suppliers.
Similarly, Li et al. (2021b) found that suppliers' market values respond significantly to their customers' qualitative risk disclosures, with CARs of -0.51% (event window [-1, +1]), -0.66% ([-2, +2]), -0.72% ([-3, +3]), and -0.85% ([-4, +4]).

This study also tested the mechanisms underlying the vertical spillover effect (i.e., cash flow and impression effects).
To test the cash flow effect, analyst predictions data were collected from the I/B/E/S database, as analyst predictions are key drivers of stock price changes.
Specifically, cash flow reflects the number of analysts who downgraded the supplier firm's sales and earnings per share (EPS) estimations following a recall event.
Because analyst data are updated monthly, a supplier's recall intensity was measured by aggregating the number of cars recalled by its buyers (i.e., automakers) in each month (including all recalls).
As Table A3 in the Online Appendix shows, the results indicate that recall intensity (captured by the cumulative number of cars recalled by all OEM buyers of a supplier) is positively related to the number of analysts who lowered their estimation of the sales and EPS of the supplier firms over the next quarter and next year.
The results confirm the cash flow effect, indicating that automobile recalls lowered analysts' estimation of supplier firms' sales and EPS.
<!-- para 13 -->
The impression effect was tested by examining negative media coverage of the supplier firm following recall events.
Specifically, this effect was measured as the number of media reports with negative sentiment scores within one month and three months after the recall event.
Data were collected from the RavenPack database.
The results (see Table A4 in the Online Appendix) indicate that the number of negative media reports on a supplier firm after a recall event is positively related to the severity of the recall event (measured by OEM firm value losses after the recall).
These results confirm the impression effect, indicating that automaker product recalls negatively affect the media's impression of suppliers.

H2 posits that the vertical spillover effect is stronger when the supplier and automaker share more common business ties.
This hypothesis is supported, as the results show a positive and significant relationship between common ties and supplier value losses (Table 4, Model 6:  $\beta = 0.061$, p < 0.01).
<!-- para 18 -->
The results also provide support for H3.
Common institutional ownership between suppliers and the recalling automaker is negatively related to firm value losses related to product recalls (Table 4, Model 6:  $\beta = -6.035$, p < 0.01).
Thus, common institutional ownership weakens the vertical spillover effect of product recalls.

Consistent with H4, the results indicate that supplier firms with more common analyst coverage with the recalling firm experience less firm value losses associated with automobile recalls (Table 4, Model 6:  $\beta = -0.129$, p < 0.01).
Thus, common analyst coverage weakens the vertical spillover effect of automobile recalls.

Second, additional tests used alternative measures of independent variables.
Common business ties were measured by structural equivalence, which is the Jaccard index for each automaker–supplier dyad (Chae et al., 2020).
The Jaccard index is calculated by the number of common customers or suppliers between two firms (e.g., A and B) divided by these firms' total number of ties.
Common institutional ownership was measured by the number of common institutional investors.
Common analyst coverage was measured using the ratio of common analyst coverage to the total number of analysts of suppliers and automakers.
The results (presented in Table A7 in the Online Appendix) for these alternative measures of independent variables are identical to those reported in Table 4.

Third, the Heckman two-stage model helped address selection bias, since supplier selection is not random.
Factors that affect supplier selection may also affect investors' reactions to suppliers during automobile recalls.
Thus, investor reactions to suppliers during automobile recalls might be a result of supplier selection.
To address the potential selection bias, the Heckman two-stage model estimated the regression models.
The first step involved estimating a supplier selection model.
For each supplier, competitors were other firms in the same industry (same four-digit Standard Industrial Classification code) and located in the same country.
Automaker financial characteristics served as the exclusion restriction because these might affect supplier selection decisions.
However, automakers' financial condition is not likely to affect investors' reactions toward suppliers (i.e., the dependent variable).
The selection model also included the independent variables (i.e., common ties, common institutional ownership, and common analyst coverage); Table A8 in the Online Appendix presents the regression results.
The inverse Mills ratio (IMR) was estimated using the selection model.
In the second step, the IMR was included in the main model.
Table A9 presents the results after inclusion of the IMR.
The IMR coefficients in the full models (Models 5 and 6) are not significant.
In addition, the results after inclusion of IMR are identical to those before inclusion.
Thus, selection bias is not a major concern in this study.

Fourth, to address the concern that individual investors might not be aware of the buyer-supplier relationships covered in the FactSet Revere database, this study analyzed firm value losses of suppliers that are also disclosed in the Compustat Segment database.
This database was developed using the Statement of Financial Accounting Standards No. 131, which requires all publicly listed firms to disclose major buyers that account for over 10% of their total sales.
As such, these buyer-supplier relationships are public information accessible to individual investors.
The results (see Table A10 in the Online Appendix) show that the findings using Compustat Segment data are similar to those in the main analysis (see Table 3).
In addition, supplier firm value losses are greater in the Compustat Segment sample.
In the event window  $[-2, +5]$, the supplier CAR is  $-0.540\%$ versus  $-0.222\%$ in Table 3.
<!-- para 30 -->
Finally, stock prices fluctuate frequently and might rebound quickly.
Therefore, to explore the long-term effects of automobile recalls on suppliers, this study examined the performance impact of automaker recalls on suppliers; that is, how does recall intensity (of automakers) affect the profit margin and sales growth of suppliers.
This analysis aggregated the total number of recalls initiated by the buyers (i.e., automakers) of a supplier in a year.
This measure, labeled recall intensity, helped predict the profit margin and sales growth of the supplier in the following year.
The results (presented in Table A11 of the Online Appendix) indicate that recall intensity is negatively related to both the profit margin and sales growth of the supplier.
Thus, automobile recalls not only affect the short-term stock price of suppliers but also affect their long-term performance.

## discussion
<!-- para 2 -->
This study examined the vertical spillover effects of negative events and how such spillovers are affected by common business ties, common institutional ownership, and common analyst coverage between suppliers and recalling automakers.
The findings indicate that recalls by automakers negatively affect the firm value of their suppliers.
The vertical spillover effects are stronger when the firms share more common business ties but weaker under high levels of common institutional ownership and common analyst coverage.
