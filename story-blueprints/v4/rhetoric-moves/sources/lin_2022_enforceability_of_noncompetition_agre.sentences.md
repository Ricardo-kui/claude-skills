# lin_2022_enforceability_of_noncompetition_agre 句子库存

## introduction
<!-- para 2 -->
We examine whether the legal enforceability of noncompetition agreements affects corporate boards' forced-turnover decisions for chief executive officers (CEOs).
Retaining a poorly performing CEO is harmful to a company; therefore, timely replacement of the CEO is considered an essential governance mechanism (Weisbach 1988, 1995; Denis and Denis 1995; Kang and Shivdasani 1995; Denis, Denis, and Sarin 1997; Guo and Masulis 2015; Dasgupta, Li, and Wang 2018).
Despite the positive effects of forced CEO turnover, only about 2 percent of CEOs at large US corporations are fired every year, and the association between forced CEO turnover and firm performance appears to be weak (Huson, Parrino, and Starks 2001; Brickley 2003; Kaplan and Minton 2012; Jenter and Kanaan 2015; Jenter and Lewellen 2021).
Most studies attribute these seemingly suboptimal forced-turnover practices to agency problems. [^1] In this paper, we argue that the potential ex post damages caused by a departing CEO's moving to (or establishing) a competitor places a significant constraint on forced-CEO-replacement decisions.
<!-- para 3 -->
A firm's success increasingly depends on human capital and proprietary information, such as trade secrets.
If such sensitive assets fall into the hands of the firm's rivals, that can impose severe competitive harm. [^2] The turnover of employees, especially top executives, can be a significant conduit of human capital and information transfers to competing firms.
When assuming a new role, a CEO can use the know-how and proprietary information gained in the previous position to the advantage of the new employer (see Lutheran Health Network of Ind., LLC v.
Bauer, 139 N.E.3d 269 [Ind.
App. 2019]).
While proprietary-information leakage is partially discouraged by trade secret laws, the transfer of the CEO's human capital—firm specific, industry specific, or general—cannot be prevented unless the CEO is barred from working for the competition altogether.
A departing CEO can also utilize the personal relationships established over the course of her tenure to hire away key employees, which results in an employee departure wave (Vanko 2002).
Such predatory hiring can significantly damage the former employer's competitive position. [^3] Corporate boards are likely to be concerned about these potential negative consequences to firm value.
Therefore, increased protection from such competitive harm should improve the efficiency of CEO-replacement decisions.
<!-- para 4 -->
Our identification strategy relies on the state-level variation of the legal enforceability of a noncompetition agreement, also known as a covenant not to compete (CNC).
The CNC is a widely used contractual mechanism to discourage employees, particularly high-level executives, from undertaking jobs at rival firms or running competing businesses following the termination of their current employment.
These compacts are a powerful tool for protecting employers from competitive harm since they discourage not only information leakages but also human capital transfers.
<!-- para 5 -->
We use state-level changes in the enforceability of CNCs (hereafter, CNC enforceability) instead of data on individual CNCs for identification.
Most companies do not disclose whether they entered into a CNC with their CEOs.
Therefore, CNCs are mostly unobservable to the researcher. [^4] Also, while some companies do disclose their contracts with CEOs, the decision to include CNCs in executive contracts and to disclose those contracts can be related to unmodeled firm-specific characteristics (Gillan, Hartzell, and Parrino 2009), which results in selection bias. [^5]
<!-- para 6 -->
As noted in Garmaise (2011), the legal enforceability of CNCs was substantially and unexpectedly altered in three US states (Texas, Florida, and Louisiana) between 1992 and 2004.
The changes are unlikely to be related to a company's ex ante characteristics that are correlated with forced-turnover decisions (Garmaise 2011; Chen, Zhang, and Zhou 2018).
We take advantage of these changes in the legal environment and conduct a difference-in-difference estimation, controlling for unobservable year- and firm-specific factors that may be jointly determined with other corporate governance mechanisms (Hermalin and Weisbach 1998; Bertrand and Mullainathan 2003; Guo and Masulis 2015).
<!-- para 7 -->
We examine whether CNC enforceability is associated with forced-CEO-turnover decisions.
Consistent with our expectation, we find that the likelihood of forced CEO turnover significantly increases in response to an increase in CNC enforceability.
The coefficient estimate suggests that an increase in CNC enforceability leads to a 1.0 percent increase in the likelihood of forced CEO turnover, representing a 40 percent increase relative to the average forced-CEO-turnover rate in our sample (2.5 percent).
We also examine the sensitivity of forced CEO turnover to firm performance and find that it also significantly increases after positive shocks to CNC enforceability.
On average, a 1-standard-deviation decrease in firm performance increases the likelihood of forced CEO turnover by 2.53 percent.
However, if CNC enforceability strengthens, the likelihood of forced CEO turnover rises by an additional 1.08 percent, representing a 43.04 percent incremental increase relative to the unconditional mean.
<!-- para 8 -->
As a falsification test, we examine the effects of CNC enforceability on voluntary CEO turnover.
The decision maker of voluntary turnover is the CEO herself, who compares the personal costs and benefits of job switching (for example, comparing transition costs with an increase in salary).
An increase in CNC enforceability reduces the set of potential new employers, thereby decreasing the benefits of job switching and discouraging voluntary turnover.
Our results confirm this conjecture.
This falsification test strengthens our inference and highlights the importance of distinguishing forced and voluntary CEO turnover when assessing the effects of CNCs. [^6]
<!-- para 9 -->
To substantiate our inference, we perform additional cross-sectional tests.
First, we examine whether the effects are more pronounced for firms facing significant product market threats.
Such firms are more likely to be concerned about potential competitive harm caused by CEOs' departures and impose more significant constraints on boards' turnover decisions.
If improved CNC enforceability lifts these constraints, we expect to find stronger effects on forced CEO turnover for these firms than for firms facing less significant product market threats.
Using in-state competition measures (Garmaise 2011; Chen, Zhang, and Zhou 2018), we find consistent evidence.
Second, we test whether the effects of CNC enforceability are stronger for firms facing a greater potential threat of predatory hiring by rival firms.
Increased CNC enforceability discourages predatory hiring via two channels.
First, strengthened CNC enforceability makes the nonsolicitation agreements between departing CEOs and firms more binding.
Second, even if such a nonsolicitation agreement is absent, strengthened CNC enforceability still discourages key employees from switching jobs via CNCs in their employment contracts.
Using the industry average level of employee turnover as a proxy for threats of predatory hiring, we find consistent evidence.
<!-- para 10 -->
We also examine how the market reaction to the announcement of a forced CEO turnover varies with CNC enforceability.
We document a more positive announcement effect after enforceability increases.
This finding provides other evidence to support our argument that increased CNC enforceability enhances the efficiency of CEO replacement decisions and thereby increases firm value.
<!-- para 11 -->
An important question is whether CNCs affect boards' dismissal decisions by preventing the transfer of human capital or proprietary information to a competitor.
To shed light on this question, we rerun our main tests using states' enactment of the Uniform Trade Secrets Act (UTSA) and adoption of the Inevitable Disclosure Doctrine (IDD) as our key independent variables in place of changes in CNC enforceability.
We find very weak or even insignificant effects of the UTSA and IDD on forced CEO turnover.
Since the UTSA and IDD specifically protect trade secrets, this finding suggests that CNCs affect forced CEO turnover primarily through preventing the transfer of managerial human capital to competing firms.
<!-- para 12 -->
Our paper contributes to the literature in two ways.
First, our study provides evidence that the potential competitive harm caused by a departing CEO affects corporate boards' dismissal decisions.
The implicit assumption embedded in the prior literature is that the low rate of forced CEO turnover is primarily due to agency problems.
Thus, governance mechanisms such as independent directors, institutional investors, and product market competition are value increasing because they reduce these agency costs (see Weisbach 1988; Denis, Denis, and Sarin 1997; Guo and Masulis 2015; Dasgupta, Li, Wang 2018).
One challenge in this literature is that the optimality of forced-CEO-turnover decisions is difficult to assess because of the lack of knowledge about potential direct costs that corporate boards consider in their CEO replacement decisions.
Taylor (2010) builds a structural model of forced CEO turnover that incorporates direct costs of dismissals, but he is silent on the question of what constitutes these costs.
We provide empirical evidence showing that the potential competitive harm caused by the departing CEO can be such a direct cost.
<!-- para 13 -->
Second, this paper adds to the literature examining whether changes in CNC enforceability or adoption of the UTSA affects the external job opportunities of employees (Marx, Strumsky, and Fleming 2009; Garmaise 2011; Png 2017a). [^7] Prior studies primarily focus on the costs imposed on managers due to reduced mobility and the negative consequences to firm value (Garmaise 2011; Samila and Sorenson 2011; Chen, Zhang, and Zhou 2018; Tang, Wang, and Zhou 2021).
What has been largely overlooked is the possibility that improved enforceability of CNCs enhances the efficiency of corporate boards' CEO replacement decisions.
Our paper fills this gap.

## theory
<!-- para 3 -->
Studies of CEO turnover date back to the 1980s, when scholars tried to evaluate changes in firm performance around CEO turnover (Coughlan and Schmidt 1985; Warner, Watts, and Wruck 1988; Weisbach 1988).
While the inverse relationship between CEO turnover and performance is statistically significant in some studies, its economic significance appears to be small in most studies.
For example, according to a survey in Brickley (2003), moving from the top to the bottom decile of performance increases the likelihood of CEO turnover in publicly traded firms by only about 4 percent.
<!-- para 4 -->
Prior studies seek to explain the weak sensitivity of CEO turnover to firm performance with agency theory.
Put differently, the literature focuses on internal corporate governance mechanisms, such as the board of directors and block shareholders, and examines whether effective governance mechanisms play a crucial role in removing poorly performing CEOs.
For example, Weisbach (1988) finds that among firms with outsider-dominated boards, the likelihood of CEO turnover increases by 5.7 percent when moving from the top to the bottom performance decile.
In contrast, among firms with insider-dominated boards, the likelihood increases by only 2.2 percent.
Goyal and Park (2002) document a much weaker sensitivity of turnover to performance in firms whose board chair and CEO duties are vested in the same individual.
Denis, Denis, and Sarin (1997) and Kaplan and Minton (2012) focus on the effects of block shareholders and find that such shareholders exercise a mild disciplinary effect in removing non-performing CEOs.
<!-- para 5 -->
A critical limitation of the studies above lies in the endogenous nature of corporate governance mechanisms (Hermalin and Weisbach 1998).
Corporate governance mechanisms, including forced CEO turnover, are jointly determined by characteristics of firms that could be unobservable or cannot be modeled.
Therefore, recent studies apply more rigorous empirical designs to address the endogeneity issue.
Guo and Masulis (2015) rely on the exogenous change in board and committee independence due to the passage of the Sarbanes-Oxley Act in 2002 to examine the causal relation between board structure and CEO turnover.
Dasgupta, Li, and Wang (2018) focus on the disciplinary effects of product market competition on forced-CEO-turnover decisions exploiting exogenous changes in industry-level tariffs as a shock to product market competition.
In sum, both causal and correlational studies suggest that the low sensitivity of CEO turnover to performance can be attributed to the lack of monitoring mechanisms.
<!-- para 7 -->
A CNC prohibits a departing employee, regardless of the reasons for departure, from competing with her former employer, either as a new employee of a rival firm or by starting a new firm.
The economic rationale for a CNC is to discourage the transfer of employees' know-how, acquired knowledge, and trade secrets to competitors through the departing employee.
Garmaise (2011) finds that 70.2 percent of firms use CNCs for their top executives.
A report by the Society of Human Resource Management (Zeidner 2017) also indicates that CNCs are widely used by companies to maintain confidentiality, which suggests that unintended negative consequences due to employee turnover are a significant concern for a majority of public companies.
<!-- para 8 -->
However, unlike most contract terms, CNCs need not be enforceable.
The enforceability of a CNC is the subject of heated debate among both legislators and scholars.
A main reason for the debate is that the CNC does not fit perfectly into a single area of law.
Instead, it involves concepts from both contract law and trade secret law.
<!-- para 9 -->
According to the contract law theory, courts should not scrutinize the adequacy of consideration or vitiate an otherwise valid contract to protect a party to an unfair transaction (Collins 2003).
That is, the court does not scrutinize the reasonableness of contract terms but leaves it to the parties to determine what they value and the price that they are willing to pay for any item.
From this perspective, the employer and the employee have the legal rights to include a CNC in an employment contract freely.
<!-- para 10 -->
From the perspective of trade secret law, the doctrine above does not necessarily apply.
In particular, a CNC can be viewed as a mechanism protecting employers' information, trade secrets, and employees' know-how.
Although a CNC can protect the interests of employers, the courts need not view this protection as entirely in line with public interests.
Many legislators and scholars believe that employee mobility is essential for information and knowledge transfer and plays a critical role in spurring innovation.
For example, Gilson (1999) asserts that California's law against enforcing a CNC provides a superior legal infrastructure for innovation, which explains the rise of Silicon Valley.
In addition, some scholars argue that a CNC violates an individual's fundamental right to earn a living (Graves 2011).
<!-- para 11 -->
For these reasons, there is no federal law governing the administration of CNCs.
Instead, firms are free to include any CNC in an employment contract, but its enforceability is determined by state law and therefore varies across states.
In some US states, the enforcement of CNCs must be based on a reasonableness test.
For example, Colorado is more permissive of CNCs in employment agreements for top executives.
Some states, such as California, prohibit the enforcement of a CNC.
Several other states, such as Texas, have increased the enforceability of CNCs (Garmaise 2011; Chen, Zhang, and Zhou 2018).
<!-- para 13 -->
Forced CEO turnover is an essential corporate governance mechanism.
However, such a decision also imposes costs on shareholders and boards of directors.
These costs may discourage a board of directors from dismissing a CEO, which leads to a lower turnover rate.
However, the various costs to shareholders resulting from a forced CEO turnover are not precisely defined or measured in prior studies.
We posit that the possibility that a departing CEO moves to or establishes a competitor, and uses know-how or confidential information acquired during her previous employment to the advantage of the competitor, represents a significant cost to shareholders.
<!-- para 14 -->
Turnover of CEOs inevitably results in human capital transfers and information spillovers among companies.
This can impose severe competitive harm on the prior employer (and its shareholders) and thus frequently leads to legal disputes.
For example, in November 2017 Lutheran Health Network, the largest health care provider in northeastern Indiana, sued its former CEO, Brian Bauer, for breach of contract for using confidential information and know-how to lure competitor Indiana University Health to the Fort Wayne area.
The Lutheran Health Network had fired Bauer because of his poor performance in a previous mergers and acquisitions deal.
However, subsequently Bauer was hired by Indiana University Health to help build a new primary-care practice in Fort Wayne (Lutheran Health Network of Ind., LLC v.
Bauer, 139 N.E.3d 269 [Ind.
App. 2019]).
Lutheran Health Network was concerned about know-how misappropriation, leakage of confidential information, and the resultant competitive harms and therefore filed a lawsuit against Bauer in Indiana.
<!-- para 15 -->
A departing executive may start a competing business.
For example, on July 14, 2011, John Kanas and John Bohlsen, two former senior executives of North Fork Bank, were alleged to breach a noncompete agreement they signed with the company (Capital One Financial Corporation v.
Kanas, 871 F.
Supp. 2d 520 [2012]).
The noncompete agreement was specially set to prevent them from misappropriating the company's proprietary information and running a competing banking business in the same state.
The probability of a departing executive establishing a competing business can be higher following dismissal, as she may face more difficulties finding a similar position in other companies because of a damaged reputation.
<!-- para 16 -->
Finally, executive departures can cause nonexecutive employees to switch jobs as well, which leads to an employee turnover wave.
Top executives typically have good knowledge of, and established relationships with, the key talent in the organization.
This increases the likelihood of predatory hiring.
For example, Robert Moyes, former president of the TerraCom Division of Conic, was alleged to solicit key TerraCom employees on behalf of his new employer, Aydin Corporation (Loral Corp. v.
Moyes, 174 Cal.
App. 3d 784 [Cal.
Ct.
App. 1985]).
Conic claimed that a large number of key employees had interviewed with Moyes and had been offered jobs at Aydin, which resulted in a turnover wave.
TerraCom had to spend over $400,000 to recruit new employees.
<!-- para 17 -->
To address these potential negative effects resulting from executive turnovers, many firms rely on CNCs to safeguard against the risk of competitive harm caused by departing CEOs.
However, as discussed in Section 2.2, because of controversies surrounding CNCs, different states hold different opinions regarding their enforcement. [^8]
<!-- para 18 -->
Several states, including Texas, Florida, and Louisiana, experienced significant changes in CNC enforceability between 1992 and 2004, which we describe in detail in Section 3.
These changes create a natural laboratory that allows us to inves- tigate whether corporate boards consider the potential cost of competitive harm before they make CEO replacement decisions.
An increase in CNC enforceability should reduce the potential cost of competitive harm in case of a CEO turnover, which in turn should increase the likelihood of forced CEO turnover.
In contrast, a reduction in CNC enforceability increases the potential risk of knowledge transfers by a departing CEO and thereby discourages the board from firing her.
This discussion leads to hypothesis 1.
<!-- para 19 -->
**Hypothesis 1.** The likelihood of forced CEO turnover increases (decreases) for affected firms following an increase (a decrease) in CNC enforceability.
<!-- para 20 -->
Prior literature often relates forced CEO turnover to firm performance because firm performance serves as a signal of a CEO's ability.
Empirical studies find an inverse relationship between forced CEO turnover and firm performance and find that effective corporate governance strengthens that relationship (Hermalin and Weisbach 1998; Guo and Masulis 2015; Dasgupta, Li, and Wang 2018).
Along the same lines, we examine whether changes in CNC enforceability influence the sensitivity of turnover to firm performance.
More specifically, we expect that increased CNC enforceability allows boards to dismiss CEOs more swiftly when performance deteriorates, thereby strengthening the inverse relationship between forced CEO turnover and firm performance.
This leads to hypothesis 2.
<!-- para 21 -->
**Hypothesis 2.** The inverse relationship between forced CEO turnover and firm performance increases (decreases) for affected firms following an increase (a decrease) in CNC enforceability.
<!-- para 22 -->
Unlike forced CEO turnover, voluntary CEO turnover is a decision made by CEOs themselves and therefore is driven by a different set of factors.
In particular, a CEO's objective in a voluntary turnover is to maximize her utility rather than shareholder wealth.
The CEO would compare the costs of job switching, such as transition costs, with the benefits, such as greater pay.
Enhanced CNC enforceability reduces the set of potential alternative employers, thereby decreasing the benefits of job switching and reducing the mobility of CEOs (Garmaise 2011).
<!-- para 23 -->
**Hypothesis 3.** The likelihood of voluntary CEO turnover decreases (increases) for affected firms following an increase (a decrease) in CNC enforceability.

## methods
<!-- para 3 -->
To mitigate the potential endogeneity issue and establish causality, we use a staggered difference-in-difference research design with firm and year fixed effects (Bertrand and Mullainathan 2003).
Following prior studies, we consider the time-series changes in CNC enforceability at the state level to be exogenous to a firm's ex ante characteristics (Garmaise 2011; Aobdia 2018; Chen, Zhang, and Zhou 2018).
We examine shocks to CNC enforceability at the state level, which exogenously affect different firms at different times in different directions.
Thus, our identification avoids a common challenge of settings with a single exogenous shock.
We estimate the following regression model to examine how changes in CNC enforceability affect the likelihood of forced CEO turnover:
<!-- para 4 -->
$$
\mathrm{Forced\ Turnover}=\beta_{1}\mathrm{Enforce\ CNC}+\beta_{2}\mathrm{Return}+\beta_{3}\mathrm{Size}+\beta_{4}\mathrm{Market\ to\ Book} \begin{aligned}&+\beta_{5}Leverage+\beta_{6}Return Volatility+\beta_{7}Duality\\&+\beta_{8}Tenure+\beta_{9}Age>60+\beta_{10}CEO Ownership\\&+\beta_{11}Equity Pay>0+\beta_{12}Delta\\&+\beta_{13}State Unemployment+\beta_{14}State GDP+\phi_{i}+\eta_{t}+\varepsilon,\\ \end{aligned} \tag{1}
$$
<!-- para 5 -->
where Forced Turnover is an indicator variable equal to one if a forced CEO turnover occurs in period t and zero otherwise; Enforce CNC is our main explanatory variable;  $\varphi_i$ and  $\eta_t$ are firm and year fixed effects, respectively; and all other variables are controls.
Forced turnover is identified following the procedures in Parrino (1997) and Peters and Wagner (2014). [^9] The dependent variable captures a change in the employment status of the CEO.
Therefore, the primary explanatory variable of interest, Enforce CNC, is defined as an ordinal variable equal to 1 for states increasing CNC enforceability, -1 for states decreasing CNC enforceability, and 0 otherwise (Garmaise 2011; Chen, Zhang, and Zhou 2018).
Garmaise (2011) identifies three states that significantly amended the enforceability of a CNC over the period 1992–2004.
Florida increased CNC enforceability beginning in 1997.
Louisiana reduced CNC enforceability during the period 2002–3 but increased it again beginning in 2004.
Enforceability in Texas was decreased effectively beginning in 1995.
Therefore, Enforce CNC equals 1 for firms in Florida between 1997 and 2004, -1 for firms in Louisiana between 2002 and 2003 and for firms in Texas between 1995 and 2004, and 0 otherwise.
<!-- para 6 -->
We note that Ertimur et al. (2018) identify additional changes in CNC enforceability after 2004 in several US states.
However, these changes did not modify the enforceability index by more than 1 point.
We therefore focus on the more significant changes in CNC enforceability identified by Garmaise (2011), as do other studies (Aobdia 2018; Cici, Hendriock, and Kempf 2021; Chen, Zhang, and Zhou 2018).
We conduct multiple robustness tests to show that our findings are not sensitive to this restriction.
<!-- para 7 -->
To capture the changes in the sensitivity of forced CEO turnover to firm performance in response to changes in CNC enforceability, we estimate the following regression model:
<!-- para 8 -->
$$
\begin{aligned}Forced Turnover&=\beta_{1}Enforce CNC+\beta_{2}Enforce CNC\times Return\\&+\beta_{3}Return+\beta_{4}Size+\beta_{5}Market to Book+\beta_{6}Leverage\\&+\beta_{7}Return Volatility+\beta_{8}Duality+\beta_{9}Tenure\\&+\beta_{10}Age>60+\beta_{11}CEO Ownership+\beta_{12}Equity Pay>0\\&+\beta_{13}Delta+\beta_{14}State Unemployment+\beta_{15}State GDP\\&+\phi_{i}+\eta_{t}+\varepsilon,\\ \end{aligned} \tag{2}
$$
<!-- para 9 -->
where the key variable of interest is the interaction of Enforce CNC and Return.
If a forced CEO turnover occurs in period t, Return is measured as the market-adjusted stock return over the 12-month period preceding the announcement month of the turnover (Peters and Wagner 2014).
If there is no forced CEO turnover in period t, Return is measured as the annual market-adjusted return in period $t-1$.
<!-- para 10 -->
We use the stock return as a primary performance measure of a firm for three reasons.
First, the stock return is a direct measure of shareholder wealth maximization that corporate boards consider when they make CEO replacement decisions.
Second, stock returns are less subject to direct manipulation by top managers.
Chen, Zhang, and Zhou (2018) document that the increase in CNC enforceability leads to earnings management because of managerial career concerns, which distorts the earnings' informativeness regarding the effort and ability of the CEO.
Hence, firm performance is captured by stock returns more appropriately in our setting.
Third, using operating performance measures does not allow us to address the timing problem in the forced-turnover setting.
For instance, when using lagged operating performance, there might be a significant gap between the departure date of a CEO and the period in which firm performance is measured. [^10] Contemporaneous operating performance could also be problematic if a CEO left the firm early in the fiscal period.
<!-- para 11 -->
We also include a comprehensive set of control variables in the regression model to control for firm-level, CEO-level, and state-level characteristics that would affect CEO turnovers (Goyal and Park 2002; Garmaise 2011; Peters and Wagner 2014; Guo and Masulis 2015).
Appendix A provides further details about these control variables.
<!-- para 12 -->
We employ a linear probability model with firm and year fixed effects for the empirical analyses (Cornelli, Kominek, and Ljungqvist 2013; Guo and Masulis 2015).
Controlling for unobservable firm-specific characteristics via firm fixed effects is essential in our setting in which corporate governance mechanisms are endogenously determined jointly with unobservable characteristics of firms (Hermalin and Weisbach 1998).
We use a linear probability model because it is more suitable in a setting with a large number of fixed effects (Neyman and Scott 1948), and it has the additional advantage that, in contrast to nonlinear models, interpretation of the marginal effects of interaction variables is straightforward (Ai and Norton 2003).
We cluster standard errors by state (Garmaise 2011; Chen, Zhang, and Zhou 2018).
<!-- para 14 -->
We use hand-collected data on forced CEO turnovers for all Execucomp CEOs over the period 1992–2004, which coincides with the sample period analyzed in Garmaise (2011) and Chen, Zhang, and Zhou (2018).
The procedure to classify turnover decisions as forced follows Parrino (1997) and uses press reports along with an age criterion and further refinements (Peters and Wagner 2014).
We use the location of the firm's headquarters to measure CNC enforceability because CNCs are enforced on the basis of the employee's principal place of employment.
That is, we assume that the CEO and key employees are principally employed at the firm's headquarters (Armstrong et al. 2019).
The empirical issue with the location data in the Compustat database is that only the firm's most recent address, not a historical one, is available.
Thus, we use the firm's historical location data, manually collected from its 10-K filing each year, and if the location is missing we use the address provided by the Compustat database (Jennings, Lee, and Matsumoto 2017; Jennings et al. 2020). [^11] We obtain data on whether the CEO is the chairman of the board, CEO tenure, CEO age, and CEO ownership from the Execucomp database.
Following Garvey and Milbourn (2006), we use a sample of Execucomp firms with nonnegative CEO tenure.
We retrieve stock return data from the Center for Research in Security Prices and financial statement data from the Compustat database, and we require the stock return and financial data to be nonmissing.
These data requirements yield a sample of 18,390 firm-year observations that correspond to 2,356 firms.
Our sample contains 451 forced and 1,369 nonforced CEO turnovers (a total of 1,820 turnovers).
To avoid the undue influence of outliers, continuous variables are winsorized at the .5 and 99.5 percentiles.

<!-- para 15 -->
We argue that an increase in CNC enforceability reduces the risk of confidential information falling into the hands of rivals, which reduces the cost of competitive harm caused by forced CEO turnover.
If this is indeed true, we expect to observe stronger effects of increased CNC enforceability for firms facing more severe ex ante competitive threats.
This is because such firms should have more significant concerns about competitive damages due to forced CEO turnover, which would lead to constrained firing.
When CNC enforceability increases and thus the constraint is reduced, firms can adapt their dismissal decisions to a greater extent.

We note that if an increase in CNC enforceability is associated with a change in product market competition, it can affect CEO turnover via a disciplinary channel of market competition.
Prior research shows that increased product market competition leads to an increase in forced CEO turnover and sensitivity of turnover to performance (Dasgupta, Li, and Wang 2018).
This is because firms facing severe product market competition are incentivized to improve organizational and operational efficiency, which leads to more efficient CEO turnover decisions to reduce agency costs.
The underlying assumption is that forced CEO turnover is not optimal because of a lack of monitoring and agency problems.
In our setting, we instead focus on the competitive costs that could arise from firing CEOs, even if assuming away agency problems, and examine whether increased CNC enforceability reduces such costs.
<!-- para 26 -->
Furthermore, even if we admit the possibility that changes in CNC enforceability directly lead to changes in product market competition, the direction of the treatment effect under this possibility is the opposite of our hypothesis.
Prior research shows that increased CNC enforceability leads to better protection of proprietary information, which reduces product market competition and thereby its disciplinary effect (Acharya, Baghai, and Subramanian 2013; Png 2017a; Conti 2014).
As a result, the decreased disciplinary effect works against finding evidence consistent with our hypothesis. [^15]

## discussion
<!-- para 2 -->
This study documents that improved enforceability of noncompetition agreements increases the frequency of forced CEO turnover and the sensitivity of CEO turnover to performance.
These findings suggest that the risk of competitive harm caused by a departing CEO is an important factor in corporate boards' CEO replacement decisions.
More generally, these results inform the discussion of the economic trade-offs of contractual agreements that restrict employees' mobility and highlight a benefit of CNCs that has been largely overlooked: by reducing the potential costs of competitive harm caused by a departing CEO, CNCs allow firms to make more efficient replacement decisions.
