# lu_2022_frenemies_corporate_advertising_under_common_ownersh 句子库存

## introduction
<!-- para 2 -->
Advertising is one of the most visible marketing expenditures.
Advertising Age (2019) reports that the top 200 leading national advertisers in the United States spent a total of $163 billion on advertising in 2018.
Zenith Media Services Inc. predicts that global advertising expenditure could reach $623 billion in 2019.[^1] Advertising spending is a cost associated with providing information to consumers and maintaining a presence in a competitive market and is an important strategic decision for firms.
A large body of literature has examined how demand factors and market competition affect advertising spending (Chintagunta and Vilcassim 1992, Villas-Boas 1993, Slade 1995, Bronnenberg 1998, Bass et al. 2005, Dubé et al. 2005, Erickson 2009, Fischer et al. 2011, Freimer and Horsky 2012).
However, little attention has been paid to how organizational factors—in particular, the ownership structure of the firm—affect advertising strategy, although anecdotal evidence is abundant.
For example, after U.S. Airways Group and AMR Corporation, the parent of American Airlines, became commonly owned by Tiger Management LLC in 1996, these two companies formed a broad marketing alliance in 1998.[^2]
<!-- para 3 -->
Our aim in this paper is to empirically examine the effect of institutional common ownership of same-industry firms on firms' advertising expenditures.
Common ownership refers to a type of ownership structure in which the same shareholder simultaneously holds ownership stakes in multiple firms.
At present, common ownership is widespread among publicly traded firms.
The fraction of U.S. public firms held by institutional blockholders (i.e., institutional investors that hold at least 5% of the common equity of a given firm [^3]) that simultaneously hold other firms in the same industry has increased dramatically, from below 10% in 1980 to approximately 60% in 2018, as documented in the recent literature (e.g., Azar 2012, 2017, 2020; Azar and Vives 2021; Backus et al. 2021). [^4]
<!-- para 4 -->
Common ownership may have significant implications for firm competition.
When firms predominantly controlled or influenced by shareholders who also hold large stakes in competitors act in these shareholders' interests, they would, in theory, maximize their beneficiary owners' utilities (i.e., their portfolio values) instead of individual firm value.
There has been theoretical work showing that shareholders' common ownership across firms in the same industry could reduce competition between firms (e.g., Hansen and Lott 1996, O'Brien and Salop 2000, Gordon 2003, Rubin 2006).
Advertising spending, as an important competitive tool, is often combative in nature (Bagwell 2007).
We, therefore, hypothesize that common ownership may foster coordination in advertising strategy through reduced advertising expenditure among firms owned by the same blockholder.
<!-- para 5 -->
There has been limited empirical research examining the consequences of common ownership on firms' strategy.
A potential reason for this limited attention is that common ownership has surged only in recent years.
Another reason is the challenge in identifying the causal impact of common ownership on firms' strategy.
The concern is that institutional blockholders do not invest randomly.
Institutions may choose to invest in firms with particular product market prospects.
Moreover, unobservable firm characteristics, such as corporate culture or managerial traits, may correlate with both common ownership and advertising strategy, leading to endogeneity concerns.
<!-- para 6 -->
To address these issues, we exploit the quasinatural experiment of the mergers of financial institutions (e.g., bank holding companies, security brokers, and asset management firms) and use a Difference-in-Differences (DiD) approach to identify the effect.
When two financial institutions merge, a firm block held by one of them is likely to experience an increase in common ownership when one of its rivals is block held by the other merging institution before the merger.
Thus, the treatment sample consists of firms that experience increased ownership linkages with same-industry firms because of mergers.
The control sample, on the other hand, consists of other block-held firms in the same institution's portfolio that do not experience the common-ownership change.
<!-- para 7 -->
The merger-induced common-ownership change is exogenous to the advertising strategy because the merger of two financial institutions is unlikely to be driven by their holdings of some specific stocks.
Most mergers are between banks, and these are often driven by financial deregulation.
Mergers between nonbank financial institutions, for their part, are often driven by attempts to achieve economies of scale in fund operations or build market share by expanding financial product offerings (Jayaraman et al. 2002, He and Huang 2017).
<!-- para 8 -->
We begin by verifying the validity of the setting by showing that before the mutual fund mergers, the treatment and control groups have similar trends in advertising expenditures.
However, after the mergers, the firms in the control group do not exhibit a change in the trend of their advertising expenditures, whereas the treated firms significantly reduce theirs.
The Difference-in-Differences results further show that the treated firms on average experience a reduction in advertising expenditure of 17% (advertising-to-sales ratio of 0.5%).
These effects are both statistically and economically significant.
The result is consistent with the prediction that firms owned by the same blockholder are likely to coordinate and therefore, reduce their advertising expenditures.
<!-- para 9 -->
Our results are robust to the use of alternative event windows.
We also conduct our Difference-in-Differences tests based on propensity score-matched samples to ensure that the treatment and control firms are similar on observable characteristics.
Our results are not driven by preexisting overlapping institutional ownership of the treated firm pair.
We further use different sets of control firms, control variables, firm fixed effects, merger fixed effects, and merger-firm fixed effects and obtain consistent results.
<!-- para 10 -->
We further explore the cross-sectional analysis to examine how the benefit and cost of coordination affect corporate advertising.
We have three measures of the benefit of coordination—the competitiveness of the product market among the treated firms, as measured by product similarity; the magnitude of the merging institutions' common ownership; and advertising intensity before the ownership change.
The empirical findings are consistent with our conjecture that a greater coordination benefit is associated with a larger reduction in advertising.
On the cost side, we use the location of the treated firms and the magnitude of total institutional ownership as proxies for the coordination cost.
Consistent with our conjecture, we find that treated firms with headquarters in the same state, more concentrated institutional ownership, and thus, lower coordination costs reduce advertising expenditure more.
Moreover, we find supporting evidence that if the sum of the market share of treated firms is small relative to that of the largest firms in the same industry, the treatment firms are less likely to cut the advertising because the major competition is not between treated firms.
<!-- para 11 -->
In summary, our empirical analysis provides consistent and robust evidence that firms' advertising expenditures are affected by common ownership of their shares and that reductions in advertising expenditures can reflect the coordination between firms owned by the same blockholder.
<!-- para 12 -->
This paper contributes to the growing empirical literature on common ownership and firm outcomes.
Azar et al. (2021, 2018) investigate the impact of common ownership on pricing in airline companies and banks.
Newham et al. (2018) and Xie and Gerakos (2021) investigate the link between common ownership and pharmaceutical entry.
He and Huang (2017) and Semov (2017) find evidence of product-market coordination by commonly owned firms.
Our analysis of firms' advertising expenditure provides another piece of empirical evidence that common ownership may induce coordination.
<!-- para 13 -->
This paper also contributes to interdisciplinary research on marketing and finance.
A large body of literature has studied the impact of advertising expenditures and marketing activities on firm valuation and market capitalization (e.g., Tuli and Bharadwaj 2009, Joshi and Hanssens 2010, Bharadwaj et al. 2011, Luo and de Jong 2012, Edeling and Fischer 2016, McAlister et al. 2016, Frennea et al. 2019).
A few papers examine the feedback effect of the stock market on firms' subsequent marketing decisions (e.g., Markovitch and Golder 2008; Chakravarty and Grewal 2011; Park et al. 2019a, b). [^5] Our paper contributes to this stream of literature and is the first paper to examine the impact of ownership structure on firms' advertising spending.
<!-- para 14 -->
The remainder of the paper is organized as follows.

Section 3 discusses the identification strategy and empirical model.
Section 4 presents the main results of the Difference-in-Differences estimators and robustness tests.
Section 5 implements a series of cross-sectional variation tests.
Section 6 discusses the implications of this study and concludes the paper.

## theory
<!-- para 2 -->
The objective of our research is to evaluate the impact of common ownership on the advertising expenditures of the firms involved.
We draw upon prior studies in economics, finance, and marketing on common ownership and competition, advertising, and coordination to develop our hypotheses.
The conceptual framework is presented in Figure 1, and we explain the details in the following.
<!-- para 4 -->
Common ownership refers to a type of ownership structure in which the same shareholder simultaneously holds ownership stakes in multiple firms.
Institutional investors, for example, typically hold large stakes in many firms.
Over the past few decades, the asset management industry has become more concentrated, which contributes to the trend whereby publicly traded firms are increasingly interconnected through common ownership.
<!-- para 5 -->
There are several different motives for funds to own multiple firms in the same industry.
First, many funds have a preset investment target (e.g., health mutual funds would focus on the medical and healthcare industry).
Therefore, these funds would own multiple rival firms (e.g., Fidelity Select Pharmaceuticals Portfolio).
Second, some funds are very large and inevitably hold many firms in the same industry.
Third, from the institutional investors' perspective, one important benefit of institutional common holdings is economies of scale in information production (Kacperczyk et al. 2005).
<!-- para 6 -->
The common ownership structure challenges the premise in the classical theory that firms act independently to maximize their value, as the objective of shareholders crossowning rival firms is to maximize their portfolio profit.
The intuition is straightforward.
The benefits of one firm from competing aggressively—for example, lowering its price to gain market share—come at the expense of firms that are part of the same investors' portfolio, leading to a reduction in total portfolio value.
An investor holding stakes in both firms would enjoy greater portfolio profits if the two firms set prices or quantities as if they were two divisions of a single firm instead of as two independent firms.
The theory thus predicts that common owners would internalize the external effects through implicit or explicit coordination among the firms.

<!-- para 10 -->
A large theoretical literature has argued that common ownership of rival firms, via mutual corporate equity holdings, could reduce competition (e.g., Rotemberg 1984, Gordon 1990, Admati et al. 1994, Rubin 2006, Margotta 2010, Azar 2012, Azar et al. 2018) and lead to higher prices (e.g., Reynolds and Snapp 1986, Farrell and Shapiro 1990, Gilo et al. 2006) or collusive outcomes (e.g., Parker and Röller 1997).
Hansen and Lott (1996) provide a theory whereby crossholders maximize their portfolio values by inducing their portfolio firms to internalize negative externalities.
A similar argument can be found in the industrial organization literature on coalition formation among firms (e.g., Yi 1997, Bloch 1997).
<!-- para 11 -->
In contrast to the theoretical side, empirical studies in this area remain limited.
Azar et al. (2018) find that common ownership has an anticompetitive effect on the airline industry.
He and Huang (2017) find that commonly owned firms experience significantly higher market share growth, innovation productivity, and operating profitability because of product-market coordination.
Semov (2017) finds that commonly owned firms move closer together in the product space and hold less cash, which is consistent with a response to relax competition and greater incentives to coordinate or collaborate on product-market strategies.
Following the literature, we hypothesize that common ownership can foster coordination and reduce competition among competing firms.
<!-- para 13 -->
In this paper, we focus on the implications of common ownership for firms' advertising spending.
Advertising is one of the most visible marketing expenditures and is an important strategic tool in market competition.
The potential coordination that arises from common ownership is likely to reflect on the firm's advertising strategy.
<!-- para 14 -->
There is a large literature in marketing showing the importance of advertising in market competition.
The use of advertising to drive product sales has been empirically supported (e.g., Lodish et al. 1995, Nijs et al. 2001, Hanssens 2009, Simester et al. 2009).
Assuming that potential demand is fixed, a sales increase for one brand implies a loss for its competitors.
Research shows that advertising is often combative (Bagwell 2007).
An increase in advertising by one firm can lead to sales reductions for its rivals, and firms tend to increase their advertising expenditures in the face of increased competition or entries by new competitors (e.g., Alemson 1970, Thomas 1999).
Chen et al. (2009) provide both theoretical and empirical evidence that combative advertising by competing brands can result in more indifferent consumers and intensify competition.
Although much of the past research indicates the competitive nature of advertising, some empirical research shows that advertising can potentially have positive spillovers on a firm's rivals.
For example, Anderson and Simester (2013) find that competitors' advertisements may activate the category need and benefit a private label retailer.
This then implies that a monopoly or concentrated market would spend more on advertising to internalize such positive externalities.
Chandra and Weinberg (2018) find evidence that an increase in local market concentration leads to higher advertising expenditure by exploiting a merger event in the U.S. brewing industry that results in a higher market concentration.
<!-- para 15 -->
Overall, it is more common and general to find a negative externality of advertising on rivals, whereas the existence of positive spillovers depends on the specific definition of product substitutes (Sahni 2015, Shapiro 2018).
In our context, we study firm-level advertising expenditure in response to the ownership change, which is broader than specific products.
Therefore, we take a combative view of advertising and hypothesize that as common ownership of rival firms tends to reduce competition between rivals, a firm's incentive to take market share from rivals through advertising would decrease.
<!-- para 16 -->
**Hypothesis 1.
The advertising spending of firms with common ownership would be less than that of similar firms without common ownership.**
<!-- para 17 -->
Theoretically, it is also worth noting that the impact of common ownership on advertising could be moderated by **the competitiveness among the common-owned firms in the industry**.
If firms A and B are in the same industry and share a common shareholder but are both small in terms of market share, the major competitor for A and B could probably be another firm with a larger market share.
In this case, A and B might not have incentives to reduce their advertising expenditure or may even coordinate to increase advertising spending in competition with the major players of the industry.
We empirically explore such a moderating effect of between-firm competitiveness on advertising reduction in the later section.
<!-- para 19 -->
There are several coordination mechanisms by which common owners of firms in the same industry could affect the firms' policy.
First, common owners could engage with the explicit aim of influencing firms' policy by voice, incentives, and vote.
Azar et al. (2018) and Schmalz (2018) offer a comprehensive discussion.
Regarding the voice channel, fund managers usually influence corporate governance through their private engagement meetings with firm managers. [^6] In addition, fund managers could demand board seats to ensure implementation of the desired product-market strategy. [^7] Regarding the incentive channel, funds can threaten management by selling the stock if management does not follow its desired product-market strategy.
Alternatively, the funds could change the compensation plan in a way that reduces managers' incentive to compete.
Regarding the vote channel, funds could vote in support of or against firms' management to align firms' interests with the fund's interests.
<!-- para 20 -->
Second, common owners can serve as an enforcement mechanism for collaboration agreements among firms in the same industry.
Because of incomplete contracting (i.e., the inability of firms to write a contract that can address every possible contingency that may arise and every action that may be feasible in the future), firms considering collaboration with rivals in the same industry may be concerned about the risk of being expropriated or held up by their counterparties (Klein et al. 1978) and therefore, avoid entering into potentially beneficial collaborative agreements.
The presence of crossholders whose objectives are to maximize the value of their combined portfolio holdings can help align the incentives of the firms considering collaboration and mitigate frictions associated with incomplete contracting.
<!-- para 21 -->
Third, common owners can facilitate information transmission among firms in the same industry and foster profitable collaboration.
Firms in the same industry have a natural tendency to conceal proprietary information from their rivals because of competitive considerations.
For example, Asker and Ljungqvist (2010) show that firms in the same industry avoid sharing investment banks out of concerns about leaking confidential information to rivals.
This fear of losing business secrets may discourage firms from developing business ties with same-industry firms that are mutually beneficial.
Common owners can facilitate information sharing among competing firms to explore possible cooperative opportunities and improve market performance.
The informational role has been discussed in other related contexts.
Past literature shows that the existence of common agencies serving several competitors, such as advertising agencies and trade associations, can facilitate collusive behavior through information transmission (Bernheim and Whinston 1985).
<!-- para 22 -->
We argue that the cost and benefit of coordination would affect firms' incentive to collaborate on strategic decisions such as advertising.
When the gain from coordination is larger, firms have more incentives to coordinate, and the decrease in advertising spending is likely to be larger.
Alternatively, if the cost of coordination is high, firms are less likely to shift their strategy, and the impact of common ownership is likely to be small.
Based on these arguments, we propose the following two hypotheses.
<!-- para 23 -->
**Hypothesis 2.
The reduction of advertising spending by firms with common ownership is greater when these firms have a higher coordination benefit.**
<!-- para 24 -->
**Hypothesis 3.
The reduction of advertising spending by firms with common ownership is smaller when these firms have a higher coordination cost.**
<!-- para 25 -->
In summary, based on the theoretical predictions of the effect of common ownership on competition and the relationship between competition and advertising, we hypothesize that the presence of a common ownership structure would decrease firms' advertising expenditures and that this effect is moderated by the benefit and cost of coordination.

## methods
<!-- para 2 -->
The challenge of identifying the impact of common ownership on firms' advertising spending is the potential endogeneity concern.
Institutional blockholders do not invest randomly.
Institutions may choose to invest in firms with certain product market characteristics.
In addition, unobservable firm characteristics, such as managerial styles and organizational traits, may correlate with both common ownership decisions and advertising strategy, leading to an endogeneity concern.
To address this issue, we exploit financial institution mergers that generate plausibly exogenous variation in firms' common ownership.
<!-- para 4 -->
The sample examined in this paper includes United States-listed firms with common stocks traded on the New York Stock Exchange (NYSE), National Association of Securities Dealers Automated Quotations (NASDAQ), and American Stock Exchange (AMEX) during the period from 1980 through 2014.
To be included in our sample, a firm must have positive values for sales and total assets and nonmissing industry classification information, and it must belong to an industry with at least two firms.
We retrieved quarterly institutional holdings data from Thomson's CDA/Spectrum database (Form 13F). [^8] Firm-level advertising expenditure data and other financial statement items come from Compustat.
Stock returns come from the Center for Research in Security Prices.
<!-- para 5 -->
We obtain a list of financial institution mergers that occurred during our sample period (1983–2011) from He and Huang (2017), consisting of 48 mergers. [^9] For each quarter, we extract institutional holdings information from the Thomson Reuters Institutional (13F) Holdings database and define a holding as a block if it exceeds 5% of a company's outstanding shares.
We focus on blockholders because they are influential shareholders.
Shareholders must file a Form 13D with the Securities and Exchange Commission (SEC) when their ownership block reaches 5% of a company's outstanding shares.
These owners often can influence the company with the voting rights awarded with their holdings. [^10]
<!-- para 6 -->
Common holdings arise when an institution simultaneously holds blocks in multiple firms from the same industry.
An industry is identified by the four-digit standard industry classification (SIC) code disclosed in financial reports.
If firms have the same four-digit SIC code, we define them as being in the same industry. [^11] Specifically, we define treatment firms as follows.
(1) The firm must be block held by one of the merging institutions during the quarter immediately before the merger announcement date, and (2) the other merging institution must not block hold the firm but must block hold at least one of its industry rivals during the same quarter before the merger.
Importantly, the procedure to identify treated firms uses only ex ante information available at the time of the merger.
This ex ante approach mitigates the concern that the assignment of treatment firms is affected by subsequent trading decisions of the merged entity, which might contain information about the prospects of portfolio firms or their advertising policies.
<!-- para 7 -->
To control for differences in the investment and stock picking styles of the merging institutions, we construct the control group as the firms that are simultaneously owned by at least one of the merging institutions but do not experience a change in common ownership.
Specifically, to be included in the control sample, a firm must satisfy two conditions in the quarter before the merger announcement.
First, the firm must be block held by the same institution that block holds a treated firm.
Second, the other merging institution must not block hold any peer firms from the same industry.
Thus, the merger does not affect the common ownership of control firms, and yet, these firms are held by the merged institutions.
<!-- para 8 -->
We use the case in which BlackRock acquired Barclays in 2009 as an example to illustrate the data structure, as shown in Figure 2.
Before the merger, BlackRock was a blockholder of Clayton Williams Energy Inc. (SIC = 1310, Crude Petroleum & Natural Gas industry) and Anglo American Plc.
(SIC = 1099, Miscellaneous Metal Ores industry).
Barclays was a blockholder of Toreador Resources Corp.
(SIC = 1310), PDC Energy Inc. (SIC = 1310), Lennar Corp.
(SIC = 1531, Operative Builders industry), Meritage Homes Corp.
(SIC = 1531, Operative Builders industry), and Granite Construction Inc. (SIC = 1622, Heavy Construction, Except Highway).
Although Barclays block held more firms in other industries, we only use these firms as a demonstration.
When BlackRock and Barclays Bank merged, Clayton Williams Energy Inc. (SIC = 1310), Toreador Resources Corp.
(SIC = 1310), and PDC Energy Inc. (SIC = 1310) were suddenly commonly owned by BlackRock.
We define these three firms as treated firms.
Anglo American Plc. is defined as a control firm because after the merger, Barclays Bank did not hold any firms sharing the same SIC code with Anglo American Plc.
Following the same logic, Lennar Corp., Meritage Homes Corp., and Granite Construction Inc. are control firms.
Thus, the merger created additional common ownership links between Clayton Williams Energy and the same-industry firms Barclays owned.
<!-- para 9 -->
The sample selection process begins with 48 mergers, involving 4,703 firm-merger observations, where 416 firms that experienced common-ownership changes are identified as the treatment group and the rest of the 4,287 firms are the controls.
We define the event year as the fiscal year of the merger announcement date.
We drop firms that do not have an annual report released after the event year.
We also drop observations with a missing value for advertising expenditure.
As a result, we have 47 mergers, containing 1,708 firms with 133 treatment firms and 1,575 control firms in the sample.
<!-- para 10 -->
The merger-induced common-ownership change is exogenous to advertising strategy because the merger of two institutions is unlikely to be driven by their specific stock holdings.
Most mergers are between banks and are often driven by financial deregulation.
Mergers between nonbank financial institutions are often driven by efforts to achieve economies of scale in fund operations or build market share by expanding financial product offerings (Jayaraman et al. 2002, He and Huang 2017).
We further verify that the mergers in our data set are unlikely to be driven by holdings of the treated firms by showing that the relative importance of treated and control firms in the fund portfolios is minimal.
Specifically, we calculate the weight as the ratio of the market value of certain stocks to the merger institutions' overall portfolio market value (the sum of the market value of stocks in their portfolio).
The median weight for the treated firms in the fund portfolio is 0.02%, and that for control firms is 0.01%.
Therefore, if a bank or fund mainly wants to commonly own another stock, it could achieve this easily by buying the stock in the market instead of merging with another institution.
<!-- para 12 -->
Our analysis of the effect of common ownership on corporate advertising expenditure utilizes a natural experiment involving mutual fund mergers.
The outcome of this process is the creation of common ownership between firms in the same industry that are separately held by the acquirer and target before the merger.
Our empirical methodology requires that we specify a representative window around the merger events.
In choosing the proper estimation window, we face a trade-off.
As is the case with most event studies.

We use the DiD methodology to identify the treatment effect of common ownership on advertising expenditure.
In this approach, the sample of firms is divided into treatment and control groups.
If we denote the average advertising expenditure in the treatment (T) and control (C) groups in the pre- and post-event periods by $Adv_{T,Pre}$, $Adv_{T,Post}$, $Adv_{C,Pre}$, and $Adv_{C,Post}$, respectively, the partial effect of the change because of the merger can be estimated as
<!-- para 17 -->
$$ DiD=(Adv_{T,Post}-Adv_{T,Pre})-(Adv_{C,Post}-Adv_{C,Pre}). \tag{1} $$
<!-- para 18 -->
By comparing the changes in mean expenditures over time for the treatment and control groups, we allow for both group-specific and time-specific effects.
This estimator is unbiased under the condition that (1) the merger is not systematically related to other factors that affect the outcome variable.
In other words, the merger should be exogenous to the firms' advertising decisions in the context of our paper; (2) the treatment group and the control group have a parallel trend in advertising expenditures over time, which we verify.
<!-- para 19 -->
An important advantage of our identification approach is that we examine multiple financial institution mergers that occurred at different times.
In other words, the treated firms in each merger are different, and the corresponding control firms are also different.
The presence of multiple shocks mitigates the concern that confounding events around the mergers explain our results.
It is unlikely that there exist persistent unobserved factors coinciding with multiple merger events that lead to changes in treated firms' advertising expenditures relative to those of the controls.
The benefit of having multiple shocks is widely acknowledged in the literature (e.g., Hong and Kacperczyk 2010, He and Huang 2017, Brav et al. 2018).
<!-- para 20 -->
We estimate the following regression model:
<!-- para 21 -->
$$ \begin{aligned}Adv_{i,j,t}&=\alpha_{i}+\beta_{1}Treat_{i,j}\times Post_{i,j,t}+\beta_{2}Treat_{i,j}\\&\quad+\beta_{3}Post_{i,j,t}+\gamma FirmCharacteristics_{i,j,t-1}\\&\quad+\delta FixedEffects_{i,j}+\epsilon_{i,j,t},\end{aligned} \tag{2} $$
<!-- para 22 -->
where i indexes firm, j indexes the institution merger, t indexes time (year), and $Adv_{i,j,t}$ is the advertising expenditure for firm i in merger j and year t.
<!-- para 23 -->
The dependent variable is the firm’s advertising expenditure.
Following the literature (Morck et al. 1988, McAlister et al. 2007, Lou 2014), we use the natural logarithm of firm i’s advertising dollar amount at period t, $\ln Adv$, as the dependent variable.
We also use two alternative measures in robustness checks.
First, we use the ratio of advertising expenditures to the average total assets of firm i during period t, $AdvAssets$, to account for the total disposable resources of the firm.
Second, we use the ratio of advertising expenditures to total sales during period t, $AdvSales$, as an advertising budget is often set in proportion to the target sales in practice.
<!-- para 24 -->
The coefficient of primary interest is $\beta_{1}$, which captures the treatment effect identified from the change in advertising expenditure after the merger for firms that experienced a change in common ownership relative to their controls.
This coefficient is econometrically equivalent to the DiD in Equation (1) with more control variables.
The dummy variable, $Treat_{ij}$, is an indicator of whether firm i experienced an increase in common ownership (treated firm) in merger j, and the coefficient $\beta_{2}$ captures any baseline difference in advertising expenditure between the treatment and the control group. $Post_{ijt}$ is a dummy that equals one if a firm-year observation is from the period (one, two, or three years) after the merger announcement date and zero otherwise.
The coefficient $\beta_{3}$ captures any time trend in advertising expenditure in the years before and after the merger event common to all the firms.
<!-- para 25 -->
Following Lou (2014), we control for other factors in the last period that may affect firms' advertising expenditures, summarized by FirmCharacteristics $_{i,j,t-1}$.
Specifically, we include the logarithm of total assets (in millions of dollars), the logarithm of sales (in millions of dollars), and the logarithm of capital market value (in millions of dollars) at the end of the fiscal year, as the size and value of a firm may affect its advertising expenditure.
We also control for BM (book-to-market ratio), lnAge (the logarithm of firm age), KZIndex (an index representing firms' financing constraints), Return (the average monthly return), Turnover (the average monthly turnover, which equals the sum of the trading volumes during that month divided by shares outstanding), Sigma (the volatility of daily return), and ROE (the ratio of net income to stockholders' equity).
The complete list of control variables and their definitions is provided in Table 1.
We further control for the firm-merger fixed effects, FixedEffects $_{i,j}$, as the firm could be affected by multiple mergers.

We start with intuitive evidence on the change in common ownership on firms' advertising expenditures.
In Figure 3, we plot the average time trend of advertising expenditure for firms in the treatment and control groups.
The time interval covers three years before and three years after a financial institution merger event.
Year 0 denotes the event year, and the x axis denotes the year relative to the event year.
Figure 3 shows the advertising expenditure trend of lnAdv (Figure 3(a)), AdvAssets (Figure 3(b)), and AdvSales (Figure 3(c)) using our sample.
As the figure shows, the control firms' advertising expenditure is relatively stable throughout the time window.
In contrast, the treated firms, while following a parallel trend with the control group before the event year, show a significant decline in advertising expenditure after the event year.
This pattern is consistent with our conjecture that same-industry firms that share common owners tend to reduce their advertising expenditures. [^12]
<!-- para 10 -->
Next, we use the Difference-in-Differences approach and estimate Model (2) to formally quantify the effect.
The benchmark period consists of the three years before the merger, and the postevent observations include three years following the merger event. [^13] The results are reported in Table 3.
Column (1) shows the baseline results when using lnAdv as the dependent variable.
The coefficient of interest, Treat × Post, is negative and statistically significant.
Consistent with the pattern in Figure 3, after being commonly owned in the merger, treatment firms experience a significant reduction in advertising spending.
In column (2), we add firm-merger fixed effects to account for unobservable firm characteristics and cluster standard errors at the merger level.
Because firm-merger fixed effects would absorb the Treat dummy, we do not have the Treat in column (2).
In column (3), we further include control variables that have been shown to affect a firm's level of advertising in the literature.
The results are qualitatively the same with these additional controls and fixed effects.
This implies that the treated firms (i.e., the firms with common ownership after the merger) significantly decrease their advertising spending compared with firms in the control group.
<!-- para 11 -->
We also examine whether our results are robust to two alternative measures of advertising spending.
First, we scale the advertising spending by total assets to account for the total disposable resources of the firm.
Second, we scale the advertising spending by sales, as an advertising budget is often set in proportion to the target sales in practice.
The results are qualitatively similar in that firms with common owners experience a significant drop in advertising expenditure after the event.
<!-- para 12 -->
The impact of common ownership on advertising is also economically significant.
The results in column (3) for each measure of advertising expenditure indicate that, compared with control firms during the same period, treatment firms on average reduce total advertising expenditure by 17.04% in terms of dollar volume, 1% in terms of the advertising-to-total assets ratio, and 0.5% in terms of the advertising-to-sales ratio.
<!-- para 13 -->
In short, Table 3 provides evidence that common ownership leads firms to significantly reduce their advertising expenditures, which is consistent with Hypothesis 1.

**4.3.1.
Propensity Score Matching.** To further alleviate the potential concern that the treatment and control firms may differ in their characteristics, we exploit propensity score matching to form a control group that has similar firm characteristics to those of the treated firms.
<!-- para 24 -->
First, we keep firms with observation in one year before the merger, resulting in a sample with 83 treatment and 1,217 control firms.
Second, we run a Logit model to predict whether a firm is treated and obtain the propensity scores for each firm.
In the regression, we use the full set of control variables as that in the DiD regression as independent variables.
Third, for every treatment firm, we select five control firms whose propensity scores are the closest to the treatment.
Fourth, we impose the common support condition in the matching approach, which requires the matched firms to have similar firm characteristics (Caliendo and Kopeinig 2008).
The matching is conducted with replacement to find the best-matched firms.
Last, in the majority of the cases, the observations are dropped because of the missing advertising expenditure, which is well documented in the literature.
Therefore, we do not require a strong balanced sample (i.e., each firm is required to have six-year observations).
This leaves us 83 treatment firms, 299 control firms, and a total of 2,425 observations.
<!-- para 25 -->
We conduct a multivariate Difference-in-Differences analysis similar to that in the previous section.
Table 4 presents the results for the propensity score-matched sample.
Consistent with our previous findings, the treatment firms significantly decrease advertising expenditure, compared with control firms, after common ownership.
Moreover, the results are qualitatively the same for a one-to-one matching sample or simple sorting based on firm characteristics.

**4.3.2.
Preshock Ownership by Other Institutions.** Another potential concern related to our Difference-in-Differences results is that the treatment could be confounded with preshock firm characteristics.
The treated firms may happen to have higher common ownership by nonmerging institutions than control firms before the mergers.
We do not find this argument plausible for two reasons.
First, the DiD regression fully considers the differences between the treatment and control groups and the within-firm changes.
It is unlikely that the effect of preshock common ownership would coincide with all of the staggered merger events.
Second, we formally examine the cross-sectional variation in preshock common ownership.
We partition the treated firms based on the sample median of the overlapping institutional ownership one year before the mergers and run the DiD analysis.
If our results are driven by preshock common ownership, we should see a significant effect in the high group and an insignificant effect in the low group.
The results are shown in Table 5.
We find that both high and low groups exhibit a significant reduction in advertising expenditures.
The estimated effect is even larger in the low group in some specifications.
Overall, these results suggest that the reduction in advertising expenditures is driven by the common ownership induced by the mergers instead of the premerger ownership structure.

**4.3.4.
Alternative Construction of Control Firms**.
We use firms block held by one of the merging institutions but that did not experience common-ownership change as the control sample.
One potential concern is that firms in the treatment and control groups are from different industries and that their advertising spending patterns may not be comparable.
Note that in the DiD regression, we have controlled the firm-merger fixed effects, so essentially, we are conducting within-firm comparison instead of cross-sectional comparison.
This would alleviate the concerns that we are comparing firms across the industries.

In this section, we investigate the cross-sectional variations of coordination benefit and cost.
The results show that firms tend to cut advertising expenditures when they have common owners.
This change is likely to occur through the coordination of the firms that become commonly owned as a result of the mergers.
When the gain from coordination is larger, firms have a greater incentive to coordinate, and the decrease in advertising spending is likely to be larger.
Alternatively, if the cost of coordination is high, firms are less likely to shift their advertising strategy following the change in common ownership.
In the following, we examine how the benefit and cost of coordination affect the impact of common ownership on advertising expenditures.
<!-- para 50 -->
Although the benefit of coordination is not directly measurable, we conjecture that firms with more similar products and greater common ownership as well as those in industries with higher advertising intensities are more likely to gain from coordination.
Therefore, they are more likely to cut advertising when they have common owners.
<!-- para 51 -->
First, we expect that firms with more similar products and therefore, facing more direct competition would cut advertising more because of the larger potential gains from coordination.
We measure the similarity of product offerings between two firms based on the text-based network industry classifications (TNIC3), defined by Hoberg and Phillips (2016).
The product similarity score, which is a real number in the interval [0,1], describes the similarity of the focal firm's product offering and that of its industry peers.
The higher the score, the more similar the firm and its peers are in the product market, which implies more direct competition.
The similarity score is based on product descriptions from the corporate annual report (10-K form) on the Electronic Data Gathering, Analysis, and Retrieval system (EDGAR) website from 1996 to the present.
These product descriptions are legally required to be accurate, as Item 101 of Regulation S-K legally requires that firms describe the significant products they offer; these descriptions must also be updated and representative of the current fiscal year of the 10-K.
The firm by firm pairwise similarity scores are calculated by forming word vectors for each firm to compute continuous measures of product similarity. [^16] We use the firms' total similarity scores one year before the mergers in the following analysis.
<!-- para 52 -->
Using the median of treatment firms' similarity scores, we partition the treatment firms into high and low groups and run the Difference-in-Differences analysis for each group. [^17] We predict that the treatment firms belonging to the high group will cut their advertising more than those in the low group.
That is, the coefficient of the interaction term of Treat and Post in the high group should be more sensitive than that in the low group.
<!-- para 53 -->
The results are reported in Table 9.
Using lnAdv as the dependent variable, column (1) indicates that treatment firms with high similarity scores on average reduce their advertising expenditures by 35.08%, relative to control firms, after the mergers.
The effect is both statistically and economically significant.
The results in column (2) suggest that treatment firms with low similarity scores reduce advertising expenditures by 3.70% relative to control firms in the postmerger period, and the effect is statistically insignificant.
Additionally, the difference between high and low groups is statistically significant.
We find a similar pattern when using alternative measures of advertising intensity (i.e., AdvAssets and AdvSales).
The effect is significantly larger for the treatment firms in the high group than the low group.
These results support our conjecture that common ownership has a stronger effect on firms facing more direct competition in the product market.

Second, the coordination benefit also relates to the magnitude of common ownership.
The larger common ownership and therefore, the greater the common interest, the stronger the incentive is to coordinate.
We predict that treatment firms held by common owners with a greater percentage of shares will tend to cut advertising expenditures more relative to their lower ownership counterparts.
In our main tests, we follow the regulation by the Securities and Exchange Commission and literature (e.g., Edmans and Holderness 2017) to use $5\%$ of shares as the threshold to define the blockholding and treatment firms.
According to the regulation, shareholders must file a Form 13D with the Securities and Exchange Commission when their ownership block reaches $5\%$ of a company's outstanding shares.
These owners have been shown to significantly influence companies through the voting rights that accompany their holdings.
However, common ownership may still occur when institutional investors hold less than $5\%$ of shares, although the corresponding influence would be much smaller.
Following Azar (2012), we construct a continuous treatment variable—CommonOwnership $(\%)$—as an alternative measure of common ownership.
Specifically, CommonOwnership $(\%)$ equals zero for control firms and equals the lower value of merging institutions' holdings in treated pairs in the event year, which captures the common interest in both firms.
For example, if BlackRock held $4\%$ of firm A and Barclays held $3\%$ of firm B in the same industry before the merger, firm A and firm B are commonly owned and defined as treated firms after the merger between BlackRock and Barclays.
In this case, the continuous treatment variable—CommonOwnership $(\%)$—is $3\%$.

If the cost of coordination is high, firms are less likely to shift their strategy, and the impact of common ownership is likely to be small.
To test Hypothesis 3, we conduct two cross-sectional variation tests based on the cost of coordination.
<!-- para 70 -->
The first proxy for the cost of coordination is the location of the firm headquarters.
Geographic distance continues to play a key role in local economies, despite sharp declines in transportation costs and vast increases in information technology (Krugman 1991, Angrist and Lavy 1997, Agarwal and Hauswald 2010).
The literature documents that the distance between firms has a strong influence on firm policy, lending decisions, and investment decisions, among other things.
Firms located in the same region have lower communication and coordination costs.
Therefore, we expect that firms with headquarters located in the same state should have lower coordination costs than firm pairs with headquarters in different states. [^18] These former firms are likely to cut advertising spending more because of easier coordination.

Another measure of the cost of coordination is the magnitude of total institutional ownership.
Theoretical work suggests that institutional investors, as large shareholders, can discipline corporate managers through active monitoring and intervention (Shleifer and Vishny 1986, Kahn and Winton 1998).
In practice, however, institutional investors face a collective action problem in playing a monitoring role because equity ownership is widely dispersed.
The diffuse ownership structure suggests that the classical “free-riding” problem can prevail (Grossman and Hart 1980).
As a result, it has been recognized that coordinated action by institutional investors is important in monitoring management (e.g., Gillan and Starks 2000).
Therefore, when institutional ownership is higher, the coordination cost would be lower because the coordination between professional investors with a significant stake in the company could be easier.
If institutional investors can coordinate with ease, they can form a coalition to share the costs and benefits of mutually beneficial monitoring activities.
For instance, institutions can coordinate their proxy votes to influence the changes in corporate governance and exert joint pressure on corporate strategy.

In this section, we further explore how the competitiveness between the common-owned firms relative to the industry might moderate or even reserve the impact of common ownership on advertising expenditure.
If both firms A and B are small players in the same industry and share a common shareholder, the major competition they face could be from another firm (firm C), which has a larger market share.
In this case, firms A and B may have fewer incentives to reduce their advertising expenditure or may even increase in advertising to compete with firm C.
<!-- para 88 -->
We empirically test this possibility using different measures of competitiveness between the firms.
We hypothesize that if the sum of the market share of A and B is small relative to that of the largest firms in the same industry, the treatment firms are less likely to cut the advertising because the major competition is not between A and B.
Specifically, we calculate a market share ratio, which is the sum of the market share of the largest three firms (excluding A and B) in the industry divided by the sum of the market share of A and B.
We divide the sample into two groups based on the median of this market ratio. [^21] The HighRatio group suggests that firms A and B are relatively small in the industry and may not be the major competitor to each other and vice versa.
Table 15 reports the results.
Consistent with our expectation, we find that firms in the LowRatio group tend to cut more advertising expenditure after the common ownership, whereas firms in the HighRatio group neither increase nor decrease their advertising expenditure.

## discussion
<!-- para 2 -->
This paper studies the impact of ownership structure on corporate advertising.
Using mergers between financial institutions as an exogenous shock to ownership structure, we provide causal evidence that common ownership leads to decreases in the advertising expenditures of firms from the same industry.
The finding also provides empirical support for the theoretical prediction that common ownership may soften competition.
<!-- para 3 -->
We argue that the reduction in advertising spending by firms with common owners is likely to result from coordination.
Consistent with our conjecture, we find that the magnitude of the advertising reduction varies with the potential benefit and cost of coordination.
Specifically, on the benefit side, firms with greater product similarity with industry peers and a higher common ownership as well as those belonging to industries with higher advertising intensities tend to cut advertising more when they have common owners with peers.
On the coordination cost side, firms with headquarters close to treatment peers and with more institutional ownership cut advertising spending more.
We also find that the competitiveness between the common-owned firms relative to the industry moderates the effect of common ownership on advertising spending.
<!-- para 4 -->
Our research contributes to several research areas.
First, research on the interaction of marketing and financial markets has mainly focused on the impact of marketing activities, such as advertising and branding, on stock returns and market capitalization.
Our research focuses on the reverse link.
We find that financial market operations and ownership structure can have a significant impact on a firm's advertising strategy.
Second, our findings can also be viewed as challenging the assumption in the literature that firms' objective (advertising in the context of this paper) is to maximize firms' value.
Under a common ownership structure, firms may act to internalize portfolio interests instead of maximizing their value.
Our research also has important implications for studies on the interaction of ownership/organizational structure and product market behavior and suggests that future research on product market competition should carefully examine firms' interconnectedness through common equity ownership.
<!-- para 5 -->
Our empirical findings also have significant implications for market participants.
First, our findings are relevant to the managers of the firms.
When shareholders vote on the policies of one company, they take into account the effects of those policies not just on that particular company's profits but also, on the profits of the other companies that they hold stakes in.
As a result, the managers of the companies with common ownership should be aware of their blockholders' interests and take that into consideration of firms' policies.
Second, our findings are relevant to the securities analysts.
Because of the potential anticompetition effect of the common ownership, the valuation and profitability of these firms would tend to be larger than noncommon-owned firms.
Therefore, the analysts should consider the common-ownership status when they value the firms.
Third, this research might be valuable to advertising agencies as their revenue depends on companies' advertising spending.
Our finding suggests that advertising agencies should pay close attention to clients' ownership structure change as it can lead to reduction of advertising budget.

Finally, our findings are relevant to policy makers.
The antitrust policy usually focuses on mergers and acquisitions, which are all-or-nothing forms of integration.
Given the prevailing trend of common ownership structure over the past two decades, antitrust authorities worldwide have begun to examine the competitive implications of common ownership links.
Examples include European Commission Article 8(2) Regulation (EC) 139/2004, Case M.7932 - Dow/DuPont or the Organization for Economic Cooperation and Development's conference on common ownership and competition.
The key question is whether and to what extent common ownership could affect firm policy, competition, and social welfare. [^22] Our empirical finding shows that firms' advertising expenditure decreases with common ownership, providing another piece of evidence that is consistent with the argument of common ownership fostering coordination among competing firms.
It suggests that one should pay attention to the portfolios of the main shareholders of each firm in the industry when assessing the potential for market power in an industry.
Further research is required to evaluate the overall consequences of common ownership for firm competition and social welfare.
