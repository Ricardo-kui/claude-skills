# mallapragada_2025_to_acquire_or_to_ally_the_impact_of_strate 句子库存

## introduction
<!-- para 2 -->
In pursuit of growth, firms often rely on two prototypical governance modes[^1]: acquisitions and strategic alliances (e.g., Robertson and Gatignon 1998; Yin and Shanley 2008).
For example, pharmaceutical firm AbbVie expanded into the cosmetics industry by acquiring Allergan, the maker of Botox.
In contrast, Gilead and Merck, rivals in the same industry, opted to form an alliance to develop innovative HIV treatment.
In a recent survey of 1,378 CEOs conducted by PwC (2024), 40% of the participants relied on alliances, while 37% used acquisitions to pursue growth, indicating that firms view these modes as alternatives and that no governance mode fits all situations.
<!-- para 3 -->
Given various contingencies, firms choose the governance mode that best mitigates ex post risks (Williamson 1996).
For example, compared with alliances, acquisitions present more challenges in assimilation but offer greater control over dayto-day decisions (Dyer, Kale, and Singh 2004).
Firms must evaluate the trade-offs while choosing the optimal governance mode (Ghosh and John 2022; Heide 1994), including numerous transaction-level factors that influence governance mode choice (e.g., Geyskens, Steenkamp, and Kumar 2006).
However, a prominent, albeit ignored, factor is the firm’s strategic emphasis, defined as the relative focus on value appropriation versus value creation (Mizik and Jacobson 2003).
Value appropriation involves harvesting revenue and profit opportunities by investing in marketing (Krasnikov and Jayachandran 2008), whereas value creation involves pursuing innovation opportunities by investing in R&D (Rosenkopf and Nerkar 2001).
<!-- para 4 -->
Two aspects of strategic emphasis are notable.
First, as strategic emphasis captures the firm’s relative reliance on value appropriation versus creation, it can be envisioned as a valuemanagement approach through which the firm executes its business pursuits.
Second, strategic emphasis is rooted in the firm’s resource (or asset) base—the foundations of a firm’s growth strategy (Barney 1991)—such that an emphasis on value appropriation relative to value creation indicates higher investments in marketing relative to R&D assets.
<!-- para 5 -->
While strategic emphasis has been tied to firm-level outcomes such as stock performance, competitiveness, and growth (e.g., Han, Mittal, and Zhang 2017; Swaminathan, Murshed, and Hulland 2008), it remains startlingly absent from the interfirm governance literature.
In practice, firms execute value appropriation and creation by crafting suitable governance structures (e.g., alliances) with external partners.
For instance, firms in the marketing sector have continued to grow through acquisitions (Coffee 2022), while strategic alliances have long been considered “the bread and butter of the tech industry” (Loughry 2022).
Thus, while we have emergent evidence for the effect of strategic emphasis on firm performance, its impact on the governance modes through which strategic emphasis translates to performance remains uncharted.
This lacuna persists even though prior interfirm research has alluded pointedly to a role for firm-specific assets or resources, in which strategic emphases are anchored, in shaping interorganizational choices (e.g., Ghosh and John 1999; Madhok 2002).
To address these gaps, we ask the following research questions: (1) What is the impact of strategic emphasis on governance mode choice?
(2) What boundary conditions influence the relationship between strategic emphasis and governance choice?
<!-- para 6 -->
Our theorization draws on transaction cost economics (TCE; Williamson 1985), which speaks to how assets can be safeguarded in exchange, and on the resource-based view of the firm (RBV; Barney 1991), which investigates the properties of assets that imbue them with value.
Starting with TCE, we argue that increasing emphasis on value appropriation relative to value creation, each of which is underpinned by a different asset type (marketing vs. R&D), poses differential governance hazards for the firm in its external relationships.
We tie these hazards to distinctive attributes of the underlying assets, specifically their valuation (i.e., the difficulty in accurately evaluating the asset’s worth), modularity (i.e., the degree to which an individual asset can be separated from the larger base of the firm’s assets), and adaptation (i.e., the frequency with which the need for adjustments arises with the asset) properties.
We posit that these exchange hazards will influence the firm’s governance mode choice in its external relationships, intending to safeguard the underlying assets.
<!-- para 7 -->
Notably, creating or extracting value in the marketplace is contingent partly on the firm’s ability to acquire and combine scarce resources from alliance partners (Barney 1991).
However, since TCE is silent about the combinative properties of assets (Ghosh and John 1999), we also develop our contentions from an RBV perspective.
We propose that strategic emphasis will also impact governance choice because there are limits to how effectively assets, particularly those characterized by tacit qualities, can be combined across firm boundaries.
<!-- para 8 -->
Subsequently, we identify two prominent actors—one internal and one external to the firm—that moderate the relationship between strategic emphasis and governance mode choice.
First, effective interfirm governance is intimately tied to the presence of top management actors who implement and supervise the collaboration blueprint (Hoetker and Mellewigt 2009).
The marketing literature identifies the chief marketing officer (CMO) as a salient actor internal to the firm (Germann, Ebbes, and Grewal 2015).
CMO “playbooks” available from the industry also identify collaboration with external partners as a key responsibility of the CMO (Villaret 2021).
As such, CMO presence is our first moderator.
Second, the efficacy of firm choices is altered by actors in the environment external to the firm.
Market analysts are one such actor who serve as critical information intermediaries (Livnat and Zhang 2012) to validate firms’ value-management strategies and facilitate their access to partnerships (Chakravarty and Grewal 2011).
As such, analyst coverage is our second moderator.
<!-- para 9 -->
We test our hypotheses using data on acquisitions and alliances conducted by publicly listed U.S. firms from 2009–2016.
Results from a probit model that accounts for unobserved heterogeneity and endogeneity support our hypothesized effects.
We find that as the firm’s strategic emphasis on value appropriation relative to value creation increases, it is more likely to engage in acquisitions (vs. alliances).
However, both CMO presence and analyst coverage weaken this positive association.
<!-- para 10 -->
Our research makes several contributions.
First, we extend the research on strategic emphasis to incorporate elements of organizational strategy.
We do so by mapping the two valuemanagement strategies (i.e., appropriation vs. creation) to distinct governance modes.
Our research thus offers a first-order blueprint for firms to choose between crucial growth modes: acquisitions and alliances.
Our findings are relevant to C-suite executives and other dealmakers, over 75% of whom are confronted with “choosing the right mechanism for growth” (PwC 2024).
<!-- para 11 -->
Second, in examining the moderating effect of CMO presence, we illuminate an expanded repertoire of CMO functions beyond those currently cataloged.
We suggest that complementing strategic emphasis with specific organizational roles can allow firms to economize on costly acquisitions.
Leading firms such as Etsy and McDonald’s have recently equivocated on—or even eliminated—the CMO role (Ritson 2024).
Our findings indicate a need for caution, given that CMOs appear to serve purposes beyond traditional marketing functions.
We also study the moderation effect of analyst coverage, which contrasts starkly with the interfirm literature where the onus for governing resides exclusively with internal actors (Heide 1994).
We shed light on the role of these information intermediaries relative to firm governance choices.
<!-- para 12 -->
Finally, our research is pertinent to both the TCE and RBV literatures, which each represent foundational but incomplete perspectives on organizations.
Thus, TCE has consistently ignored the role of firm-specific resources in governance (Madhok 2002), while RBV is silent on relationship-level governance choices (Hinterhuber 2013).
By linking governance choices to asset positions, our work advances the current theoretical program of “bridging” these distinct literatures, which is expected to yield a better-specified theory of firm performance.
Our work is also the first to theoretically distinguish between marketing and R&D assets in terms of their distinctive attributes, which moves the literature beyond current conceptualizations that recognize these assets as different but fail to specify what these differences are.
In describing how asset attributes influence governance, we respond to calls for better articulation of the “interdependence of production and exchange relations” (Madhok 2002, p. 536).

## theory
<!-- para 2 -->
This section outlines our conceptual framework.
Per the TCE and RBV frameworks, and consistent with the “governance literature” in marketing (Bergen, Dutta, and Walker 1992; Ghosh and John 2022; Wuyts and Geyskens 2005), we adopt the perspective of the focal firm (“principal”) whose strategic emphasis and governance choices are the focus of inquiry.
<!-- para 4 -->
Research identifies marketing expenditures, specifically in advertising, which build the critical assets of brand equity and customer loyalty, as the basis of a value-appropriation emphasis (Krasnikov and Jayachandran 2008; Mizik and Jacobson 2003).
Srivastava, Shervani, and Fahey (1998, p. 5) detail how “market-based assets” such as brand equity “are the result of extensive advertising.” Superior brand–customer equity facilitates the firm’s ability to occupy favorable positioning in customers’ minds, shields these positions against the competition, and aids in capitalizing on existing offerings (i.e., harvesting incremental revenues and profits; Aaker 2008).
In contrast, value creation has been linked to “technology capabilities [and patents] driven by R&D expenditures” (Mizik and Jacobson 2003, p. 65).
The ensuing R&D assets facilitate the creation of quality and cost-effective offerings.
<!-- para 5 -->
Consistently, scholars have viewed marketing assets as supporting value appropriation (exploitation) and R&D assets as supporting value creation (exploration) activities (e.g., Mizik and Jacobson 2003).
Lavie, Kang, and Rosenkopf (2011, p. 1519) explicitly state, “Scholars have associated a firm’s tendency to acquire and generate new knowledge through exploration with R&D alliances, contrasting them with marketing alliances that serve for exploitation.” As value-appropriation and value-creation activities require deploying these assets, which accumulate only incrementally through experiential, path-dependent, and expenditure-intensive processes, firms typically specialize in these activities to different degrees.
A firm’s strategic emphasis reflects this relative focus between value appropriation and value creation.
<!-- para 7 -->
A firm’s strategic emphasis is an internal choice but is brought to bear in external partnerships for value-management purposes.
The interfirm literature, particularly its transaction cost branch (Williamson 1985), suggests that to the degree that the partners’ interests are not consistently aligned, the focal firm must safeguard its assets in exchange.
Several exchange hazards that impact the incidence of partner opportunism have been identified, such as valuation ambiguity (i.e., difficulty in accurately assessing parties’ respective contributions to the partnership) and market uncertainty (i.e., unanticipated disturbances that necessitate adaptive reactions and realignments to exchange) (Wathne and Heide 2000).
<!-- para 8 -->
Acquisitions and alliances are two archetypal governance structures that can mitigate exchange hazards and are viewed as alternative modes of governance available to firms (Wang and Zajac 2007).
Alliances represent the “market” mode, where the exchange partners coordinate transactions using arm’s-length or neoclassical contracts.
Absent exchange hazards, alliances allow firms to capitalize on the benefits of specialization available in the marketplace without incurring acquisition costs (Williamson 1991).
As such, alliances are the default mode for organizing transactions.
However, as hazards intensify and assets become misappropriation-prone, integrated governance modes such as acquisitions are preferred (Wang and Zajac 2007).
Internalizing the transaction into the “hierarchy” is cost-intensive but offers a structural safeguard against opportunism (Villalonga and McGahan 2005).
We provide an overview of these two governance modes in Table 1.
<!-- para 10 -->
Drawing on TCE (Williamson 1985) and its refinements (e.g., Ghosh and John 1999), we posit that a firm’s strategic emphasis, which reflects predominant reliance on certain underlying assets—which are potentially exposed to partner opportunism —should influence governance mode choice.
First, invoking the “efficiency criterion” in TCE, which holds that firms choose governance structures that best mitigate their exposure to exchange hazards, we articulate a “discriminating alignment” between the exchange properties of assets that underlie strategic emphasis and the unique safeguarding competencies of the chosen governance mode.
As noted at the outset, we trace these properties to the assets’ ambiguity, modularity, and adaptation attributes.
We then employ a resource-based lens to assess whether the TCE and RBV predictions concur.
In contrast to TCE’s safeguarding motives, RBV’s focus is the ease with which assets can be shared and recombined across partners (Madhok 2002).
Thus, our narrative considers both an “economizing” logic (i.e., governance structures are chosen to reduce opportunism) that pervades TCE research and a “strategizing” logic (i.e., governance structures should facilitate firms ability to enhance mutual gains), which is expressed in the RBV literature (Barney 1991) but largely overlooked in TCE research (Ghosh and John 2005).

Subsequently, we outline two moderators, each reflecting the firm’s broader endowment profile.
The first moderator is CMO presence in the focal firm, a key organizational role that reflects the firm’s managerial resources tied to the marketing domain (Barney 1991; Nath and Mahajan 2008).
In addition to being centrally relevant to the marketing function, CMOs have documented roles in shepherding the firm’s value-management activities (Germann, Ebbes, and Grewal 2015) and overseeing its partnerships (Swaminathan and Moorman 2009).
Our second moderator is analyst coverage, defined as the number of analysts covering the firm (Chang, Dasgupta, and Hilary 2006).
In contrast to the CMO, who is internal to the firm, analysts serve as powerful information intermediaries situated externally to the firm.
As expressed by contingency theories, the fit between firm strategy (strategic emphasis) and structure (governance mode) depends on the characteristics of the environment (Nickerson, Hamilton, and Wada 2001).
Market analysts are critical and persistent environmental actors who monitor firms’ asset allocation decisions and may influence their governance choices (Chakravarty and Grewal 2016).
<!-- para 15 -->
Thus, as both CMO presence and analyst coverage appear to be ecologically related to strategic emphasis (our antecedent variable) and governance mode choice (our outcome variable), a theoretical case exists for incorporating them as moderators in our framework.
Beyond their theoretical relevance, these moderators also have a recognized history in marketing research (e.g., Chakravarty and Grewal 2016; Germann, Ebbes, and Grewal 2015) and hold considerable significance for the industry, as seen in their frequent mentions in the trade press.

<!-- para 18 -->
We contend that increasing emphasis on value appropriation relative to creation, which involves greater reliance on marketing versus R&D assets, exposes the firm to increasing governance hazards in alliances for the following reasons.
First, compared with R&D assets such as patents, which are often manifested as physical product or process features (e.g., GE’s R&D in organic LED technology is tangibilized in its screens’ display attributes), marketing assets such as brand equity are more intangible because they exist as cognitive associations in the customer’s mind (Aaker 2008).
Thus, R&D primarily builds value equity, that is, objective quality and product parameters, while marketing builds brand equity, which involves subjective assessments of product “image” and “personal meaning” (Lemon, Rust, and Zeithaml 2001) (see Table 2).

<!-- para 21 -->
This greater subjectivity or intangibility of marketing (vs. R&D) assets will trigger greater ambiguity in gauging the asset’s contributions to the partnership (Ertekin, Sorescu, and Houston 2018).
Highlighting this ambiguity, Han and Manry (2004) suggest that many investors view advertising as merely an expenditure whose value expires in the short term rather than as long-term investments.
Such ambiguities are exacerbated by a lack of external benchmarks and of an active market for “hard-to-value assets” (Doz and Hamel 1998, p. 67), such as brand equity and customer relationships.
These ambiguities set the stage for conflict between the parties and might be purposely seized on by the partner to extract unfair rents (Wathne and Heide 2004).
In response, the focal firm will be motivated to engage in acquisitions.
Ghosh and John (1999) suggest that stronger brands rely less on alliances because the value of brand equity “become[s] subject to the bargaining process” (p. 140).
Industry experts, too, hold that “it’s a mistake not to understand brand value during M&A” (Bothe 2020).
<!-- para 22 -->
Second, a shift in emphasis toward value appropriation entails deploying more marketing (vs. R&D) assets, which are conspicuously tied to the firm’s identity.
For such firms, brand name and customer equity are the core of their business.
Unlike value equity elements (e.g., product specifications, warranty length), which can be parsed out individually, brand equity is experienced holistically as a “gestalt” (Lemon, Rust, and Zeithaml 2001).
As such, partner actions threatening the focal firm’s marketing assets endanger the asset owner’s entire business, which is held “hostage” to partner actions.
Thus, when franchisees engage in quality shading, the reputational costs are externalized to the franchisor.
Damage might even spill beyond the immediate brand, threatening the entire channel.
These risks will drive firms toward integration.
Robertson and Gatignon (1998) find that advertising intensity, which they argue builds brand value, predicts “ownership” of subsidiaries, and Lafontaine and Slade (2007) note that vertical integration arises when the “value of the brand is greater” (p. 632).
In contrast, R&D assets are more modular in comparison, so individual technologies in a firm’s portfolio can, to a degree, be mutually demarcated or “compartmentalized” (Levitas and McFadyen 2009).
Modularity limits the need for disclosures and the risk of know-how leakage, reducing the need to safeguard assets (Ghosh and John 2022).
An established patenting regime can further help isolate assets shared with the partner from the firm’s larger base of R&D assets.
As such, the opportunistic actions of the partner in one technological arena need not jeopardize other R&D assets.

Third, collaborating partners must often jointly “fill gaps, correct errors, and effect efficient realignments” (Williamson 1996, p. 102).
While value-appropriation and value-creation objectives entail realignments, such adaptations are relatively more frequent for value-appropriation tasks involving marketing assets, performed in the downstream market and exposed directly to daily market disturbances.
These disturbances call for what Heide and John (1990) term “joint action”—that is, “conscious, deliberate, and purposeful” (Williamson 1996, p. 103; see also Aaker 2008) coordinated responses from the parties—which strain the alliance repeatedly.
Williamson (1996, p. 116) avers that “the effects of more frequent disturbances are especially… pertinent” for such coordinated responses.
<!-- para 27 -->
Thus, as customer preferences shift, often erratically, the parties must make recurrent adaptations to product-brand features.
Expedient modifications might be warranted along the full value chain, but particularly to front-end customer-facing routines, where a failure to make speedy adjustments will dilute the customer’s “personal meaning” (i.e., equity toward the brand; Lemon, Rust, and Zeithaml 2001).
Yet, as each party’s gains from such adjustments are “indeterminate,” the partner might refuse to adapt without concessions.
As such, recurrent disturbances will propel the firm toward acquisitions, as documented across studies (e.g., Heide and John 1990; Wang and Zajac 2007).
For example, Wathne and Heide (2000) describe the “cola wars,” where noncompliance from independent bottlers forced Coke and Pepsi to move toward “captive systems.” John and Weitz (1988, p. 341) likewise find that “uncertainty … with respect to… marketing activities” motivates firms to “incorporate more elements of hierarchy.” Notably, value-creation activities (e.g., product R&D) also demand adaptations to deliver customer value equity, but likely not with the same frequency or immediacy as they unfold in the back end, away from daily market fluctuations.
Customer-facing tasks and brand interactions in the front end need not change, at least in the short term.
<!-- para 28 -->
In sum, increasing emphasis toward value appropriation versus value creation, which involves greater reliance on marketing versus R&D assets, entails greater valuation ambiguity, risks encompassing the firm’s larger asset base (lower modularity), and more frequent disturbances.
Thus, increasing value-appropriation emphasis is characterized by greater governance hazards, which we predict will motivate internalization for safeguarding purposes.
Consistent with our prediction, Dyer, Kale, and Singh (2004) detail how technology majors such as Cisco have grown through alliances, while the marketing powerhouse, Coke, has grown through acquisitions.
<!-- para 30 -->
The resource-based lens conceptualizes firms as heterogeneous repositories of imitation-resistant “sticky” assets and know-how that foster competitive advantage (Barney 1991).
Firms typically do not possess in-house the complete menu of resources required to compete in complex environments and must instead acquire them via external partnerships (Wang and Zajac 2007).
In this view, the assimilation and recombination of resources across firm boundaries are paramount to firm performance.
In turn, the properties of resources, particularly their embeddedness (tacitness) features, which aid or hamper such assimilation, become crucial (Kogut and Zander 1992).
Embedded or tacit assets resist codification; they remain bound to the firm and are challenging to transfer to external partners.
Given their firm-specific and implicit attributes, transference and assimilation of such assets are facilitated through strong ties and close (e.g., face-to-face) copractice, which is lacking in market relationships relative to the hierarchy.
As such, the vertically integrated enterprise is considered a more effective vehicle for harnessing such embedded capabilities (Argyres 1996; Lee 2018).
<!-- para 31 -->
We argue that an emphasis on value appropriation favors pre-cisely such an integrated structure.
Key marketing assets (e.g., brand equity, channel trust) are inherently relational and personalized (Aaker 2008; Moorman and Day 2016).
Many marketing capabilities are also deeply experiential and rooted in the focal setting, so they resist documentation.
Lacking a ready recipe or blueprint, such know-how is context- and firm-specific (Klein and Leffler 1981).
These implicit or “sticky” features impede the cross-firm transfer and integration of marketing know-how and might even invite underinvestment by alliance partners.
Therefore, we propose that a value-appropriating emphasis is exploited more effectively within the firm’s boundaries.
<!-- para 32 -->
R&D know-how can also be tacit, but some elements are more codifiable.
For one, scientific knowledge is supported by a rich and explicit language of symbols, signs, figures, and conventions, which aid documentation and elaboration.
Technological models, their underlying assumptions, and related data can all be shared and reproduced efficiently through such means (Steinmueller 2000).
A system of cross-referencing and patenting further supports such “externalization” of R&D know-how (Burk 2008).
Here, an “open architecture” of relationship ties will facilitate sourcing complementary inputs for value creation.
Lorange and Contractor (2002) also observe that a value-creation emphasis “favors alliances” whereby value chains “under different ownership cooperate with each-other” (p. 488).
<!-- para 33 -->
Thus, like TCE—but independent of TCE’s safeguarding logic—RBV also suggests a positive association between an appropriation emphasis and the likelihood of acquisitions.
<!-- para 34 -->
H<sub>1</sub>: Increasing strategic emphasis on value appropriation (relative to value creation) is associated positively with the likelihood of choosing acquisitions over alliances.
<!-- para 36 -->
Boyd, Chandy, and Cunha (2010) assert that CMOs possess unique informational expertise grounded in marketing, owing to their professional education and firm- and industry-specific experiences.
Homburg et al. (2014) detail how CMOs possess formal training in “advanced tools” (e.g., conjoint analysis, segmentation algorithms).
CMOs are also directly involved in resource allocation, which offers them proprietary insights into the firm’s value-appropriation activities (Srinivasan, Wuyts, and Mallapragada 2018).
For instance, CMOs generate and analyze information regarding market offerings, brand perceptions, and channel relationships.
CMOs can use this expertise to tangibilize the brand’s value to alliance partners.
To the degree that the CMO can demonstrate the brand’s value to the alliance, ambiguities regarding the brand’s contributions (per $\mathbf { H } _ { 1 } )$ are dissipated.
In turn, the focal firm can be more assured of appropriating fair value in an alliance structure (i.e., its need to engage in acquisitions with increasing value-appropriation emphasis [per H<sub>1</sub>] is attenuated).
<!-- para 37 -->
As boundary-spanners “embedded” in a matrix of internal and external ties, CMOs possess a distinctive relational role, which can facilitate the management of alliance partners (Boyd, Chandy, and Cunha 2010).
The CMO’s ties to channel partners can relay credible information regarding a prospective partner’s reputation and performance history.
Informal interactions via referral networks can uncover “soft” information regarding the prospect’s capabilities and motivations.
CMOs can leverage these inputs to weed out poor-quality partners and reduce the risk of adverse selection ex ante (Bergen, Dutta, and Walker 1992).
They can also draw on learnings from prior collaborations and their “situated” understanding of the marketplace to craft agreements, processes, and benchmarks to motivate alliance partner cooperation ex post (Whitler and Farris 2017).
CMOs’ marketplace ties further support the firm’s ability to monitor partners and deter breaches.
Nonmarketing executives do not possess the same array of boundary-spanning ties and cannot duplicate these CMO advantages.
Consistently, industry reports depict how CMOs “develop partners” (Court 2007) and are often also “looped in” in the “M&A game” (PMW 2022).
Academics agree, noting that CMOs at firms such as GE “facilitate alliances” (Aaker 2008, p. 4; see also Whitler, Morgan, and Rego 2020).
<!-- para 38 -->
Recall here that an emphasis on value appropriation motivates reliance on acquisitions also because marketing (vs. R&D) assets (1) are less modular (which exposes broader swathes of brand–customer assets to the risk of dilution) and (2) require more frequent adaptations (which offers the alliance partner repeated openings to engage in opportunistic holdup) (H1).
Essentially, the modularity and adaptation attributes represent governance hazards, which make “market” exchange less efficient than “hierarchical” forms, as per the standard TCE prediction.
However, to the degree that CMOs facilitate ex ante the selective entry of high-quality alliance partners and their diligent monitoring ex post, the partner will be motivated to cooperate even in the presence of these hazards.
This implies that when a CMO is present, the requirement to engage in acquisitions—a costly, high-commitment strategy —which ordinarily would be pronounced with an appropriation emphasis (per $\mathbf { H } _ { 1 } ) ,$ is diminished.
In contrast, absent the CMO, the firm is comparatively lacking in partner selection and management capabilities; in this scenario, the need for the value-appropriating firm to safeguard via integrative structures persists (per H1).
Notably, since the hazards (modularity and adaptation attributes) are more pronounced with a value-appropriation (vs. creation) strategy to begin with $( \mathrm { H _ { 1 } } ) ,$ it is precisely for marketing (vs. R&D) assets that the moderating effect of CMO presence should be most palpable.
<!-- para 39 -->
Finally, consider this moderation through the RBV lens.
As noted previously, marketing assets such as brand–customer relationships, which undergird an appropriation emphasis, possess tacit qualities.
Sharing and integrating such “embedded” resources are impeded in market alliances; instead, we pre-dicted that an appropriation emphasis would be associated with acquisitive modes (H1).
However, $\mathrm { C M O s ^ { \ast } }$ boundary-spanning role endows them with collaborative know-how—that is, competencies in building relational ties across stakeholders (Boyd, Chandy, and Cunha 2010)—which is conducive to transferring tacit competencies.
Thus, Whitler, Morgan, and Rego (2020, p. 13) find that CMOs leverage their relational role to foster “communities of practice” that “strengthen collaboration” between marketing organizations.
Kale, Singh, and Bell (2009) describe how CMOs helmed interfirm alliances at the multinational giant Phillips for executing critical “go-to-market” tasks.
Thus, from the standpoint of RBV, to the extent that CMOs can promote the exchange of tacit knowledge between firms, the focal firm’s need to rely on acquisitions, which follows from an appropriation emphasis (per $\mathrm { H _ { 1 } ) } ,$ is diminished.
Absent the CMO, however, these tacit tasks will continue to prompt a greater likelihood of acquisitions with increasing value-appropriation emphasis (per H1).
<!-- para 40 -->
In sum, both the TCE and RBV lenses suggest that CMO presence will attenuate the value appropriation–acquisition linkage proposed in $\mathrm { H } _ { 1 } .$ .
Therefore, we hypothesize:
<!-- para 41 -->
$\mathbf { H _ { 2 } } ;$ CMO presence weakens the positive association between value-appropriation (relative to value-creation) emphasis and the likelihood of choosing acquisitions over alliances.
<!-- para 43 -->
We hypothesized that increasing appropriation emphasis will enhance the likelihood of acquisitions as marketing (vs. R&D) assets are characterized by more pronounced governance risks (greater valuation ambiguity, greater adaptation demands, and lower modularity).
We contend that analysts, as independent external evaluators of firm decisions, foster an information environment (Livnat and Zhang 2012) in which these governance risks are alleviated (Chakravarty and Grewal 2016).
<!-- para 44 -->
First, consider the valuation ambiguity problem, which we argued is associated to a greater degree with marketing (vs. R&D) assets.
Barth, Beaver, and Landsman (2001) hold that analyst assessments can imply “informative pricing” to reflect the fundamental value of precisely such intangible assets.
By utilizing objective and subjective frameworks to “link” intangible assets to their business generation prospects, analysts “validate” the business logic of marketing resources and “interpret” their relevance for outside partners (Luo and De Jong 2012).
Thus, analysts can help parse out uncertainties regarding the performance potential of a firm’s brand–customer assets (Bayer, Tuli, and Skiera 2017).
For instance, in the Disney– Charter Communications cable deal, analysts projected some plausible pricing for the specific transaction, given Charter’s marketing resources and Disney’s production content (Reuters 2023).
Such analyst commentary, which illuminates the coaccretive worth and value-generation potential of alliance partners’ assets in a targeted fashion (i.e., specific to a deal) can be informative even for established firms.
These inputs can serve as one basis to justify firms’ contributions to the alliance, particularly where marketing assets identified with greater valuation ambiguities are involved.
As such, an exchange atmosphere is fostered where disagreements and conflicts between the alliance partners regarding their contributions are eased.
<!-- para 45 -->
In sum, analysts can clarify to alliance partners the future performance expected to follow from the focal firm’s assets (Srivastava, Shervani, and Fahey 1998), creating an information backdrop wherein the potential for interpartner conflicts —which characterize marketing (vs. R&D) assets to a greater degree—is attenuated.
Analyst scrutiny also exerts normative pressure on managers to advance candid disclosures of their assets (Ayres et al. 2019); so, the firm’s claims regarding the business-generation potential of its assets might come across as more credible to external partners, particularly for marketing assets that suffer from greater ambiguity to begin with (Aaker 2008).
Thus, to the degree that analyst coverage can assuage the relevant ambiguities—associated to a greater extent with marketing (vs. R&D) assets—the need for acquisitions, which follows from an appropriation emphasis (per H<sub>1</sub>), is tempered.
<!-- para 46 -->
Aside from ambiguity, a value-appropriation emphasis motivates acquisitions also because of the greater adaptation frequency and lower modularity attributes of marketing (vs. R&D) assets (H<sub>1</sub>).
We expect analyst coverage to serve a moderating role here, too.
<!-- para 47 -->
Recall here that analysts foster a robust information environment pertaining to the firm.
Compatible with this information role, “analysts are selective about the firms they follow” (Hubbard et al. 2018, p. 1989) and “favor firms whose … performance is expected to be good [while] avoiding poor performers” (Rao, Greve, and Davis 2001, p. 502).
As such, analyst coverage confers validation, status, and legitimacy on the firm (Collet and Philippe 2014; Ozcan and Overby 2008).
Consistently, evidence indicates that greater coverage reduces perceived risks regarding the firm as an alliance partner (Bettinazzi, Amore, and Reuer 2024) and signals its continued viability, so that such firms are viewed as superior prospects by external stakeholders (Pollock and Gulati 2007), whereas firms with low coverage suffer an “illegitimacy discount” (i.e., are viewed as having low status and inferior prospects; Zuckerman 1999).
As such, we argue that existing alliance partners who value their affiliation to the well-covered (i.e., high-status, prominent) firm will, ceteris paribus, be less likely to engage in opportunism because doing so would confront them with the loss of the valued tie if their malfeasance is uncovered.
Moreover, heightened legitimacy and validation, which follow from greater analyst coverage, should also “enhance [the firm’s] access to … high-quality alliance partners” (Dacin, Oliver, and Roy 2007, p. 175) who a priori might be less predisposed to opportunism (Wathne and Heide 2000).
Thus, increasing analyst coverage creates an information environment where, all else equal, partner opportunism is likely to be reduced.
<!-- para 48 -->
Consider next how the effects of greater adaptation frequency and lower modularity attributes of marketing (vs. R&D) assets are moderated by analyst coverage.
Both attributes represent exchange hazards, which enhance the likelihood of acquisitions (H1).
However, to the degree that analyst coverage fosters an information environment where partner opportunism is stifled, the need for the focal firm to erect costly safeguards in the form of integrated structures, which follows from a value-appropriation emphasis, is reduced for increasing coverage.
Thus, although greater adaptation frequency and lower modularity of marketing (vs. R&D) assets motivate acquisitions (per H1), we expect this effect to be tempered with increasing analyst coverage.
Notably, since the hazards are more pronounced with a value-appropriation (vs. creation) strategy to begin with (H1), it is precisely for marketing (vs. R&D) assets that the moderating effect of analyst coverage should be most palpable.
<!-- para 49 -->
Finally, consider the RBV view here.
As we reasoned, based on the nature of assets, marketing (vs. R&D) assets are more challenging to combine across firms; such tacit skills are integrated better through personalized ties and close copractice.
However, extant theories do not ascribe an express or direct role to analyst coverage in cultivating such ties.
Consequently, we propose a moderating role for analyst coverage only from TCE, not from the RBV perspective: H<sub>3</sub>: Analyst coverage weakens the positive association between value-appropriation (relative to value-creation) emphasis and the likelihood of choosing acquisitions over alliances.

## methods
<!-- para 3 -->
Our sample comprises acquisitions and alliances announced by publicly listed U.S. firms during 2009–2016, obtained from the SDC Platinum database (e.g., Swaminathan and Moorman 2009).
Our sample, which includes both public–public and public–private transactions, covers the gamut of deals that happen in practice; this stands in contrast to prior research, which has focused only on public–public transactions (see [Table W1 in Web Appendix A](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/suppl/10.1177/00222437251320024)).
We merged this sample with data from Compustat (financials), 10-K reports (CMO information), Thomson Reuters (number of analysts and institutional ownership), and Execucomp (top management team [TMT] information).
Per extant practice (e.g., Jindal and McAlister 2015), we excluded observations where advertising and R&D data were not reported (firm-year observations where values were reported as 0 were included).
The preestimation sample consists of 1,053 focal firms that engaged in 6,581 transactions (see [Table W2, Web Appendix B](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/suppl/10.1177/00222437251320024) for sample construction details).
Our unit of analysis is an individual transaction.
We lagged the firm-level variables by a year.
Next, we describe our measures.
<!-- para 5 -->
Following prior research (e.g., Villalonga and McGahan 2005), we operationalized governance mode choice (GOVMODE) as a decision between forming an alliance (coded 0) or an acquisition (coded 1).
<!-- para 7 -->
Following prior research, we measured strategic emphasis (SE) as the difference between advertising and R&D expenditures, gathered from Compustat, divided by total assets (Mizik and Jacobson 2003).
As the variable increases in magnitude, it indicates a greater emphasis on value appropriation rather than value creation.
<!-- para 9 -->
Following prior research, we coded CMO presence as 1 if a marketing executive is listed among executive officers in the 10-K or annual reports, and 0 otherwise.
<!-- para 11 -->
We coded analyst coverage as the number of analysts following (covering) a firm, as reported in Thomson Reuters’ I/B/E/S Database (Chakravarty and Grewal 2016).
<!-- para 12 -->
We highlight characteristics of the key theoretical variables in [Table W3 in Web Appendix B](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/suppl/10.1177/00222437251320024).
We also accounted for several control variables at the firm, industry, and transaction levels, which we next describe.
<!-- para 14 -->
We included leverage as it can deter acquisitions and also include receivables, which research has begun considering as a form of value appropriation.
As larger firms possess more resources for acquisitions, we accounted for market share, firm size, and cashflows.
At the industry level, market concentration might impact acquisitions, so we included the Herfindahl–Hirschman index (HHI).
Institutional ownership is included, as institutional investors can influence corporate decisions owing to their large shareholdings.
Firms might prefer a particular governance mode based on prior experience with that format, which we accounted for using the number of alliances and acquisitions by a firm in the prior year.
Per the literature on CEO power, we included CEO marketing background, CEO duality, CEO equity compensation, CEO ownership, CEO tenure, and CEO age.
<!-- para 16 -->
Whether the deal involved firms within the same industry or not (within-industry transaction) and whether it occurred between firms in the technology sector versus not (technology transaction) were both included, as they can affect governance mode choice (Swaminathan, Murshed, and Hulland 2008).
As firms might govern domestic versus foreign transactions (foreign transaction) and public versus private partners (public partner) differently, we included them too.
<!-- para 17 -->
Transaction- (deal-) level heterogeneity from announcements.
We also considered observed and unobserved heterogeneity at the transaction (deal) level.
For observed heterogeneity, we developed text dictionaries to code each transaction on its marketing and R&D focus.
Two graduate research assistants read 100 announcements each (across acquisitions and alliances) and coded whether the transaction was focused on marketing and/or R&D.
They also identified the keywords in the announcement that indicated whether the transaction was focused on marketing and/or R&D.
We next used the Oxford Thesaurus of English to generate a set of synonyms for all the keywords, which were then combined with text dictionaries for marketing and R&D used in prior research (e.g., Srinivasan, Wuyts, and Mallapragada 2018).
All redundant words were removed.
This process yielded two text dictionaries, one representing marketing and the other R&D.
We then used Linguistic Inquiry and Word Count (LIWC; Pennebaker et al. 2015) and the custom dictionaries to code all the transactions as either marketing (transaction marketing focus) or R&D (transaction R&D focus), based on which count was higher (for the dictionaries, see [Tables W4 and W5, Web Appendix B](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/suppl/10.1177/00222437251320024)).
We also coded transaction risk using LIWC’s internal dictionary for “risk,” which can impact governance choices.
<!-- para 18 -->
Finally, we coded transaction resource emphasis using the text of deal announcements.
Conceptually similar to the coding for strategic emphasis, we computed it as (marketing words − R&D words)/(marketing words + R&D words), where “words” refers to the marketing and R&D word counts in the announcements.
Increasing transaction resource emphasis represents a relatively greater marketing (vs. R&D) focus in the transaction.
We included this as additional control.
<!-- para 19 -->
To account for unobserved heterogeneity in the announcements, we deployed a topic model that relies on latent Dirichlet allocation (LDA) and Gibbs sampling to extract unobserved groups in the data (e.g., Tirunillai and Tellis 2014).
We identified that a model with five topics offered the best fit to data based on perplexity and coherence scores.
The model categorizes each transaction in the sample into one of the five topics.
We then used the discrete assignment of the transactions across the topics as an additional regressor in our model.
The latent distribution of the topics across transactions accounts for unobserved heterogeneity and limits potential endogeneity biases that might arise due to transaction-specific omitted variables (see [Web Appendix B](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/suppl/10.1177/00222437251320024) for details).

The dependent variable in our study is governance mode choice (acquisition vs. alliance).
Given the choice framework, the logit and probit models emerged as alternatives.
However, as the probit specification does not require the restrictive independence of irrelevant alternatives assumption, we use it for our primary model.
Thus, we adopt the specification for a standard panel probit model and specify the latent utility for a transaction t by a firm i being an acquisition as follows:
<!-- para 30 -->
$$
\begin{array}{r l} \mathrm{y} _ {\mathrm{it}} ^ {*} = & \mathrm{x} _ {\mathrm{it} - 1} ^ {\prime} \beta + \varepsilon_ {\mathrm{it}}, \text {where} \mathrm{i} = 1, 2 \dots , \mathrm{N} \text {and} \\ & \mathrm{t} = 1, 2 \dots , \mathrm{T} _ {\mathrm{i}}. \end{array}\tag{1}
$$
<!-- para 31 -->
Then the probability of choosing an acquisition can be modeled as
<!-- para 32 -->
$$
\Pr (\mathrm{y} _ {\mathrm{it}} = 1 | \mathrm{x} _ {\mathrm{it} - 1}) = \mathrm{f} _ {\varepsilon} (\mathrm{x} _ {\mathrm{it} - 1} ^ {\prime} \beta) = \Phi (\mathrm{x} _ {\mathrm{it} - 1} ^ {\prime} \beta).\tag{2}
$$

Next, we discuss potential threats to identify the effect of strategic emphasis on governance mode choice and how we mitigate them.
<!-- para 37 -->
First, we incorporate a fairly exhaustive and rich battery of control variables at the industry, firm, and transaction levels, some of which have been overlooked in prior studies (e.g., Frennea, Han, and Mittal 2019; Han, Mittal, and Zhang 2017; Moon, Tuli, and Mukherjee 2023; Villalonga and McGahan 2005).
These variables account for variation in choices that might arise due to common practices at the industry level, managerial actions specific to the firm, and unique characteristics of the transactions.
We also included yearly dummies that soak up variation specific to a particular year that might be common across all firms and industries.
<!-- para 38 -->
Second, we model the potential bias that might arise due to selective disclosure of advertising and R&D (e.g., Han, Mittal, and Zhang 2017; Moon, Tuli, and Mukherjee 2023).
We first ran a probit model to predict whether firms disclosed both these expenditures, and then calculated the resulting inverse Mills ratio and included it as an additional regressor in Equation 2 (see [Table W6 in Web Appendix C](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/suppl/10.1177/00222437251320024) for the first-stage estimates).
We used the incidence of disclosure by peer firms in the four-digit Standard Industrial Classification (SIC) industry and two-digit sector, leaving out the four-digit industry firms, as instruments in the probit model.
These are relevant instruments because firms mimic and learn strategic disclosure (e.g., Han, Mittal, and Zhang 2017; Shi, Grewal, and Sridhar 2021).
However, such peer behavior is very unlikely to directly impact the individual governance mode choices of a focal firm.
Instead, its effect is likely routed only through the focal firm’s disclosure, thereby satisfying the exclusion restriction.
This argument is further strengthened for peer firms in the two-digit sector because, while they are related, they also operate in more diverse businesses, and their impact on the focal firm’s governance choice, if any, should be channeled only through the focal firm’s disclosure.
As disclosure outside the focal industry but within the sector is less relevant to a focal firm’s disclosures (Shi, Grewal, and Sridhar 2021), our use of sector peers provides us with strong exogenous variation.
<!-- para 39 -->
Prior literature (Han, Mittal, and Zhang 2017) notes that unobserved firm-level factors could be confounded with strategic emphasis.
To account for this bias, we first constructed two instruments following a similar procedure used to construct peer-based instruments for selective reporting of strategic emphasis and regressed the focal firm’s strategic emphasis on the instruments along with other controls.
We then added the residual from this estimation as an additional control in the primary analysis, thereby mitigating the bias due to endogeneity in strategic emphasis (see [Table W7 in Web Appendix C](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/suppl/10.1177/00222437251320024) for the first-stage estimates).
<!-- para 40 -->
Third, unobserved heterogeneity in transactions might lead to endogeneity in strategic emphasis, thereby biasing its estimated effect.
Although such bias should be minimal in our data, as the strategic emphasis variable is at the firm-year level and the unobserved variables at the transaction level, we nevertheless accounted for it.
As indicated in our “Data and Measures” section, we ran a topics model (e.g., Tirunillai and Tellis 2014) on announcements and identified a five-topics model that best explained the unobserved groupings in our transactions.
Including this latent categorical variable mitigates the omitted variable bias at the transaction level, thus reducing a primary source of endogeneity in our model (see [Web Appendix B](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/suppl/10.1177/00222437251320024)).
<!-- para 41 -->
Finally, for potential endogeneity in our CMO variable, we followed recent applications (e.g., Moon, Tuli, and Mukherjee 2023) to employ a two-stage residual inclusion method (Terza, Basu, and Rathouz 2008).
We first estimated a probit model that predicted CMO presence and then included the model’s residual as an additional regressor in Equation 2 (see [Table W8 in Web Appendix C](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/suppl/10.1177/00222437251320024) for first-stage estimates).
As instruments, we used CMO presence in peer firms in the four-digit SIC industry and two-digit SIC sector, excluding firms from the four-digit SIC industry.
These are relevant instruments because firms look to each other while deciding on C-suite roles (e.g., Germann, Ebbes, and Grewal 2015); these effects are also statistically significant in the first-stage model.
However, such behavior is very unlikely to be directly correlated with individual governance choices of the focal firm but is most likely channeled only through the focal firm’s CMO appointment, thereby satisfying the exclusion restriction.
This argument is stronger for peer firms in the two-digit sector; while they are related, they also operate in more diverse businesses, and their impact on governance mode choice, if any, happens only through the focal firm’s CMO appointment.
<!-- para 42 -->
Overall, we expect these empirical strategies to mitigate the identification threats in our setting.
Finally, we used simulated maximum likelihood with bootstrapped standard errors (n = 600) and estimated the model using the CMP routine in Stata (Roodman 2011).

Alternative specifications.
In our primary analyses (Table 6) we used a probit specification to model governance choice, a categorical variable.
As a variation of the functional form specification, we estimated a logit model, the results of which are presented in [Table W9 in Web Appendix D](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/suppl/10.1177/00222437251320024).
The results were consistent across the two specifications, suggesting that our inferences hold regardless of a particular model specification.

Alternative endogeneity correction.
In our primary analysis, we used a peer-based instrument to correct for endogeneity in the effect of strategic emphasis.
As an alternative, we used a non-parametric control function residual approach (e.g., Breitung, Mayer, and Wied 2024) as a robustness check (see [Table W10 in Web Appendix D](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/suppl/10.1177/00222437251320024)).
The results of these alternative analyses were consistent with those from our primary analysis (Table 6), suggesting that our inferences hold regardless of the method used to mitigate endogeneity in strategic emphasis.

Alternative operationalization of unobserved heterogeneity in transactions.
In our primary analysis, we used a discrete variable to capture the latent topics distribution in transactions.
As an alternative, we also added the estimated probabilities from the topic model and reestimated Equation 2.
The pattern of results from this analysis (see [Table W11 in Web Appendix D](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/suppl/10.1177/00222437251320024) for the effects after incorporating the maximum probabilities for each case) was largely consistent with the primary analysis.
<!-- para 15 -->
Mediating role of transaction risk.
We argued that increasing appropriation emphasis can intensify governance hazards.
If so, strategic emphasis might be associated with transactionlevel risk (a global assessment of risks in the deal, including but not limited to governance hazards; it is a control variable in our model in Table 6), which should, in turn, drive firms to choose the “safer” acquisitive mode (i.e., transaction risk might be a mediator between strategic emphasis and governance mode choice).
Moreover, CMOs and analysts, who we have argued buffer firms against governance hazards, might plausibly reduce the impact of transaction risk on acquisition likelihood.
To test this, we estimated a model where transaction risk mediates the strategic emphasis–governance mode relationship and also allowed CMO presence and analyst coverage to moderate the risk–governance mode link.
As seen in [Table W12 in Web Appendix D](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/suppl/10.1177/00222437251320024), increasing value-appropriation emphasis enhanced acquisition likelihood, per $\mathbf { H } _ { 1 } .$ Per our theorization, an appropriation emphasis enhanced transaction risks, and these risks in turn enhanced acquisition likelihood.
However, CMO presence attenuated the effect of risk (and thereby that of strategic emphasis) on acquisitions, highlighting the CMO’s risk-shielding role.

## discussion
<!-- para 3 -->
We make several theoretical contributions.
Our study is the first to investigate the impact of strategic emphasis on governance mode choice.
Our results show that as a firm’s emphasis on value appropriation relative to value creation increases, it is more likely to engage in acquisitions than in alliances.
Thus, we extend the marketing literature on strategic emphasis that has focused principally on examining its impacts on shareholder value and risk.
Rooted in TCE and RBV paradigms, we offer an enhanced view of the mechanisms through which strategic emphasis impacts organizational structure and, eventually, performance.
We show that the choice of interorganizational structure is partly an outcome of strategic emphasis, a key firm-level strategic decision that is consequential even after accounting for a host of transaction characteristics.
<!-- para 4 -->
The strategic emphasis construct emanates from the marketing–finance literature and adopts an inward-looking lens, where the focus of the inquiry is the firm’s internal budgetary allocations to different activities.
This literature largely takes the resources to be given and focuses on their value-management consequences without delving into how and through which organizational forms the value-creating resources are deployed in practice.
Traditionally, the latter question has been the purview of interorganizational and strategy literatures.
These theories embrace an outward gaze, where the predominant concerns are cooperation (TCE) and resource integration (RBV) between firms.
However, to the degree that each perspective ignores the other, they both represent “atomistic” accounts, where a key (internal or external) relationship that constrains strategy implementation is excluded from the narrative.
By amalgamating the strategic emphasis literature with TCE-RBV ideas, we offer a more realistic and richer portrayal of the phenomenon.
<!-- para 5 -->
Our study also contributes to strategy literature, particularly its TCE and RBV strands.
TCE theorists lament that while resource-sharing is the raison d’être for alliances, theorization on firm-specific assets and their interdependence with governance arrangements are conspicuously absent (Ghosh and John 2022).
In particular, the choice of governance modes is ascribed almost wholly to transaction-level attributes, which essentially ignores firm-level resources (Madhok 2002).
In contrast, RBV lacks a predictive orientation, particularly in its inability to explain organizational forms (Hinterhuber 2013).
Historically, these perspectives have developed in mutual isolation and are sometimes viewed as distinct and even contradictory portrayals of firm strategy.
Our framework bridges this disjuncture between the two theories by linking strategic emphasis to governance choice.
We find that the TCE and RBV perspectives advance independent but common predictions, suggesting that the separation between these lenses might not be as stark as previously supposed.
<!-- para 6 -->
Interestingly, while firm-specific assets, particularly marketing and technology assets, are a foundational element of both marketing and strategy literatures, prior research still needs to distinguish between the underlying attributes of these assets.
Our work is the first to detail how different asset types are attended by differential exchange hazards and distinct combinative (tacit) properties, which must be harnessed selectively through specific exchange structures.
Conversely, a failure to jointly consider strategic emphasis and governance choices could result in performance losses, as evidenced by our event study.
<!-- para 7 -->
Our study also surfaces a novel moderating role for marketing leadership.
We find that CMO presence decreases the likelihood of acquisitions as emphasis shifts toward appropriation.
Thus, we advance the literature on marketing’s role in the C-suite by highlighting how marketing presence in TMTs can enable governance.
Further, while the business relevance of marketing resources (e.g., brands) is generally well understood, our study is the first to highlight marketing leadership’s role in managing inorganic growth.
<!-- para 8 -->
We also offer new insights into the role of analyst coverage in shaping firm outcomes.
We add to the strategy and interfirm literatures, which have both relegated analyst coverage to a “background variable” status.
We find that the informational role of analysts conditions the impact of strategic emphasis on acquisitions.
While firms prefer tighter controls for value appropriation (H<sub>1</sub>), independent monitoring by analysts seems to channel unique and nonredundant information, reducing the need for costly acquisitions.
These findings depict how the alignment between a firm’s internal endowments (emphasis) and the external environment (analysts) influences organizational structure.
<!-- para 9 -->
Our supplementary analyses indicated that transaction risk mediates the effect of strategic emphasis on governance mode choice.
Thus, our work identifies a prominent intermediate mechanism through which strategic emphasis impacts exchange structures.
These findings highlight the need to consider exchange risks, which have remained largely neglected in recent marketing scholarship, in conjunction with firms’ strategic choices.
More broadly, we recommend incorporating factors across different levels of analysis, both transaction and firm, for a nuanced portrayal of firms’ governance calculus.
<!-- para 10 -->
Finally, we employ unstructured analysis of textual data (Tirunillai and Tellis 2014) to account for unobserved heterogeneity in transactions by extracting latent groupings in deal announcements.
While prior empirical applications in marketing have used coded theoretical variables, we conceptualize a new way of incorporating information on unobserved heterogeneity gleaned from textual data into empirical applications in marketing.
<!-- para 12 -->
A survey of 500 global executives by McKinsey & Company finds that value-management initiatives (i.e., strategic emphasis) present a vexing dilemma for managers, who, in the absence of any guidelines, often underinvest or overinvest in these efforts (Babcock, Williamson, and Koller 2021).
Firms recognize that value-management tasks are executed through a matrix of internal and external ties.
Still, existing frameworks need more guidance regarding how to organize these ties.
In Dyer, Kale, and Singh’s (2004) survey of 200 U.S. firms, over 85% of the participants lacked any policy to choose between alliances and acquisitions, highlighting the need to study this issue.
<!-- para 13 -->
At a basic level, our findings depict a link between strategic emphasis and governance mode choice.
While these descriptive regularities do not speak directly to the performance benefits of choosing particular governance options, they suggest underlying trade-offs that accompany specific value-management strategies.
In particular, growth through value appropriation might necessitate a commitment to costly integrative structures, whereas the lower-cost alliance route might suffice for growth through value creation.
Several industry examples also resonate with our findings.
The Coca-Cola Company, known globally for its marketing assets, has typically preferred the integrative mode, as seen recently in its acquisition of the Glacéau energy drink brand.
In contrast, Merck, a pharma major with a deep R&D bench, has built various external alliances for growth purposes.[^4]
<!-- para 14 -->
Our study also holds implications for how marketing leadership and external monitoring by analysts influence the consequences of strategic emphasis.
Marketing traditionally has had an elevated role in building and managing cooperative structures (Moorman and Day 2016), which can help safeguard brand–customer assets.
As the custodian of the brand, the CMO is uniquely positioned to highlight the “nuances and power of brand … [for which] there may be no other role positioned to make nearly so great an impact” (Blum 2022).
Yet, the retirement of the CMO title by some prominent firms (e.g., Wells Fargo) raises questions regarding the role’s value in practice.
Against this backdrop, our work suggests that CMOs can help shape internal decisions regarding the need and suitability of exchange structures for valuemanagement.
Marketing presence in the TMT could thus be leveraged as a unique filter to judge the appropriate deployment of assets in growth pursuits.
Similarly, coverage by analysts appears to reduce the likelihood of acquisitions for value-appropriating firms.
In sum, firms may be able to capitalize on CMO presence and analyst coverage to reduce their governance costs associated with value-management strategies.
<!-- para 15 -->
Finally, we also conducted a preliminary exploration of the performance implications of governance mode choice.
If firms indeed “match” their strategic emphasis to an appropriate governance form, such an alignment should lead to improved performance per standard TCE logic.
To test this, consistent with prior research (e.g., Swaminathan and Moorman 2009), we used an event study to calculate the abnormal returns of an alliance or acquisition announcement for the firms in our sample.
We used the standard Carhart four-factor model to estimate the short-term abnormal returns.
We used a period of 120 days for prior returns, ending 10 days before the event day.
Then, we estimated the abnormal returns as the difference between the actual and expected stock returns and used that to calculate cumulative abnormal returns.
<!-- para 16 -->
We find that as emphasis shifts toward value appropriation versus creation, abnormal returns for acquisitions are higher than that for alliances (i.e., the joint effect of increasing appropriation emphasis and acquisitions is favorable).
This result is consistent with TCE’s logic that performance (abnormal returns in our case) results from a “match” between exchange hazards (reflected in strategic emphasis, i.e., the underlying assets) on the one hand and the safeguarding structures (governance modes) on the other.
<!-- para 18 -->
Our study has certain limitations, which also point to research opportunities.
First, we viewed strategic emphasis in marketing and R&D terms, but integrating other elements in the operationalization (e.g., receivables) could provide new insights.
Second, our arguments regarding the lack of modularity of marketing assets (H<sub>1</sub>) might hold more strongly for mono- versus multi-brand firms.
Next, we considered expenditures in advertising and R&D to capture the strategic emphasis construct, but direct measures of capabilities (in marketing and technology domains) could enrich the construct.
Relatedly, we assumed that advertising and R&D expenditures convert to corresponding resource bases, but firms differ in how efficiently they can convert expenditures to assets.
As an initial step, we directly estimated the efficiency of the firms in our sample concerning converting the inputs (advertising & R&D expenses) to performance outputs (return on assets) through stochastic frontier estimation.
The resulting estimates of (1) marketing capabilities had a positive effect while (2) R&D capabilities had a negative effect on governance mode choice, which parallels the pattern observed for our strategic (value appropriation vs. claiming) emphasis measure ([Table W13, Web Appendix D](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/suppl/10.1177/00222437251320024)).
Thus, these capability measures behave similarly to our expenditurebased measure, but firm-specific efficiency differences for strategic emphasis merit further investigation.
<!-- para 19 -->
Further, CMOs might be dropped postacquisition, so firms might no longer accrue the benefits of their relational competencies.
The prevalence and upshot of such scenarios need to be ascertained.
Finally, the effects of a partner firm’s strategic emphasis and those of the partner firm’s CMO and analyst coverage relative to the focal firm’s decisions and outcomes remain wholly uncharted and represent interesting avenues for future research.
