# mao_et_al_2021_before_it_s_too_late_product_re 句子库存

## introduction
<!-- para 2 -->
Due to increasing product complexity and more stringent product safety standards, product recalls are inevitable, hurt firm performance, and must be well managed (Berman 1999, Tang 2008).
Firms make multiple decisions throughout a recall process.
Shortly after a potential defect is exposed, a firm must investigate whether this defect requires an extensive recall and, if warranted, when to initiate this recall.
An immediate recall, which occurs right after firms identify their product defects, may lead to significant sales losses, but a recall that takes place a long time after a defect is identified (i.e., delayed recall) may lead to high costs: to fix defects, to offset high safety risks to consumers, and to pay heavy penalties issued by governments.
As product safety is crucial for product users and society, exploring how firms make their respective recall timing decisions deserves further investigation, so that consumers may benefit from fewer severely delayed recalls.
<!-- para 3 -->
A firm may make passive, at times unethical, recall timing decisions in two ways to delay the negative impact from a recall, especially for products with long life cycles.
First, the firm can deliberately ignore its investigation result and delay a recall long after the investigation.
The 10-year recall delay due to General Motors' (GM) faulty ignition switches offers an example (U.S. House of Representatives 2014): although GM started to investigate its defects in 2004, it did not initiate the recall that eventually involved more than 30 million vehicles until 2014 (long after its investigation had ended).
Second, a firm can be passive in its investigation process or even manipulate the investigation duration needed to identify a defect.
Toyota's recall scandal of unintended acceleration in 2009–2010 revealed that Toyota had identified its sticky pedal issue causing unintended acceleration in 2008 but had nonetheless concealed this issue from regulators (U.S. Department of Justice 2014).
Both recalls received great attention.
In 2014, Toyota was fined US$1.2 billion for dismissing the criminal fraud charge by the U.S. Department of Justice, admitting that it hid safety defects from the public (U.S. Department of Justice 2014).
Similarly, GM paid a fine of US$900 million to defer the criminal prosecution from the U.S. Attorney's Office (in New York) for concealing information (Shepardson 2018).
Moreover, additional reports have shown that some large firms, including GM and Ford, conducted cost-benefit analyses that revealed that the cost/loss of recalling those defective products actually outweighed the amount of money they would pay for damages in lawsuits; thus, such firms acted passively with respect to consumer complaints and accidents related to defect issues (Levine Law 2014).
<!-- para 4 -->
In this paper, we explicitly model the defect investigation and recall process for products with long life cycles and ask the following three questions.
First, as not all defects require recalls, when a potential defect occurs, will a firm proactively exert investigation efforts to quickly determine whether a recall is needed?
Second, if a firm has identified a defect that necessitates a recall, will this firm initiate a delayed recall?
Finally, after understanding the driving forces for recall delays, how can governments use various instruments to deter (long) recall delays?
<!-- para 5 -->
We adopt a Bass diffusion model (Bass 1969) that can characterize dynamic sales over a product cycle, in which sales are affected by external influences (e.g., public media) and internal influences (e.g., word of mouth).
However, we modify our Bass diffusion model by incorporating an incidence of a product defect, which is exposed at a certain time and is only observable to the firm and its existing product users.
The defect may be associated with a low-harm or a high-harm hazard to product users, and only a high-harm defect would require a recall.
We then focus on a profit-maximizing firm's ex post decision after the defect is noticed: this firm must decide whether to exert extra investigation efforts to identify whether the defect needs a recall and, if so, when to make that recall.
A prompt recall leads to negative media coverage, whereas a delayed recall leads to negative word of mouth among existing users.
Both will lead to future sales losses.
Moreover, a delayed recall inevitably leads to higher costs to fix the defect (as more units are sold) as well as a higher penalty for delaying the recall (including reputation risks to the firm, legal settlements for safety accidents, and governmental penalties).
Therefore, a firm decides upon its recall timing by trading off its after-recall sales losses with its total recall expenses.
<!-- para 6 -->
Using this modified Bass diffusion model, we determine the firm's investigation effort and recall decisions using backward induction.
We first analyze a firm's recall timing decision when a recall is needed, and we find that the decision relies upon (1) the defect notice time, (2) the relative dominance of internal-to-external influences, and (3) the margin-to-recall-cost ratio.
Not all recalls will be delayed, but a firm that considers a delayed recall must have the following characteristics: the firm's product defect is noticed at a relatively early stage, the firm's product sales suffer less from internal influences, and the firm's product has a margin-to-recall-cost ratio that cannot be too low.
Second, we analyze the firm's investigation effort and find that it is associated with the firm's ex post recall decision, the prior probability of a high-harm defect, and the defect notice time.
We show that a firm that eventually considers a delayed recall always exerts a lower investigation effort than a firm that does not.
The investigation effort associated with a delayed recall decreases with the probability of a high-harm defect but increases with an earlier defect notice time if the defect is (expected to be) identified after sales peak.
These results remain largely the same even when we consider a learning-by-selling effect (i.e., firms requiring time to learn about the product) or a defect signal (i.e., firms can obtain private information about the defect).
Moreover, our main model results on delayed recalls are supported by empirical evidence using data from the automobile industry.
<!-- para 7 -->
Our model offers policy implications for governments should a severely delayed recall need to be discouraged.
First, governments can design penalties to induce firms to quickly respond to safety defects.
We compare three penalty schemes that governments could use to suppress a firm's incentives to pursue a long delay and/or a lengthy investigation due to potential manipulation.
However, as policy changes or legislation can be time-consuming and challenging, our model also provides directions for inspecting potential product defects as a short-term fix.
Our model suggests that inspecting complaints on products with high margins, when compared with their unit recall costs, and on products that are at an early stage of a product cycle may efficiently deter severely delayed recalls.
Furthermore, our model suggests that an internal word-of-mouth effect can effectively disincentivize recall delays, and governments can assist firms with their investigations, which in turn can shorten delays.
<!-- para 8 -->
This paper contributes to the vast extant product recall literature mainly in two aspects.
First, this study helps explain how firms make their product recall decisions and decide upon their investigation efforts.
Prior research has emphasized the adverse effects of product recalls on firm value (Liu et al. 2017, Mukherjee et al. 2021), on product demand (Crafton et al. 1981), and on the effectiveness of marketing activities (Van Heerde et al. 2007).
To reduce recall risks, firms can adopt ex ante operations strategies, such as product geographical distribution operations (He et al. 2017).
Once a product-harm crisis occurs that requires product recalls, firms may need to consider various factors when they decide when to announce their recalls.
For example, Eilert et al. (2017) find that a more severe defect increases recall delays but that such a positive relation is negatively moderated by brand reputation and recall history.
Our theoretical method is the first to depict a product cycle that embodies time stamps in a recall process, which not only reveals the impacts of a recall on a firm's sales performance but also unveils a firm's decision-making process with respect to the product recall.
<!-- para 9 -->
Second, this paper offers policy implications that can help governments better design penalties or monitoring to help reduce product recall delays and associated social harms.
Prior research on government policies/regulation with respect to product recalls focuses on product liability (e.g., Hua 2009).
Recently, Cho et al. (2021) investigated different regulators' investigation announcement effects and provide policy suggestions with respect to regulation design on investigation rules and information disclosure.
However, our paper focuses on firm characteristics (e.g., margin-to-recall-cost ratios, internal and external influences on sales), as well as the defects themselves (e.g., defect notice time, risks of being a high-harm defect).
We offer policymakers suggestions on which products should be carefully investigated/supervised, on how to design penalties to deter long-delayed recalls and/or manipulation in investigations, and on which types of information should be disclosed.

To investigate the effect of a product recall, we start with a Bass diffusion model that mathematically describes product cycles.
Similar to Bass (1969), we assume that potential consumers of a new product are affected through two channels: mass media and word of mouth.
The former is known as an external influence, whereas the latter is known as an internal influence (Fourt and Woodlock 1960, Mansfield 1961).
Following Bass (1969), we write out the hazard function (the conditional probability of a purchase made at time t by a consumer, given that the consumer has not yet made that purchase):
<!-- para 12 -->
$$ \frac{f(t)}{1-F(t)}=\gamma+\lambda F(t) \tag{1} $$
<!-- para 13 -->
in which $f(t)$ ( $F(t)$) is the probability density (cumulative distribution) function of a purchase at time t over the entire population, $\gamma$ represents the coefficient of the external influence of mass media, and $\lambda$ represents the coefficient of the internal influence of word of mouth.
We assume that each consumer only purchases one unit.
Thus, the product sales at time t become $s(t) = Nf(t)$, in which N is the total number of potential consumers.
We denote $T_m$ as the time when $s'(t) = 0$.
By making minor assumptions with respect to $\lambda > \gamma$ and $F(0) = 0$ (similar to those in Bass 1969), we find that only one interior solution of $T_m$ exists and that sales increase before $T_m$ and decrease after $T_m$.
Figure 1(a) depicts this sales pattern.
<!-- para 14 -->
We next introduce the timeline of a recall; see Figure 2 for an illustration.
The product is launched at time 0, and its production is terminated at time $T_R$.
Bass (1969) implicitly assumes that all potential consumers will purchase the product, whereas we consider operations efficiency and production costs and assume a minimum production quantity (denoted by $N_0 \equiv \gamma_0 N$) in the production termination decision.
Assuming that $\gamma \geq \gamma_0$ to ensure efficient production at the product launch time, the end time of the product cycle satisfies $s(T_R) = N f(T_R) = N_0$.
<!-- para 15 -->
A potential defect can be classified as a “high-harm” or a “low-harm” defect; a high-harm defect requires a recall so as to avoid a potentially severe hazard to consumers, whereas a low-harm defect can be easily repaired (e.g., through after-sale services) and thus does not require a recall.
As shown in Figure 2, a defect is noticed at time $t_{C}$, its type is identified at time $t_{I}$, and the product is recalled at time $t_{R}$ given a high-harm defect.
The defect notice time $t_{C}$ is the time when a potential defect first raises a firm’s attention via consumer complaints or via the firm’s internal inspection; it could happen at any stage of a product cycle.
<!-- para 16 -->
A firm privately knows the probability of a high-harm defect (denoted by $p > 0$) at time $t_C$, but to confirm the defect type, the firm must first conduct an investigation.
The firm can choose to exert effort $e$ in its investigation with an associated cost $c(e)$, in which $c'(e) > 0$, $c(0) = 0$, and $c'(0) = 0$.
We denote the firm to be passive if $e = 0$, which results in an investigation duration of $I = I_0 \in (0, T)$, and proactive if $e > 0$, which shortens the investigation duration to $I = I_0 - e \in (0, I_0)$.
Note that $I_0$ is assumed to be publicly known, whereas $e$ is assumed to be private information to the firm.
The defect identification time $t_I$ is then $t_C + I$.
<!-- para 17 -->
Finally, the defect harm is reported to the public after the investigation at time $t_{I}$.
If it is a low-harm defect, then the firm announces a compensation $r_{L}$ for each consumer who has already purchased the product, and the products sold afterward will not possess this low-harm defect.
However, if it is a high-harm defect, then the firm recalls all affected products sold before the recall time and announces a compensation $r_{H}$ for each affected consumer.
The products sold after time $t_{R}$ will be free of the defect.
We denote $t_{R} - t_{I}$ as the delay duration.

We next express the hazard function of sales by chronicling a defect occurrence.
First, we consider a low-harm defect that is realized at time $t_{I}$.
The hazard function of consumer purchases is modeled as the following:
<!-- para 24 -->
$$ \frac{f_{L}(t)}{1-F_{L}(t)}=\left\{\begin{aligned}\gamma+\lambda F_{L}(t),&\quad when t<t_{C},\\\gamma+\lambda(l)F_{L}(t),&\quad when t_{C}\leq t<t_{I},\\\gamma+\lambda(I)F_{L}(t),&\quad when t\geq t_{I},\end{aligned}\right. \tag{2} $$
<!-- para 25 -->
in which we add a subscript L to reflect a low-harm hazard function, as well as let $l = t - t_C$ measure the time elapsed since the defect notice time.
Before a potential defect is noticed, the sales pattern follows the Bass diffusion model in Equation (1).
Once such a potential defect is noticed (and thus is effective), it starts to affect word of mouth among consumers who have already purchased the product, thus affecting the coefficient of internal influences.
Specifically, we assume $\lambda(l) < \lambda$ to describe the negative impact of bad experience among existing users, and we assume $\lambda'(l) < 0$, $\lambda''(l) \leq 0$ to reflect an accelerating learning process of existing users.
Finally, after the firm announces the investigation result of a low-harm defect, consumers who have not yet purchased the product receive such information via external influences and will not be significantly affected, as no recall is granted; in contrast, internal influences are (permanently) harmed by bad word of mouth during the investigation duration.
<!-- para 26 -->
Next, we consider a high-harm defect.
The hazard function is
<!-- para 27 -->
$$ \frac{f_{H}(t)}{1-F_{H}(t)}=\left\{\begin{aligned}\gamma+\lambda F_{H}(t),&\quad when t<t_{C},\\\gamma+\lambda(l)F_{H}(t),&\quad when t_{C}\leq t<t_{R},\\\gamma(\tau)+\lambda(\bar{l})F_{H}(t),&\quad when t\geq t_{R},\end{aligned}\right. \tag{3} $$
<!-- para 28 -->
in which $\bar{l} = t_R - t_C \geq I$, and $\tau = t - t_R$ measures the time elapsed since a recall is announced.
The difference between a high-harm hazard function and a low-harm one lies in what occurs after $t_I$.
As the firm can postpone a recall, the product sales via internal influences can be negatively affected for a longer time.
Then, after a product recall is publicly announced, consumers who have not yet purchased the product receive information about a confirmed product recall via external influences, thus affecting the value of $\gamma$.
We let $\gamma(\tau) < \gamma$ represent the negative impact of the recall on external influences and also let $\gamma'(\tau) < 0$ and $\gamma''(\tau) \leq 0$ reflect once again an accelerating learning process of the public.
See Figure 1(b) for an illustration of the sales pattern with a recall.
<!-- para 30 -->
We consider a forward-looking profit-maximizing firm.
Two important decisions should be made after a potential defect is noticed: whether to exert an additional effort at time $t_{C}$ to accelerate an investigation and when to initiate a recall should a high-harm defect be realized at time $t_{I}$.
<!-- para 34 -->
We first investigate a firm’s recall timing decision, given that a high-harm defect is realized at time $t_I$ following an investigation.
We denote $m$ as the product margin (assumed to be a constant),[^1] $^1$ denote $c_R$ as the constant per-unit recall cost, including costs to recall and fix a defective product, and denote $r_H$ as a harm compensation to consumers.
Because delays can be very harmful to society, as the GM and Toyota examples demonstrate, we therefore incorporate a penalty function $\delta(\cdot)$, which is increasing and convex in the delay duration with $\delta(0) = \delta'(0) = 0$.
We then focus on a firm’s ex post recall decision after a defect is noticed and solve the following optimization problem:
<!-- para 35 -->
$$ \begin{align*}\max_{t_{R}\geq t_{I}}\pi_{H}=&mN[F_{H}(T_{R})-F_{H}(t_{C})]-(c_{R}+r_{H})NF_{H}(t_{R})\\&-\delta(t_{R}-t_{I}),\end{align*} \tag{I} $$
<!-- para 36 -->
in which this profit function includes the profit earned from time $t_{C}$, the recall cost and harm compensation from the cumulative pre-recall sales, and the delay penalty.
Given the defect notice time and the investigation effort, a firm must decide whether to implement an instant recall or a delayed one, as well as determine how long to delay the recall, should one exist.
In particular, to avoid the trivial case in which a firm postpones a recall until the end of a product cycle, we assume that $\gamma'(0) > \lambda'(T_{R} - t_{I})F_{H}(T_{R})$.
We present the recall delay decision in the following proposition; all proofs are in Online Appendix A.
<!-- para 37 -->
Proposition 1.
(A) When $\gamma + \lambda(I)F_H(t_I) \leq \gamma_0 / [1 - F_H(t_I)]$, a firm will terminate its production before finishing its investigation.
However, when $\gamma + \lambda(I)F_H(t_I) > \gamma_0 / [1 - F_H(t_I)]$, (B) a firm may not delay a recall if $\lambda'(I)F_H(T_R) \leq \gamma'(\overline{\tau})$ or $m/(c_R + r_H) \leq \underline{h}$.
When $\lambda'(I)F_H(T_R) > \gamma'(\overline{\tau})$, (C1) a firm will always delay a recall if $m/(c_R + r_H) > \overline{h}$, and (C2) a firm will delay a recall when $t_I$ is at a relatively early stage of the product cycle (i.e., $t_I < \tilde{t}_I$) if $\underline{h} < m/(c_R + r_H) \leq \overline{h}$.
<!-- para 38 -->
In particular, $\tilde{t}_I$ satisfies $h(\tilde{t}_I) = m/(c_R + r_H)$, in which $h(t_I) = \underline{f}_H(t_I) f_H'(T_R) / \{\gamma_0 [\gamma'(\overline{\tau}) - \lambda'(I) F_H(T_R)] [1 - F_H(T_R)]\}$, and $\overline{h} = \max h(t_I)$ and $\underline{h} = \min h(t_I)$ over the range of $[I, T_R - I]$.

We visualize the scenarios shown in Proposition 1 in Figure 3.
The recall decision depends on three sequential conditions: the defect notice time, the relative dominance of internal-to-external influences, and the margin-to-recall-cost ratio.
Case (A) of Proposition 1 (denoted as the “never delay” case) implies the situation in which a potential defect is noticed at a (sufficiently) late stage of a product cycle (in which sales approach the minimum production quantity), such that the firm only has a limited time window to investigate the defect, let alone consider a delayed recall.
<!-- para 40 -->
When a potential defect is noticed sufficiently early, we have cases (B) and (C).
Specifically, they differ by the extent to which the incremental negative impact of a product defect on internal influences dominates a similar negative impact on external influences at the defect identification time.
Although a delayed recall can mitigate the negative impact of a recall on external influences, it also leads to negative word of mouth among existing users, thereby resulting in deteriorated internal influences.
Therefore, if the incremental negative impact of internal influences weakly dominates that of external influences, then the firm may not delay its recall timing (i.e., case (B), the “may not delay” case).
<!-- para 41 -->
Finally, if incremental negative external influences dominate internal ones, then a firm may consider to delay a recall in order to reduce losses in sales, which would require the firm to take the product margin, recall costs, and the product cycle status into consideration.
Specifically, when the total recall cost (to fix the defect and to compensate existing users) is substantial when compared with the product margin (i.e., a low margin-to-recall-cost ratio), it may force a firm to implement an instant recall to avoid a large recall of all products sold.
However, if the margin-to-recall-cost ratio is high, then a delayed recall would then be more beneficial for a firm, which is denoted as case (C1), the “always delay” case.
Finally, if the ratio is at a moderate level, then it is then beneficial for a firm to delay the recall only when the product defect is identified at an early stage of the cycle, which is denoted as case (C2), the “early-stage delay” case; this is because the negative impact on product sales with an instant recall at an early stage has a long-lasting impact when compared with that at a late stage.
Moreover, in Section 5, we provide an empirical investigation for Proposition 1.
<!-- para 42 -->
In addition to delay decisions, we are also interested in whether an earlier notice time leads to an earlier recall time; we present our analysis in Corollary 1.
For the simplicity of notation, we refer to cases (C1) and (C2) as the delayed firm, and refer to cases (A) and (B) as the nondelay firm, even though a firm categorized in case (B) may still pursue a delay.
Also, we let $t_{R}^{*}$ denote the optimal recall timing in Optimization Problem (I).
<!-- para 43 -->
Corollary 1.
Given an investigation effort, a nondelayed firm always initiates an earlier recall if a defect is noticed at an earlier time (i.e., $t_{R}^{*}$ increases with $t_{C}$); however, for a delayed firm, this can happen when the following condition is satisfied:

(a) Fixed $I_{0}$ (without learning); (b) $I_{0}$ decreases with $t_{C}$ (with learning)*

$$ \frac{\gamma^{\prime\prime}(\overline{\tau})-\lambda^{\prime}(\bar{l})f_{H}(T_{R})}{\gamma^{\prime}(\overline{\tau})-\lambda^{\prime}(\bar{l})F_{H}(T_{R})}\geq\gamma(\overline{\tau})+\lambda(\bar{l})F_{H}(T_{R})+\frac{f_{H}^{\prime\prime}(T_{R})}{f_{H}^{\prime}(T_{R})}. $$
<!-- para 48 -->
A nondelayed firm always benefits from an instant recall, and, hence, an early notice time motivates the firm to recall quickly and avoid any negative sales impact.
However, the case for a delayed firm is rather complicated.
Although an early defect notice time gives a firm more time to consider a delayed recall decision, it also implies a more severe negative impact on the firm's internal reputation as well as a heavier penalty.
With these two driving forces in mind, we provide a condition under which a delayed firm will always initiate an earlier recall when a defect is noticed earlier.
This condition implies that an earlier defect notice time will motivate a firm to initiate an earlier recall time when doing so helps prolong the entire cycle.

We next investigate whether a firm will decide to exert additional effort to accelerate an investigation.
Given the optimal recall timing that we analyzed previously, a firm chooses its investigation effort by considering the following profit optimization problem:
<!-- para 51 -->
$$ \max_{e\geq0}\pi=p\pi_{H}(t_{R}^{*})+(1-p)\pi_{L}-c(e) \tag{II} $$
<!-- para 52 -->
in which $c(e)$ is the cost of the additional effort $e$, $p$ is the probability of a high-harm defect, $\pi_H(t_R^*)$ is the optimal profit in Optimization Problem (I) given a high-harm defect, and $\pi_L$ is the profit given a low-harm defect.
Specifically, $\pi_L = mN[F_L(T_R) - F_L(t_C)] - r_LNF_L(t_I)$, which includes the total profit after time $t_C$ less the compensation to consumers purchased before defect identification.
We let $e^*$ denote the optimal effort and summarize it in the following proposition.
<!-- para 53 -->
Proposition 2.
For a defect noticed at $t_C$, a delayed firm always exerts a lower effort $e^*$ than that of a nondelayed firm with identical margins and compensation.
Moreover, for a delayed firm, a higher probability of high-harm defects leads to a lower investigation effort (i.e., $e^*$ decreases with p), and, when p = 1, the delayed firm will not exert additional effort.
<!-- para 54 -->
Combining Propositions 1 and 2, we use Figure 3 to demonstrate a firm's decision tree with respect to investigation effort and recall timing.
First, the proposition shows that the majority of firms will accelerate their investigation process, though a nondelayed firm is always more proactive than a delayed firm.
This is because both types of firms can benefit if they identify a low-harm defect; however, this benefit is smaller for a delayed firm that considers the possibility of a high-harm defect.
Second, if a high-harm defect is almost certain, then the delayed firm will not exert additional effort and thus be passive during an investigation, as being passive can legitimately prolong the entire recall process, as well as save investigation costs.
<!-- para 55 -->
We next investigate whether a firm will exert a higher effort to shorten the investigation duration when a defect is noticed at an earlier time.
<!-- para 56 -->
Corollary 2.
A delayed firm will always exert a higher investigation effort if a defect is noticed at an earlier time but is identified after the mature time (i.e., $e^*$ decreases with $t_C$ when $t_I > T_m$), and a nondelayed firm will do so when $\lambda'(I)F_H(T_R) > \gamma'(\overline{\tau})$.
<!-- para 57 -->
A later defect notice time always makes it optimal for a delayed firm to exert a smaller effort at a late stage, as the marginal benefit of the additional efforts to reduce the low-harm compensation is decreasing.
For a nondelayed firm, the incentive to exert a costly effort is a result of savings from not only a low-harm defect but also from a high-harm one.
Nonetheless, a higher effort is costly.
Our result in Corollary 2 shows that the nondelayed firm will also exert a higher effort with a low or medium margin-to-recall-cost ratio, as the condition implies the presence of nondelay firms as in cases (B) and (C2).

To better visualize Proposition 2 and Corollary 2, we use a numerical example, in which we use the following parameters: $\gamma = 0.3$, $\lambda = 0.6$, $\lambda(l) = 0.5 - 0.05l$, $\gamma(\tau) = 0.2 - 0.05\tau$, $I = 0.6$, $\gamma_0 = 0.1$, $m/r_L = 70$, $p = 0.5$, and $c(e) = e^2/2$.
We consider three firms in Proposition 1: (C1), a firm that always delays and that has a high margin-to-recall-cost ratio (i.e., $m/(c_R + r_H) = 65$); (C2), a firm that delays in an early stage and that has a medium ratio (i.e., $m/(c_R + r_H) = 48$); and (B), a firm that does not delay and has a low ratio (i.e., $m/(c_R + r_H) = 20$).
We show our results in Figure 4(a).
We find that (1) all three firms present a decreasing effort trend when a defect is identified at a late stage, and $t_I > T_m$ is a sufficient condition, showing that the main result in Corollary 2 may apply to a wide range of defect notice times; (2) for the same defect notice time, a nondelayed firm, including the early-stage delayed firms with the defect being noticed at a late stage, always exerts a higher effort than that of a delayed firm; and (3) a delayed firm has a (slowly) decreasing effort trend, whereas a nondelayed firm may have an increasing and then decreasing trend.

In this section, we consider three extensions.
First, we assume that firms can learn how to efficiently investigate a potential defect throughout the product cycle by making the investigation duration change with the defect identification time.
Second, we assume that firms can receive private signals, which may help them further identify a product defect type.
Finally, we analyze various penalty schemes designed to reduce, if not eliminate, deliberate delay behaviors and potential manipulations during investigations.
<!-- para 62 -->
In certain cases, a product defect noticed at an early stage may take a longer time to investigate, as the firm is still “learning” about its product, which, in turn, affects a firm’s investigation effort as well as its recall timing decision.
To address this concern, we relax the constant investigation-duration assumption and consider that $I_{0}$ is decreasing with the defect notice time.
We present how firms behave in the presence of the learning effect in Proposition 3.
<!-- para 63 -->
Proposition 3.
(A) When the defect identification time increases in the defect notice time (i.e., $I_0'(t_C) \geq -1$), the optimal recall timing and investigation effort follow Corollaries 1 and 2.
(B) When $I_0'(t_C) < -1$, (1) the recall time $t_R^*$ for a nondelayed firm decreases with $t_C$, whereas that for a delayed firm decreases with $t_C$ when $\delta''(\cdot)$ is sufficiently large.

Intuitively, when an early defect notice time always leads to an early identification time (the case in Proposition 3(A)), the firm's optimal decisions follow Corollaries 1 and 2.
However, when the learning effect dominates the defect identification time, the firm's optimal decision will be different.
Proposition 3(B1) shows that even though a defect is noticed earlier, a nondelayed firm will have a later recall time due to its slower learning effect.
However, a delayed firm's recall timing decision depends on the penalty function, which differs from the independent relationship shown in Corollary 1.
For a defect noticed earlier, the learning effect leads to a longer investigation period, thereby reducing the delay duration should the recall time be fixed.
In turn, a firm can avoid paying a high penalty for a delayed recall, and such savings are amplified when the penalty increases sharply with the delay duration, thereby enabling the firm to have a later recall.
<!-- para 70 -->
For the optimal investigation effort, we also identify a pattern different from Corollary 2.
When a defect is noticed at a relatively early stage, a delayed firm is motivated by a slow learning effect to exert higher effort that can reduce compensation for a low-harm defect, as it can optimally choose a recall time to manage the risk (and thus the associated expected profit) of a high-harm defect.
However, a nondelayed firm may not do so, as the marginal benefit from the high-harm expected profit to shorten the investigation duration becomes smaller, due to the slower learning effect when a defect is noticed earlier.

Although learning effects matter, we also note that these effects are minor.
In Figure 4(b), we present the impact of the learning by assuming that $I_0 = 0.6 - 2t_C$.
Comparing panels (a) and (b) of Figure 4, we find that the patterns of the investigation effort that change with the defect notice time remain the same, except that (1) $e^*$ first increases and then decreases with $t_C$ when $t_I > T_m$ for the early-stage delayed line, and (2) the increasing trend of $e^*$ lasts for a longer period for both the early-stage and the nondelayed lines.
<!-- para 73 -->
In our main model, we assume that a firm must decide upon its investigation effort at the defect notice time.
In this extension, we assume that a private signal (from initial investigation results or suppliers) arrives during the investigation period.
Upon the receipt of this signal, the firm can update its belief and make its investigation effort decision.
In this section, we explore how the reliability of such a signal affects a firm's incentive to exert investigation effort.
<!-- para 74 -->
Specifically, the firm receives a private signal $\sigma \in \{\tilde{H}, \tilde{L}\}$, in which $\tilde{H}$ indicates a high-harm defect (denoted by status $H$) and $\tilde{L}$ indicates a low-harm defect (denoted by status $L$).
By following Chen et al. (2001), we assume that a signal is unbiased (i.e., $Pr(\tilde{H}) = Pr(H) = p$ and $Pr(\tilde{L}) = Pr(L) = 1 - p$).
We also use their definition of signal accuracy as the probability of a signal indicating the correct status of product harm, $T = Pr(\tilde{H} \mid H)Pr(H) + Pr(\tilde{L} \mid L)Pr(L)$, and define signal reliability as $\rho = (T - T_{min}) / (T_{max} - T_{min})$, in which $T_{min}$ ( $T_{max}$) represents a completely unreliable (reliable) signal, $T_{min} = Pr(H)^2 + Pr(L)^2$, and $T_{max} = 1$.
<!-- para 75 -->
After the firm receives the private signal $\sigma$ at a random time $\hat{t}$ during the investigation period $(t_C \leq \hat{t} < t_I)$, the firm chooses its investigation effort and recall timing by considering the following optimization problem:
<!-- para 76 -->
$$ \max_{e\geq0}\pi=P r(H\mid\sigma)\hat{\pi}_{H}(t_{R}^{*})+P r(L\mid\sigma)\hat{\pi}_{L}-c(e) \tag{III} $$
<!-- para 77 -->
in which $\sigma \in \{\tilde{H}, \tilde{L}\}$, $\hat{\pi}_H = mN[F_H(T_R) - F_H(\hat{t})] - (c_R + r_H)NF_H(t_R) - \delta(t_R - t_I)$, and $\hat{\pi}_L = mN[F_L(T_R) - F_L(\hat{t})] - r_LNF_H(t_I)$.
Note that $\hat{t}$ is exogenous, which will not affect the optimal effort choice $e^*$ in the maximization.
Thus, Optimization Problem (III) is essentially the same as Optimization Problem (II) (without signal), except for the probability of high/low harm.
We denote $e^{**}(e^*)$ as the optimal investigation effort with (without) the signal.
We next compare the optimal investigation effort with and without the signal in the following proposition.
<!-- para 78 -->
Proposition 4.
For a delayed firm that receives a high-harm signal, we have $0 \leq e^{**} \leq e^{*}$, whereas for a delayed firm that receives a low-harm signal, we have $e^{**} \geq e^{*}$.
For a nondelayed firm, the results remain the same as that of the delayed firm when $\partial \hat{\pi}_H / \partial e \leq \partial \hat{\pi}_L / \partial e$ and become the opposite when $\partial \hat{\pi}_H / \partial e > \partial \hat{\pi}_L / \partial e$.
In particular, $e^{**} = e^{*}$ when the signal is completely unreliable, and $e^{**} = 0$ for the delayed firm when the signal is perfectly reliable and indicates a high-harm status.
<!-- para 79 -->
First, it is intuitive that when a signal is completely unreliable, it will not affect a firm's investigation effort.
Second, our results imply that when a firm can get additional useful information about a defect type during an investigation, that information will change the firm's investigation effort incentives heterogeneously.
A low-harm signal incentivizes a delayed firm to behave actively in an investigation.
However, if a signal indicates a high-harm status, then the delayed firm will not exert additional effort.
This result is consistent with the findings in Cho et al. (2021) in that a firm is more likely to cover up a potential defect that poses significant harm.
It also echoes Toyota's case: before the recall, Toyota acted passively, even after receiving information and initial test results showing that their vehicle pedal might suffer from inferior design, thereby causing unintended acceleration.
Nondelayed firms behave similarly; nevertheless, when the marginal benefit of an investigation is higher for a high-harm defect compared with that for a low-harm defect, then the high-harm defect could make the nondelayed firm act more promptly in the investigation.
<!-- para 81 -->
We consider a penalty function in a general form to capture various concerns/risks when a firm considers a delayed recall.
Besides being a government penalty, the penalty could also represent the internal losses of a firm; such losses could include a total loss of a firm's future business due to lower consumer lifetime value, or even a restricted credit line that can affect a firm's future operations and financial situation and/or lead to financial pressure from investors.
In this subsection, we first study how to design this delay-duration dependent penalty in ways that encourage firms to avoid long delays in Corollary 3, and then we consider different penalty schemes to counter firms' potential manipulation behaviors during an investigation.
<!-- para 82 -->
Corollary 3.
A sharply increasing delay penalty function with the delay duration (i.e., a higher value of $\delta'(\cdot) > 0$) leads to a shortened delay duration.
<!-- para 83 -->
This corollary indicates that a more “convex” penalty can be more effective in shortening the delay duration.
However, although a sharply increasing penalty function may incentivize firms to have a more proactive recall (i.e., shorter delays), it neither promotes a proactive investigation (i.e., higher investigation efforts) nor helps firms avoid potential manipulation during an investigation.
In particular, three time categories exist with respect to a firm’s recall process: the defect notice time, the identification time, and the recall time.
The first and the last are publicly observed and rarely manipulated, but the second is usually less scrutinized.
Although we have considered a firm’s passive investigation (i.e., exerting zero additional effort), we extend our scope further by considering the case in which a firm can manipulate its investigation by concealing evidence and being uncooperative.
That is, it can pretend to still be engaged in an investigation even after a defect is identified at $t_{I}$ , and then “officially” finish the investigation at time $t_{R}^{*}(>t_{I})$ ; in doing so, the firm avoids a large penalty by having a zero delay duration (at least officially).
<!-- para 84 -->
Assuming that this type of opportunistic behavior may occur, we design two penalty schemes: a penalty based on both the delay duration and the manipulated investigation duration, and a lump-sum penalty based on manipulation behaviors.
The former is to identify an expected investigation duration I and take the manipulated investigation duration (denoted by $\Delta I$ ) into the penalty function, for which $\Delta I = \tilde{I} - I \geq 0$ and $\tilde{I}$ represent the manipulated investigation duration.
However, regulators may not be able to determine the exact identification time, especially when the product and/or the defect involves complex and professional knowledge.
Thus, the latter penalty scheme suggests that a firm can be penalized by a lump-sum fine if a regulator finds evidence showing traces of manipulation.
<!-- para 85 -->
Proposition 5.
Consider the case when a firm will make a delayed recall by manipulating its investigation for a longer duration $\Delta I$.
A lump-sum penalty B can completely deter a firm's manipulation behavior (i.e., making $\Delta I = 0$) when a firm can gain a relatively low profit from manipulation.
However, the penalty function $\delta(t_R - t_I, \Delta I)$ can always reduce recall delays as well as the manipulated investigation duration if $0 < \partial^2 \delta / [\partial(t_R - t_I) \partial \Delta I] < \partial^2 \delta / [\partial(t_R - t_I)]^2 + \partial^2 \delta / [\partial \Delta I]^2$.
<!-- para 86 -->
Proposition 5 shows that a lump-sum penalty can completely deter manipulation only when firms cannot gain high profits from manipulation; otherwise, this fine can only reduce such behavior.
Second, the condition in Proposition 5 implies that not only do the recall delay and manipulated investigation duration need to be complements when raising penalties, but this complementarity cannot be too high so as to ensure an effective penalty scheme for deterring both recall delays and manipulated investigation duration.
We summarize the pros and cons of these three penalty schemes in Table 1 and provide a formal analysis in Online Appendix B.

Next, we use data from the U.S. automobile industry to study the applicability of our proposed recall-timing decision tree on those high-harm defects that eventually lead to recalls.
To do so, we collect our data from the National Highway Traffic Safety Administration (NHTSA) recall database and the IHS Markit Automotive Sales Forecast database.
The NHTSA database contains both recall-related variables (e.g., recalling vehicle brand and model, the number of units of potentially affected products, recall dates) and complaint-related information (e.g., the number of crashes, fires, injuries, and deaths for each complaint recorded on a specific auto model).
<!-- para 91 -->
In addition, we resort to the monthly recall reports that are publicly available on the NHTSA's website, as recalling firms must report a clear chronology of events, as illustrated in Figure 2.
As not all recall events exert a material effect on manufacturers and consumers, we only study the effect of large recalls on vehicle sales, because consumers have become largely insensitive to recalls due to their high frequency and media exposure.
Following Eilert et al. (2017), we use the principal component analysis (PCA) to give a univariate score to each recall on the number of (1) vehicles affected, (2) crashes, (3) fires, (4) injuries, and (5) deaths in a single recall campaign.
In this study, we only focus on large recalls with severity scores above the 80th percentile cutoff.
Specifically, we classify 771 recalls from the NHTSA recall database as large recalls, 658 of which were initiated by the firm and 113 of which were launched by NHTSA.
<!-- para 92 -->
In addition, the IHS database provides sales information on light vehicles (e.g., monthly sales, total sales duration, price class).
To match the recall data, we restrict our data to auto models sold in the U.S. market from January 2012 to August 2015, which yields 27 manufacturers, 49 brands, and 529 car models with 15,361 model-month observations in our data set.
Of the 529 auto models that we include in our final sample, 222 models suffered large recalls.
Our unit of analysis is thus the set of 222 (first) large recalls associated with these 222 models.
<!-- para 93 -->
Based on the monthly recall report, we identify three key time stamps—a defect's notice time, its identification time, and its recall time—and calculate their corresponding product cycle status, which is the ratio of the current product age when the event occurred divided by the total sales duration (as reported in the IHS database).
We note that our data only cover product defects that eventually lead to recalls and only include the NHTSA database records recalling firms' actions when high-harm defects are identified.
In Section 5.1, we describe how we estimate the parameters in Equation (3), and, to simplify the notation, we suppress the subscript $H$ in Equation (3).
Using these estimated parameters together with the time stamps, we then classify the 222 models that had suffered from large recalls as a member of one of the four cases in Figure 3, after which we then estimate the propensity of each case that leads to a recall delay.
<!-- para 95 -->
We follow Srinivasan and Mason (1986) to estimate the modified Bass diffusion model in Equation (3).
Specifically, we discretize the continuous sales pattern and denote $s_{jt}$ as the sales of auto model $j$ in month $t$, and it is estimated by
<!-- para 96 -->
$$ s _ {j t} = N [ F _ {j} (t) - F _ {j} (t - 1) ] + \epsilon_ {j t}, \tag {4} $$
<!-- para 97 -->
in which $F_{j}(t)$ is the cumulative sales of model $j$ until month $t$.
By solving the first-order differential equation in Equation (3), we know that $F_{j}(t)$ follows the form
<!-- para 98 -->
$$ F _ {j} (t) = \frac {1 - e ^ {- (\lambda_ {t} + \gamma_ {t}) t}}{1 + (\lambda_ {t} / \gamma_ {t}) e ^ {- (\lambda_ {t} + \gamma_ {t}) t}}, \tag {5} $$
<!-- para 99 -->
in which $\lambda_{t}$ and $\gamma_{t}$ reflect the corresponding parameters, given $t$ in (3).
To capture the decreasing pattern of $\lambda(l)$ and $\gamma(\tau)$, we assume a linear form for these two (i.e., $\lambda(l) = \lambda + \overline{\lambda} l$ and $\gamma(\tau) = \gamma + \overline{\gamma}\tau$), in which $\overline{\lambda} < 0$ and $\overline{\gamma} < 0$ measure the (constant) negative marginal time effect of a recall from internal and external influences, respectively.
In sum, the coefficients to be estimated include the initial and the marginal time effect of internal-influence parameters ($\lambda$ and $\overline{\lambda}$), the initial and the marginal time effect of external-influence parameters ($\gamma$ and $\overline{\gamma}$), and $N$ (the number of total potential consumers).
We then use the nonlinear least-squares estimator to jointly estimate the three equations in (3) by utilizing Equations (4) and (5) at different time stamps, and we assume that the error terms for each equation are correlated.[^2]
<!-- para 100 -->
We present our results in Table 2.
Specifically, both the external and internal influences are significantly positive.
Moreover, the internal influence of word of mouth significantly outweighs the external influence of mass media for auto purchases in terms of the magnitude of coefficients, which is consistent with the literature (e.g., Bass 1969), and also justifies the model assumption that $\lambda > \gamma$.
Note that $\overline{\lambda}$ is significantly negative, indicating that once a potential high-harm defect is noticed, the negative impact from existing users in terms of word of mouth would gradually diminish with time; $\overline{\gamma}$ is also significantly negative, which suggests that once a product recall occurs, negative information spreads via media exposure, thereby significantly affecting potential consumers who have not yet purchased the product.
Moreover, the absolute value of the coefficient of $\overline{\lambda}$ is far larger than that of $\overline{\gamma}$, which suggests that once a large recall is publicly announced, the internal source exerts more sizeable effects than the external one.
Finally, the total number of potential auto buyers N is estimated to be 102,613 based on our modified Bass diffusion model.

## discussion
<!-- para 2 -->
Not all defects lead to a recall, and not all recalls are delayed (see Table 4).
Our analysis shows that even though a firm may be proactive in its defect investigation, it is not necessarily proactive in issuing an instant recall (e.g., scenarios (I) and (II) in Table 4).
The trade-offs between proactive and passive investigations/recalls rely on both a firm's understanding of its products, as well as external environments (e.g., consumers, governments).

From our model, two key factors encourage firms to act proactively when a defect occurs: low-harm defects and reputation risks.
When the probability of a high-harm defect is low, a firm always acts proactively in an investigation, as it can quickly resolve negative impacts from an unlikely recall.
This result can explain Pepsi-Cola Company's proactive decision to investigate the supposed discovery of a syringe (confirmed later to be a hoax) in one of its products in 1993 (AP News 1993), which greatly reduced consumers' doubts about its product quality and also helped the firm avoid an unnecessary recall.
Moreover, reputation risks compel a firm to protect its brand image from any actions that could harm consumer confidence and loyalty.
In our model, we classify reputation risks as a negative internal influence (e.g., bad word of mouth), a negative external influence (e.g., negative media coverage), and a penalty function.
Consumers may always interpret a recall event as a responsible action for a firm with a strong brand that can suffer less damage from negative external media exposure on negative events (e.g., scenarios (III) and (IV) in Table 4); thus, it is not necessary for the firm to delay the recall.
Furthermore, the reputation risk associated with penalties captures the fact that a firm should pay for its misconduct/dishonesty in the form of a total loss of a firm's future business and/or restricted credit lines, which can affect a firm's future operations and financial situation.
<!-- para 7 -->
Our model results also provide policy implications as well as instruments that regulators can use to suppress (severely) delayed recalls and to encourage firms to act responsively; these include (1) information disclosure, (2) penalty design, (3) investigation effort deployment, and (4) investigation assistance.
<!-- para 8 -->
First, as reputation risks can mitigate recall delays, if more information (e.g., existing users' unpleasant experiences, consumer complaints) can be released, firms facing this internal pressure are less likely to delay their recalls and prolong the recall process (i.e., making scenarios (III) and (IV) in Table 4 more likely to happen).
However, the extent to which it can achieve these effects relies on coverage as well as the impact from private sources.
For example, in Johnson & Johnson's (J&J) recall of baby powder over asbestos concerns, it was reported that J&J had refused to accept internal and external scientific tests that verified the existence of asbestos in their products and had threatened the U.S. Food and Drug Administration not to expose this information (Hsu and Rabin 2019), which placed thousands of consumers who trusted J&J in a harmful situation.
Hence, policymakers and/or nonprofit organizations must reconsider, establish, or reinforce their information disclosure channels in ways that help disseminate defect information.
Second, besides the aforementioned reputation loss associated with penalties, we also provide different penalty schemes that governments may choose, subject to different objectives (e.g., shorten delay duration or deter manipulation) and pros and cons (see Table 1).
<!-- para 9 -->
Third, our model results also propose an investigation rule that complements the current investigation scheme for NHTSA: [^5] for a product with a high margin but a low recall cost (which can be roughly estimated by the components involved in complaint reports and reported accidents) or for a product with a low margin compared with the recall cost and severe complaints that occur in the early stage of a product cycle (i.e., scenario (I) in Table 4), NHTSA can enforce investigations more frequently, as firms with these products—even those with sufficient complaints—tend to delay their recall decisions.
<!-- para 10 -->
Fourth, we propose that by considering the learning-by-selling effect during investigations, regulators should consider sharing their investigative reports and consumer complaints with firms (especially with less experienced firms) to help them identify product defects, rather than simply conduct more frequent investigations.
In turn, when regulators monitor a firm's investigation, they can impose pressure on the firm to be more proactive in an investigation.
This way, firms can be more proactive in responding to future recalls, regardless of whether the recalls are delayed or not.
<!-- para 11 -->
In sum, managing product-harm crises has become an important function of product quality management.
Product recalls are the most commonly used (albeit double-edged) tools for managing such crises, as recalls release bad signals with respect to product quality and result in heterogeneous impacts in terms of product demand for recalls with different revenue-cost structures and for recalls released at different stages of product cycles.
Our paper contributes to the product recall literature in that we examine a firm's product defect investigation and recall timing decisions over a long product cycle.
By understanding how firms make such decisions, we extend this literature by providing counter actions that a government could take to address product recalls from a social welfare perspective.
Our research is the first to consider firms' product recall timing decisions over an entire product cycle in the last step of product quality management, and we believe that this line of research can be enriched by future studies that explore further the dynamics among consumers, firms, and regulatory bodies.
