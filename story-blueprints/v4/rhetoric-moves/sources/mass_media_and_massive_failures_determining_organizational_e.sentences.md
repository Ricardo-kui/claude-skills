# mass_media_and_massive_failures_determining_organizational_e 句子库存

## introduction
<!-- para 2 -->
Early in the evening of September 27, 1997, Blas Lopez drove across a railroad crossing in rural south-central Washington State.
As he drove onto the crossing, a Union Pacific train collided with his truck.
When an eyewitness suggested that warning lights at the crossing had activated too late, the Lopez family’s lawyer arranged to inspect the crossing signal box.
Just a few hours before the inspection, a Union Pacific engineer drove to the crossing and replaced potentially defective parts.
By the time the lawyer arrived, the signal functioned properly.
Only later did the lawyer’s legal team notice that serial numbers on the inspected parts did not match those on a list provided by the railroad (Bogdanich, 2004a).
<!-- para 3 -->
Following this and several other incidents at Union Pacific, reporters at the _New York Times_ published a series of articles questioning railroad safety procedures.
However, their investigations did not focus exclusively on Union Pacific.
Rather, the reporters attempted to expose what some observers called a “systemic failure” in safety across the industry (Bogdanich, 2004a).
Their research revealed flawed accident investigations at other major railroad companies (Bogdanich, 2004b) as well as conflicts of interest among railroads and federal investigators (Bogdanich, 2004c).
<!-- para 4 -->
Much is known regarding how individual organizations respond to accidents, scandals, and other disruptions that arise directly through their own operations (Carroll, Rudolph, & Hatakenaka, 2002; Cyert & March, 1963; Elsbach & Kramer, 1996; Suchman, 1995).
However, as the example above suggests, these disruptions can also impair legitimacy throughout an organizational field, or an institutionally recognized set of interacting organizations and stakeholders, as scrutiny diffuses broadly following crises, potentially affecting organizations far from the initial events (Barnett & King, 2008; Jonsson, Greve, & Fujiwara-Greve, 2009; Yu, Sengul, & Lester, 2008).
Yet the existing literature in institutional theory provides little guidance regarding how these other organizations respond when an entire field’s legitimacy is threatened (Jonsson et al., 2009: 225).
One notable exception, a stream of research on institutional entrepreneurship, suggests that fieldwide crises create opportunities for organizational agents to change the institutional environment by revising taken-for-granted practices (Greenwood & Suddaby, 2006; Hoffman, 1999).
However, some observers have speculated that change was less widespread than they desired following the case described above.
For instance, citing a subsequent increase in accidents, a former regulatory official wondered if railroads were actually implementing the safety improvements encouraged by regulatory agencies following the _New York Times_ investigations (Bogdanich, 2005).
<!-- para 5 -->
Although institutional theory recognizes that fieldwide disruptions can lead to significant change (Greenwood, Suddaby, & Hinings, 2002; Hoffman, 1999; Meyer, 1982; Suchman, 1995), examples exist of industries that fail to reorient, transform, or substantially improve following crises (Baumard & Starbuck, 2005; Perrow, 1984; Shrivastava, 1988).
These anecdotal examples highlight the lack of a formal theoretical framework for understanding what actions organizations may pursue to preserve the status quo following disruptions in their field.
Indeed, although new institutional theory originally aimed to explain forces that preserve the status quo (DiMaggio & Powell, 1983; Meyer & Rowan, 1977), much attention has turned to developing theories regarding institutional change (Arndt & Bigelow, 2000; Fiss & Zajac, 2006; George, Chattopadhyay, Sitkin, & Barden, 2006; Greenwood et al., 2002; Hoffman, 1999; Suddaby & Greenwood, 2005).
Perhaps presciently, Arndt and Bigelow implied the need for greater research into “organizational agency in the preventive use of the very institutional forces that create isomorphism” (2000: 513).
<!-- para 6 -->
To address this gap, I develop theory regarding how organizations, following disruptions within their organizational field, may seek to preserve or repair the field’s legitimacy by using public relations efforts to engage in what Maguire and Hardy (2009) called “defensive institutional work”—purposive action to minimize disruptions via rhetoric that counters assertions of negative events or inappropriate practices or that otherwise communicates reassuring information regarding the ongoing activities of organizations within an impacted field (Barnett & King, 2008; Maguire & Hardy, 2009).
This concept is in line with a broader line of research on discourse in institutional theory, according to which the ability of disruptive events to drive institutional change depends on a social process through which their meanings are managed, interpreted, and contested (Hoffman, 1999; Hoffman & Ocasio, 2001).
<!-- para 7 -->
Below, I discuss defensive institutional work along with competing arguments regarding its direct relationship with fieldwide disruptions.
I argue that particular characteristics of an organization and its field moderate these competing perspectives and predict whether, and which, organizations engage in defensive institutional work following disruptions.
Specifically, I focus on the role of prior scrutiny of contested issues, as well as on similarities between crisis-prone firms and potential responders, as each factor impacts how negative judgment spreads across a field following disruptions and also influences organizations’ propensities to defend against this contagion (Jonsson et al., 2009; Yu et al., 2008).
I test related hypotheses on a panel of U.S. railroad organizations.
<!-- para 8 -->
This study yields several contributions to institutional theory and to organization theory more broadly.
First, the study forwards theory regarding whether firms influence other firms and their overall field following crises and predicts which firms are likely to engage in this field-level process.
This is an important contribution, given the role of crises in research on field-level institutional change (Greenwood et al., 2002; Hoffman, 1999; Hoffman & Ocasio, 2001).
The study also adds to theoretical knowledge regarding what field-level actions are available to firms following disruptions.
Related contributions are discussed following the study’s results.

## theory
<!-- para 3 -->
Institutional theory acknowledges the importance of disruptive events in promoting change across organizational fields.
Variously referred to as “jolts,” “disruptions,” “shocks,” and “discontinuities” (Greenwood & Suddaby, 2006; Hoffman, 1999), such events have been defined (Meyer, 1982) as transient occurrences that are difficult to foresee and whose impacts on organizations are potentially inimical.
These events may take various forms, such as legal or administrative happenings, catastrophes, and environmental shifts (Hoffman, 1999).
<!-- para 4 -->
In particular, salient accidents, including industrial disasters, airline crashes, toxic spills, and other catastrophes, have the potential to disrupt associated organizational, social, and cultural systems (Shrivastava, 1988).
Such events threaten the legitimacy of organizations deemed to be responsible (Greening & Gray, 1994).
Legitimacy, in this context, refers to judgments that an organization’s actions are appropriate within some socially constructed set of norms (Suchman, 1995).
However, this threat to legitimacy may also extend far beyond the organizations initially viewed as culpable, since stakeholder reactions associated with disruptive events can spill over organizational boundaries, affecting the legitimacy of other organizations and their overall field (Jonsson et al., 2009; Rhee & Valdez, 2009; Yu et al., 2008).
Contagion of judgment can impair other organizations’ access to resources, increase operational uncertainty, and ultimately threaten the survival of organizations that were not directly involved in the disruptions (Jonsson et al., 2009).
<!-- para 5 -->
Institutional theory can be adapted to suggest at least two organizational responses in this situation.
First, agents at organizations that were not directly involved in the disruptions may seek to preserve or repair the organizational field’s legitimacy by producing texts, statements, or other rhetoric that communicates reassuring information regarding the contested practices or other related activities (Barnett & King, 2008; Maguire & Hardy, 2009; Suddaby & Greenwood, 2005).
Such efforts could, for instance, counter assertions that challenged practices are inappropriate, dispute claims regarding negative events, blame actors outside of the field, or provide reassurances that ongoing activities are more reliable than typically presumed (Elsbach, 2003; Maguire & Hardy, 2009; Suchman, 1995).
This defensive institutional work is related to Oliver’s (1991) notion of manipulating external constituents’ expectations.
Oliver provides a typology of actions that organizations take to manage threats to their legitimacy.
Of these types, manipulation is the one most appropriate to theory regarding how organizations may influence an overall field’s legitimacy.
Other tactics, such as compromise with constituents, defiance of institutional demands, and concealment of activities, affect how external parties view the focal firm.
In contrast, manipulation of external constituents’ expectations may more generally affect how those external parties view all organizations in the field.
<!-- para 6 -->
On the other hand, defensive institutional work may be risky following disruptive events.
Institutional theory recognizes that attempts to influence external constituents’ expectations may backfire, as these efforts can be seen as relatively superficial or directly manipulative (Elsbach, Sutton, & Principe, 1998; Suchman, 1995).
Following accidents or other disruptions, efforts to manage impressions may be met with heightened skepticism.
Organizations that are not directly involved in disruptions may benefit by avoiding unwanted attention (Barnett & King, 2008; Hudson, 2008).
This avoidance could be combined with other steps to avoid association with stricken firms, such as exiting exchange relationships with them and discontinuing contested practices (Yu et al., 2008).
Collectively, organizations may decrease their overall “impression management” efforts and become less likely to visibly defend ongoing activities following disruptions within their organizational field, given the risks associated with more active impression management.
<!-- para 8 -->
The perspectives forwarded above suggest contrasting organizational responses to disruptions within organizational fields.
Defensive efforts may help organizations to repair a field’s legitimacy and avoid the contagion of judgment following disruptions, but these efforts may instead backfire, drawing more negative attention to the defensive organizations (Maguire & Hardy, 2009; Suchman, 1995).
Therefore, it is important to determine the conditions under which organizational agents either engage in—or avoid—fieldwide defensive efforts.
<!-- para 9 -->
Although little work in institutional theory addresses fieldwide defensive efforts, research on organizations’ efforts to defend their own legitimacy from external threats provides some insight into this process (Goodrick & Salancik, 1996; Oliver,
<!-- para 10 -->
1991; Suchman, 1995).
According to this literature, organizations are more likely to engage in active defensive efforts when their own legitimacy is directly threatened and when their defensive efforts can reasonably be expected to succeed (Oliver, 1991).
Extending this process to fieldwide defense suggests two broad considerations.
First, an organization should be negatively impacted by disruptions elsewhere in its organizational field.
This impact can be direct, such as when, through contagion of blame, the focal firm is also blamed for the disruptive event, or it can be indirect, such as when the entire organizational field loses customers.
Organizations unaffected by disruptions would likely have little incentive to engage in fieldwide defense rather than to take a more passive approach.
Second, organizational agents should reasonably conclude, in view of their environment, that their fieldwide defensive efforts can successfully improve the field’s legitimacy or at least reduce negative spillover to their own firm.
The latter argument is consistent with research on organizational decision making, which suggests that actors evaluate alternatives on the basis of “their understanding of the world and the probable consequences of engaging in the proposed behaviors” (Gavetti & Levinthal, 2000: 114–115).
Agents who expect active fieldwide defensive efforts to yield little change, or even to backfire, would likely pursue a more passive approach.
<!-- para 11 -->
A broad variety of factors affect an organization’s exposure to spillover and its ability to successfully intervene following disruptions within its organizational field.
Of these factors, prior research points most directly to issue scrutiny and organizational similarity, given their central influence on both the spread of and influence over negative spillover following disruptions (Jonsson et al., 2009; Yu et al., 2008).
First, spillover is known to be more rapid and intense in fields and organizations characterized by prior scrutiny of issues related to the disruptions, as constituents expect performance problems to persist in fields (Hoffman & Ocasio, 2001; Jonsson et al., 2009; Yu et al., 2008).
Second, similarities between stricken organizations and potential responders are also known to affect the spread of judgment following disruptions, as audience members extend their evaluations to other organizations that share comparable characteristics or experiences with the stricken ones (Jonsson et al., 2009; Yu et al., 2008).
In addition, the nature of these similarities may influence organizations’ abilities to successfully overcome negative spillover, as discussed further below (Hudson, 2008).
<!-- para 12 -->
Although prior research points to issue scrutiny and similarity, other factors may also influence this process, but I do not consider them here because of their less central influence.
For instance, large organizations may face greater exposure to spillover because of their visibility, but visibility is more directly captured by examining prior scrutiny of an organization’s practices, and size is instead controlled in this study’s empirical analysis.
Or older and more established organizations may be able to successfully influence public opinion across a field but may not necessarily face spillover following disruptions; therefore, age is also controlled in the study’s analysis.
Below, the respective influences of scrutiny and similarity are discussed.
<!-- para 13 -->
**_Issue scrutiny._** Scrutiny refers to ongoing, obtrusive, and critical attention to particular organizational activities coming from customers, stockholders, board members, regulators, the press, and other members of the public (Sutton & Galunic, 1996).
This general, ongoing scrutiny is conceptually distinct from specific negative evaluations following crises (Sutton & Galunic, 1996).
<!-- para 14 -->
Negative evaluations following disruptions tend to diffuse more readily across organizational fields characterized by greater prior scrutiny involving related issues or activities (Barnett & King, 2008; Deephouse & Heugens, 2009; Maguire & Hardy, 2009).
Accidents and other disruptions in a field suggest that the stricken organizations were unable to successfully manage aspects of their operations or to resolve threats involving some of their activities (Marcus & Nichols, 1999; Shrivastava, 1988).
A high degree of critical, ongoing scrutiny involving related activities in the field suggests that audience members presume other organizations may also be unable to manage similar threats to their own operations; this presumption is made more salient when related crises arise within the field (Deephouse & Heugens, 2009; Maguire & Hardy, 2009; Sutton & Galunic, 1996).
As a result, organizations in fields characterized by much prior scrutiny of particular issues face a greater potential for negative spillover when disruptions involving those or related issues occur.
<!-- para 15 -->
Organizations occupying such a field are more likely to actively manage the field’s legitimacy following disruptions.
Since negative judgments diffuse broadly across such a field, organizations that fail to manage external impressions following other organizations’ crises face several threats from spillover, such as impaired access to resources, higher operational uncertainty, and limited discretion over future activities (Deephouse, 1999; Suchman, 1995).
Furthermore, organizations in such a field would not necessarily benefit heavily by attempting to avoid unwanted attention, since the broad and diffuse spread of negative evaluations following disruptions would likely expand to include these organizations, even if they attempted to reduce impression management efforts, avoid direct association with stricken firms, or take other steps to reduce their visibility.
Therefore, agents in an organizational field may conclude that active defense will yield greater success than a more passive approach.
<!-- para 16 -->
**_Hypothesis 1.
Following accidents in their field, organizations in fields receiving high scrutiny of related issues become more likely to engage in defensive institutional work than organizations in fields receiving lower scrutiny of related issues.**_**
<!-- para 17 -->
Although scrutiny may vary across fields, scrutiny of particular issues can also vary among organizations within a given field.
Organizations receiving high ongoing scrutiny are known to attract greater attention following crises that occur elsewhere within their organizational field (Jonsson et al., 2009; Yu et al., 2008).
High scrutiny involving certain activities suggests that audience members believe an organization may be unable to effectively manage those activities; this presumption is made more salient when related crises arise in its field (Deephouse & Heugens, 2009; Hoffman & Ocasio, 2001; Sutton & Galunic, 1996).
As a result, organizations characterized by much prior scrutiny involving certain issues are particularly exposed to negative spillover, and its associated operational threats, when disruptions involving those issues occur elsewhere in their field.
Furthermore, organizations with high ongoing scrutiny will also not necessarily benefit by attempting to avoid unwanted attention following disruptions, since audience evaluations will likely expand to include these organizations even if they attempted to reduce their subsequent visibility.
Therefore, agents in these organizations may also conclude that active defense will be more successful than a more passive approach.
<!-- para 18 -->
_Hypothesis 2.
Following accidents in their field, organizations receiving high scrutiny of related issues become more likely to engage in defensive institutional work than organizations receiving lower scrutiny of related issues._
<!-- para 19 -->
**_Organizational similarity._** Research also points to the role of similarity between stricken organizations and potential responders, as audiences use commonalities among organizations to simplify their evaluations when assigning blame (Jonsson et al., 2009; Yu et al., 2008).
In general, organizations that appear similar to crisis-prone organizations are likely to receive more direct negative spillover following crises in their field (Fiss & Zajac, 2006; Jonsson et al., 2009).
However, the expected success of defensive efforts likely depends on the nature of this similarity.
<!-- para 20 -->
First, research suggests that organizations that share relatively central, enduring, and salient attributes with stricken firms, such as major product lines and a prominent brand image, are heavily penalized by association, as audience members extend negative evaluations to aspects of the organizations that are central to their identity (Barnett & King, 2008; Hudson, 2008; Jonsson et al., 2009).
Although these organizations face direct negative spillover, active defensive efforts would likely be unsuccessful.
Given their perceived similarity to crisis-prone firms, institutional theory recognizes that these organizations often become stigmatized as members of a culpable in-group (Hudson, 2008; Jonsson et al., 2009; Suchman, 1995).
Constituents may interpret these organizations’ legitimacy management efforts as relatively superficial, given the organizations’ central and well-established reliance on technologies, processes, and strategies that overlap with those in use at the crisis-prone firms.
In such cases, these organizations may benefit by waiting for legitimacy to recover naturally (Jonsson et al., 2009).
This waiting could be combined with steps to reduce association with stricken firms, such as efforts to exit the stigmatized group by altering core organizational features (Deephouse, 1999; Yu et al., 2008).
<!-- para 21 -->
On the other hand, organizations in the crisisstricken firms’ field that do not share core attributes with the stricken firms may initially have little need to engage in fieldwide defense, since their lack of similarity protects them from the most direct negative spillover following crises.
However, as disruptions in the field increase in volume or severity, indirect penalties increasingly affect all organizations in it, regardless of their perceived association (Barnett & King, 2008; Short & Toffel, 2008; Yu et al., 2008).
For instance, customers are more likely to seek alternatives and entirely exit organizational fields characterized by chronic product recalls, disasters, or major service interruptions (Marcus & Goodman, 1991; Rhee & Valdez, 2009).
Similarly, regulatory policy changes tend to expansively include all firms in fields characterized by severe disruptions (Barnett & King, 2008; Short & Toffel, 2008; Yu et al., 2008).
Therefore, as accidents or other disruptions increasingly affect their field, organizations that are not directly associable with stricken firms face growing pressure to actively intervene.
Collectively, these arguments suggest:
<!-- para 22 -->
_Hypothesis 3.
As accidents in their field increase, organizations that share high core similarity with accident-stricken organizations become less likely to engage in defensive institutional work than organizations with lower core similarity._
<!-- para 23 -->
Second, defensive efforts may be more successful when organizations share characteristics that were not believed to be directly responsible for the disruptions (Jonsson et al., 2009).
Stakeholders are known to blame organizations more intensely for events that could have been prevented or controlled, such as outright scandals and accidents due to operator errors, than for events that seem externally induced, such as weather-related crashes or acts of sabotage (Burger, 1981; Kouabenan, 1985; Marcus & Nichols, 1999).
When externally induced disruptions arise elsewhere in an organization’s field, scrutiny is known to diffuse more readily to the focal organization to the extent that it has previously experienced comparable disruptions, given the perceived similarity of events and experiences across the firms (Jonsson et al., 2009).
However, fieldwide defensive efforts will likely yield greater success under these circumstances, as audiences are already susceptible to the possibility that organizations in the field were not directly or entirely responsible for the disruptions (Hudson, 2008).
<!-- para 24 -->
Institutional theory recognizes that substantial debate may surround how disruptive events and their causes are perceived and interpreted (Hoffman, 1999; Maguire & Hardy, 2009; Suddaby & Greenwood, 2005).
Although some events, such as natural disasters, are unequivocally imposed by outside forces, the causes of a broad range of events that might be external in origin are nonetheless subject to considerable conflict and debate.
Even in these cases, efforts to emphasize the external origins of crises and to reassure audiences regarding the reliability of ongoing organizational activities could reduce debate and impose coherence onto evaluations of organizations in a field (Maguire & Hardy, 2009).
Collectively, these arguments suggest that organizations that experience disruptions similar to those occurring elsewhere tend to face direct negative spillover and that active defense will be preferable to a more passive approach if the events’ external origins can be highlighted.
<!-- para 25 -->
_Hypothesis 4.
Following accidents in their field that could involve forces outside direct organizational control, organizations that have ex-_ _perienced similar accidents become more likely to engage in defensive institutional work._
<!-- para 28 -->
I tested these hypotheses on a panel of all U.S. class I railroad firms operating between 1980 and 2003.
The U.S. railroad industry was deregulated in 1980, making prior years incomparable with later ones (Smith & Grimm, 1987).
Firms are grouped on the basis of their revenues, with class I carriers accounting for over 95 percent of the industry’s traffic (Association of American Railroads, 1984).
There were a total of 36 class I carriers in 1980; the number declined to 7 by 2003 primarily as a result of mergers and exits.
The unit of analysis was the company-year, of which the sample contained a total of 391.
Most data came from the annual Analysis of Class I Railroads published by the Association of American Railroads.
News articles and press reports were also collected, as described below.
<!-- para 30 -->
**Defensive institutional statements.** Research has suggested that organizations can influence external expectations by managing the media discussion involving their activities (Bansal & Clelland, 2004; Deephouse, 1996; Rindova, Pollock, & Hayward, 2006).
Organizational agents attempt to manage their organizations’ own media coverage by using press reports, spokespersons, and other tactics to influence discourse regarding particular issues (Elsbach & Kramer, 1996).
Organizations may use similar tactics to manage media coverage more broadly throughout the entire organizational field to which they belong (Maguire & Hardy, 2009).
<!-- para 31 -->
Therefore, to develop the dependent variable, I collected press releases and other official statements issued by companies in the panel.
Searches were conducted on the Lexis-Nexis and Factiva media databases for press releases as well as articles including keywords such as “spokesperson.” “spokeswoman,” “spokesman,” and “company official.” Defensive institutional work is most closely understood as engaging in purposive actions to directly counter disruptions (Maguire & Hardy, 2009); since this study specifically examines disruptions involving rail accidents (as described further below), the relevant press releases and articles were those that directly discussed issues related to railroad safety or accident performance.
Applying this criterion for relevance yielded 347 unique releases.
<!-- para 32 -->
Defensive institutional efforts, in this context, are efforts to communicate reassuring information about the safety of one or more railroad companies or the overall field (Barnett & King, 2008; Maguire & Hardy, 2009).
Such statements fall into several related categories.
First, statements can assert that organizations in the field perform more reliably than organizations in other fields (Elsbach, 2003; Maguire & Hardy, 2009).
For example, a 1995 Conrail release argued that, of all hazardous shipments transported by railroads, “99.99 percent reach their destination without incident.
In comparison, for every billion ton-miles of hazardous materials transported, trucks are involved in five times as many accidents as are railroads.” Second, statements can place blame for accidents or poor safety performance on actors or forces outside of organizations in the field (Elsbach, 2003; Maguire & Hardy, 2009), as a 1994 Illinois Central release tried to do, suggesting that “city officials share IC’s concerns about often-casual attitudes many motorists display toward [highway-rail] crossings.” Third, statements can communicate gains made or planned in the industry, such as improvements in reliability, recognition for an exemplary track record, or efforts to strengthen future safety (Barnett & King, 2008; Elsbach, 2003; Marcus & Goodman, 1991).
A 1997 CSX release, for instance, suggested that the firm was “identifying and evaluating best safety practices in place throughout the rail industry and applying those proven practices.”
<!-- para 33 -->
Out of 347 press releases, 296 contained one or more statements meeting any of the above criteria.
I read and coded all releases, and a colleague used the same coding scheme on 100 of them (29%), selected randomly.
The two raters agreed on 98 of the codings, a level of agreement resulting in a Cohen’s kappa of 0.91, suggesting high intercoder reliability (Weber, 1990).
The dependent variable equals a count of the press releases issued by a focal firm that include one or more of the three types of statements identified above.
<!-- para 34 -->
**Others’ accidents.** The study’s primary independent variable is a count of the number of accidents (in hundreds) involving other firms, and not the focal firm, during a prior year.
Railroads are required to report all accidents that result in fatalities, significant injuries, or damage above an established value.
These include, for example, derailments, hazardous material spills, and impacts with pedestrians or motorists.
<!-- para 35 -->
**Issue scrutiny.** Scrutiny is reflected in media discourse, as media coverage reveals how prominent or influential stakeholders view organizations and their activities (Deephouse, 1996; Rindova et al., 2006).
Although coverage volume conveys some information about the salience of particular issues, the tenor of media coverage is more appropriate for understanding critical or obtrusive scrutiny (Bansal & Clelland, 2004; Deephouse, 1996; Sutton & Galunic, 1996).
Media communication reflecting negative viewpoints tends to be more insidious, demanding of companies and leaders, and indicative of issues that are seen as both important and unfulfilled (Sutton & Galunic, 1996).
<!-- para 36 -->
To measure issue scrutiny, I collected a stratified random sample of all print media (excluding press releases) pertaining to the company panel.
All articles entered the sampling scheme if a relevant company name appeared anywhere and the article was an editorial, commentary, or front-page story, since these represent media items with high visibility (Deephouse, 1996; Deephouse & Carter, 2005).
I also randomly sampled 33 percent of general news stories in each company-year if a relevant company name appeared in the headline or lead paragraph.
When fewer than 5 articles were available in any company-year, I collected all articles to increase accuracy.
This procedure yielded 4,828 articles.
<!-- para 37 -->
I read all articles and separated those focusing primarily on safety issues.
The tenor of each of these safety-related articles (_n_ 5 705) was rated.
Articles were rated as negative when they presented evidence that safety practices or reliability were being contested by any constituents.
Examples include community concerns regarding dangerous rail thoroughfares or comments from shipping customers regarding the potential for damage or delays due to accidents.
Articles were instead rated as positive if they presented endorsements, and they were coded as neutral if a main stance could not be discerned.
I read and coded all articles, and a colleague used the same coding scheme on 15 percent (105) of the articles, selected randomly.
The two raters agreed on 98 of these (93.3%); Cohen’s kappa was 0.81 (Weber, 1990).
Disagreement tended to involve overuse of the neutral category; excluding articles coded as neutral yielded a Cohen’s kappa of 0.95.
<!-- para 38 -->
The variable measuring scrutiny of safety-related issues across the field was the ratio of negatively toned articles about safety for all companies (excluding the focal firm) to total articles about any issue.
This approach allowed fieldwide scrutiny to vary over time and also directly accounted for differences driven solely by the overall volume of media coverage of the field (Bansal & Clelland, 2004; Deephouse, 1996).
The variable measuring scrutiny of safety-related issues at the focal firm was calculated similarly, for articles pertaining to the focal firm.
To test moderating hypotheses regarding issue scrutiny, I interacted the others’ accidents variable with each of these issue scrutiny variables.
<!-- para 39 -->
**_Core similarity._** To test the hypothesis regarding core similarity, it was necessary to identify a salient core attribute shared by some, but not all, firms in the focal organizational field.
In the railroad industry, one of the most prominent and distinguishing attributes is the provision of passenger service.
All firms in the sample engaged in freight transportation, and several firms also offered passenger service for many years during the study period.
Companies offering passenger service are seen as fundamentally distinct, as they are exposed to a different market environment and must maintain drastically diverse technologies (Drury, 2000).
<!-- para 40 -->
To track a firm’s own level of passenger service, I measured its passenger service revenue (in millions).
For only those models testing core similarity, the others’ accidents variable was split into a variable tracking accidents at other firms offering passenger service and a control for accidents at other firms without passenger service.
The firm’s own passenger service revenue was interacted with the variable tracking accidents at other firms with passenger service.
A higher value on the interaction variable indicates greater core similarity with accident-prone firms, as it reflects a higher level of passenger service provision along with a greater number of accidents at other passenger service providers.
<!-- para 41 -->
**_Similarity of experience with accidents that could involve external forces._** To test the hypothesis regarding similarity with externally induced events, it was necessary to identify accidents whose causes, though subject to debate, could have arisen at least in part through forces outside of direct organizational control.
In the rail industry, substantial debate exists regarding the causes of collisions between rail equipment and pedestrians or motorists, with prominent viewpoints suggesting that these types of accidents may be more subject to forces outside of direct organizational control than accidents primarily due to other factors such as operator error, faulty rail equipment, or track and signal defects (Bogdanich, 2004a, 2004b, 2004c).
The latter causes reflect failures to effectively maintain or safely operate railroad technologies.
In contrast, collisions (excluding those arising through equipment problems, operator error, or track and signal defects, as was done here) can be more difficult for railroad operators to anticipate or prevent.
For example, under certain conditions trains may take over a mile to stop, making it difficult for them to avoid obstructions.
In addition, pedestrians or motorists may occasionally attempt unsafe crossings.
On the other hand, some collisions occur when trains exceed safe operating speeds and are unable to stop for crossing motorists as a result.
Nonetheless, railroad decision makers mighty reasonably believe that constituents can be persuaded to view collisions as externally induced, for instance by emphasizing that railroads may have less direct control over pedestrian and motorist behaviors than they have over equipment maintenance schedules, operator training and preparedness, and other factors that primarily precede noncollision accidents.
<!-- para 42 -->
As discussed further below, the empirical models in this study controlled for each focal firm’s own accidents.
For only those models testing similarity with external events, I split this control into two variables, one counting a firm’s own accidents involving collisions, and the other controlling for the firm’s noncollision accidents.
Similarly, the others’ accidents variable was split into a variable tracking collisions and a control for noncollisions throughout the field.
The focal firm’s experience with collision accidents was interacted with the variable tracking collision accidents elsewhere.
A higher value on the interaction indicated greater similarity, as it reflects more accidents occurring across the field that could involve external forces, combined with increases in the firm’s own experience with similar accidents.
<!-- para 43 -->
**_Control variables._** Past research and industry characteristics suggested a number of relevant controls.
I controlled for the number of press releases issued by a focal firm that do not include defensive institutional statements, since general public relations intensity can affect defensive efforts.
I also controlled for the number of media articles discussing safety and nonsafety issues, respectively, since a firm’s general media exposure can influence outcomes independently of issue scrutiny and other hypothesized processes.
A variable controlled for the total number of accidents directly involving each focal firm.
Accident severity was controlled by including the average number of injuries or deaths per accident for each focal firm as well as for all other firms, respectively.
I controlled for size by including a measure of the total assets of each railroad firm.
This variable was logged to reduce skewness.
As expanding firms attract greater attention, each focal firm’s net expansion rate (percent change in miles of railroad track over the prior period) was included.
I also controlled for the number of mergers or acquisitions involving the focal firm prior to the observation period.
Company age was also included.
Finally, year was included to control for changes that would affect the entire industry over time, such as regulatory and technological shifts.
Independent and control variables were lagged to reduce the possibility of reverse causality.
<!-- para 45 -->
I estimated models on the pooled data set with an observation for each railroad company in each year it operated between 1980 and 2003.
The dependent variable is a count variable that approximates a negative binomial distribution.
Therefore, final models were estimated using conditional fixed-effects negative binomial regression.
Panel members with all-zero outcomes on the dependent variable do not add information to conditional fixed-effects models and were automatically dropped from the respective estimations as a result.
Thus, the models include 234 company-years instead of the full sample of 391 available company-years.

## results
<!-- para 3 -->
Tables 2 and 3 present models estimating the number of defensive institutional releases issued by firms in the panel.

Results are similar across models.

I conducted several supplemental analyses related to the study’s findings.
First, although the study’s theoretical predictions could extend to either more frequent or more severe disruptions, final models focus on accident frequency.
To check for consistency, I replicated analyses using various severity measures, such as the costs or number of injuries generated by fieldwide accidents, instead of their frequency, and obtained similar results.
Second, the theoretical logic motivating Hypothesis 3 suggests that core similarity stigmatizes firms.
As a result, organizations sharing core attributes with accident-prone firms would become less likely to engage in defensive institutional work.
Results displayed in Table 3 support this statement.
Since it can be difficult to engage in impression management as a member of a stigmatized group, the stigmatization argument would be more compelling if these organizations simultaneously attempted to exit the group by altering core organizational features (Yu et al., 2008).
Thus, I conducted an analysis to determine whether core similarity with accident-prone firms made firms less likely to provide passenger service (the shared core characteristic).
Table 4 presents logistic regression models estimating the propensity to provide passenger service (as measured by a dichotomous variable set to 1 for firms that transported passengers).
Model 3 adds the lagged interaction between passenger service revenue in the prior period and accidents at other passenger service providers, with higher values indicating greater core similarity.
The negative and significant interaction effect suggests that firms that provided higher levels of passenger service were more likely to exit passenger service when more accidents occurred at other passenger service providers.
This finding is directly consistent with the stigma explanation; it appears that firms that share core attributes with accident-prone firms are not only less likely to engage in defensive institutional work, but also more likely to attempt exiting the stigmatized group.
Finally, I sought to determine whether organizations’ defensive institutional efforts were actually successful at influencing scrutiny of safety in the field.
To that end, model 2 in Table 5 estimates the effect of defensive institutional statements on fieldwide negative scrutiny of safety, as defined with respect to the main analysis described above (a small number of control variables from the original analysis could not be estimated in these models and were automatically dropped as a result).
As shown in this model, defensive institutional statements have a negative and significant impact on subsequent scrutiny of safety issues in the field.
Although prior research has suggested that organizations manage their own public images (e.g., Elsbach, 1994; Greening & Gray, 1994), results of this analysis provide the first direct empirical evidence known to me that organizational public relations efforts can successfully be used to influence scrutiny of particular issues across an entire organizational field.

## discussion
<!-- para 2 -->
Collectively, the findings of this study establish that organizations seek to influence impressions of their overall field following disruptions.
These efforts are more likely in organizational fields characterized by greater scrutiny of contested issues, or following disruptions that could have been externally induced.
Conversely, these efforts are less likely at firms that share prominent core features with crisis-prone firms.
<!-- para 4 -->
This study’s most prominent contribution is development of theory regarding whether firms influence other firms and their overall fields following crises and which firms are likely to engage in this field-level process.
Institutional theory researchers, and some organizational theorists, have recognized that organizations manage their own public images following crises (Bansal & Clelland, 2004; Elsbach, 1994; Elsbach et al., 1998; Elsbach & Kramer, 1996).
However, very little is known regarding organizations’ efforts to influence their overall fields following these and other disruptions (Hoffman, 1999; Jonsson et al., 2009; Maguire & Hardy, 2009).
This is an important issue; organizations in these situations have strong incentives to protect their fields to maintain stability and preserve access to resources (Jonsson et al., 2009; Yu et al., 2008).
However, defensive efforts by organizations not directly involved in crises are risky, and it is important to determine whether firms engage in these efforts (Barnett & King, 2008; Maguire & Hardy, 2009).
This study forwards that aim by developing theory to predict whether and which firms actively attempt to protect an organizational field when disruptions occur.
This study also adds to theoretical knowledge regarding what field-level actions are available to firms following disruptions.
According to institutional theory, whether and how disruptive events drive field-level change depends on how they are socially constructed, presented, and interpreted (Hoffman, 1999; Hoffman & Ocasio, 2001).
However, with few exceptions, related research has focused on the use of rhetoric to overcome inertia, override the status quo, and promote change following catalytic fieldwide disruptions (Greenwood et al., 2002; Greenwood & Suddaby, 2006; Hoffman, 1999; Phillips, Lawrence, & Hardy, 2004; Suddaby & Greenwood, 2005).
This study differs by examining how rhetoric in an organizational field can instead be used with the intent of defending existing practices following disruptions.
To that end, I build on Maguire and Hardy’s (2009) concept of defensive institutional work and develop theory regarding the use of defensive efforts following disruptions in a field.
Whether and how firms use defensive efforts to protect their field is an understudied topic, and its implications are important to institutional theory as well as related research on field-level change, impression management, and organizational crises (Elsbach et al., 1998; Greenwood et al., 2002; Hoffman & Ocasio, 2001; Suchman, 1995).
<!-- para 5 -->
This study differs from Maguire and Hardy’s (2009) pioneering research on fieldwide institutional defense in at least two ways.
First, Maguire and Hardy did not directly examine or develop theory regarding which firms in a field are likely to engage in defensive efforts, and this question also cannot be answered by existing research on disruptive events (Hoffman, 1999; Hoffman & Ocasio, 2001; Jonsson et al., 2009; Yu et al., 2008).
Given the possibility that defensive work is voluntary and potentially risky, this is an important research question directly examined by the present study (Barnett & King, 2008; Maguire & Hardy, 2009).
Second, it is interesting to note that defensive efforts were ultimately unsuccessful in Maguire and Hardy’s case study of fieldwide deinstitutionalization.
Although the present study does not focus on outcomes of defensive institutional work, the supplement reported in Table 5 provides some evidence that defensive efforts can reduce scrutiny of an organizational field.
<!-- para 7 -->
Despite these contributions, this study contains several limitations.
First, it treats external constituents as a relatively homogeneous group.
However, the railroad industry is characterized by highly diverse stakeholder groups, such as labor unions, shippers, investors, employees, and proximate communities, each including members with their own particular interests and judgments regarding railroad companies.
Organizations in many empirical contexts likely independently approach and manage each of these groups.
Research in stakeholder theory, for instance, has asserted that organizations first attend to the needs of powerful groups and those with more immediate and credible requests, since these groups may control a disproportionate share of valuable resources (Mitchell, Agle, & Wood, 1997; Pfeffer & Salancik, 1978).
This study’s empirical focus somewhat limited these possibilities.
Anecdotal review of media reportage and other industry documents revealed that various stakeholder groups commonly see accidents
<!-- para 8 -->
and safety lapses as negative and fairly urgent.
These crises delayed shipments, damaged community infrastructure, decreased investor valuations, and imposed other harm on the industry’s constituents.
As a result, railroads’ media management activities tended to focus more generally on alleviating broad concerns regarding accidents and safety performance and rarely targeted specific groups’ concerns in isolation.
<!-- para 9 -->
Nonetheless, additional work is needed to understand how stakeholder heterogeneity influences organizations’ fieldwide defensive efforts.
For instance, research should examine how organizations manage fieldwide impressions following disruptions that disproportionately impact disenfranchised stakeholders, stakeholders whose interests directly conflict with those of the organization’s owners or managers, and stakeholders who would be displaced by disruptions to the status quo.
Future work in these and related areas could more directly address the causes and consequences of fieldwide institutional efforts in organizational fields characterized by competing political interests and considerable fragmentation of stakeholder groups.
<!-- para 10 -->
In addition, its industry context potentially limits this study.
Studies conducted entirely within one industry are beneficial, since specific measures can be developed to directly reflect various aspects of the research setting.
However, railroad companies faced strong pressures to manage their industry’s image during the study period.
With the growing prevalence of alternatives such as trucking and commercial aviation, railroads were increasingly forced to collectively defend their industry’s reliability and efficiency (Smith & Grimm, 1987).
This collectivism may make field-level defense more likely to have occurred in this setting than in other settings, such as the airline industry, where internal competition is more salient.
Therefore, future research should seek to replicate this study’s findings in industries, such as commercial aviation, that are characterized predominantly by internal rather than external competitive threats.
<!-- para 12 -->
Beyond the directions identified above, my arguments and findings suggest several avenues that merit further exploration.
First, this study focuses on crises, as institutional theory points to the role of crises and other disruptions in destabilizing existing patterns of activity within organizational fields (Hoffman, 1999; Hoffman & Ocasio, 2001; Suchman, 1995).
However, organizations also likely seek to influence fieldwide perceptions fol lowing events with more positive outcomes.
Specifically, organizations may aggressively promote their entire field following periods of high industry-wide performance in order to attract new customers, investors, and other resources.
Though somewhat counterintuitive, these fieldwide promotions may be more likely to come from poor or moderate performers in an organizational field, because good performers would instead seek to differentiate themselves by emphasizing their strong performance relative to the field rather than the overall field’s strength (Deephouse, 1996, 1999).
Future research should examine how positive events spur fieldwide institutional efforts and should develop theory regarding which organizations in a field are more likely to engage in these efforts.
<!-- para 13 -->
In addition, further work examining the outcomes of institutional defense is strongly warranted.
Although Maguire and Hardy (2009) found that defensive efforts were unsuccessful, a supplemental analysis reported above suggests that defensive efforts did indeed reduce fieldwide scrutiny in this study’s empirical context.
The ultimate success of fieldwide institutional defense likely depends on a complex array of organizational, stakeholder, and environmental characteristics, such as the level of prior consensus regarding perceptions of firms in a field, the credibility of firms’ defenses, and the nature of viewpoints forwarded by third parties such as professional associations and consumer advocates.
Although little research examines the factors motivating fieldwide institutional defense, even less work examines its outcomes.
Research that specifically examines the outcomes of fieldwide institutional work would contribute substantially to scholars’ understanding of how organizational fields develop and evolve over time.
<!-- para 14 -->
In conclusion, this study shows that certain organizations may engage in field-level efforts to defend against threats posed by disruptions elsewhere in their organizational field.
Specific organizational and environmental characteristics influence this response.
Collectively, my findings contribute to research in institutional theory and further understanding of how organizations influence perceptions of other organizations and their entire field.
Future research should extend these predictions into different competitive environments and examine how broader classes of events may motivate fieldwide institutional efforts.
