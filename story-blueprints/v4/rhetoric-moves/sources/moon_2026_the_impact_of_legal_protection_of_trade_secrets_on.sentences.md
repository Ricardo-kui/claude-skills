<!-- para 2 -->
Trade secrets are valuable and confidential information that firms use to gain a competitive advantage (Kim et al. 2021).
By some estimates, the aggregated commercial value of trade secrets of publicly listed firms in the US is almost $5 trillion (U.S. Chamber of Commerce 2014).
However, annual losses from intellectual property theft, which includes trade secrets, range from$225 billion to $600 billion (Commission on the Theft of American Intellectual Property 2017).
Accordingly, firms often restrict employee access to trade secrets and rely on legal frameworks to protect them (Kang and Lee 2022).
Intergovernmental organizations such as the World Intellectual Property Organization (WIPO) also advocate for trade secret protection because it promotes fair competition by allowing firms to leverage their trade secrets (WIPO 2024).
<!-- para 3 -->
Reflecting the importance of trade secret protection, extant work examines its impact on firm decisions related to financial and employee management (e.g., Chen et al. 2022), innovation (e.g., Png 2017), and firm valuation (e.g., Qiu and Wang 2018).
Little, however, is known about how stronger trade secret protection affects firms' efforts to leverage their trade secrets through marketing actions.
This is surprising because the protection of trade secrets has limited value unless firms can convert them into commercial outcomes (WIPO 2024).
Indeed, the effectiveness of intellectual property protection depends on the extent to which these assets can be protected and leveraged for growth (Krasnikov and Jayachandran 2022).
<!-- para 4 -->
Accordingly, this study examines the impact of trade secret protection on a key facet of firms' actions to leverage trade secrets: advertising spending.
We focus on advertising spending because it is a key budgetary lever that reflects a firm's focus on value appropriation, with the aim of increasing consumer awareness and building brand equity (see Frennea, Han, and Mittal 2019; McAlister et al. 2016).
Importantly, from a theoretical perspective, the impact of enhanced trade secret protection on firms' advertising spending is not clear.
On the one hand, enhanced trade secret protection can lower the competitive pressure faced by firms and lead to a decline in their advertising spending (e.g., Klasa et al. 2018).
On the other hand, stronger trade secret protection also means that stakeholders from financial markets, i.e., investors and financial analysts, expect firms to increase their sales growth, and therefore can increase managers' attention to leveraging trade secrets and advertising for brand building.
<!-- para 5 -->
From an empirical perspective, we focus on the staggered recognition of the Inevitable Disclosure Doctrine (IDD) by U.S. state courts.
The IDD is a legal doctrine that allows a firm to prevent an employee from joining a rival if it can demonstrate that “the person’s new duties will inevitably require disclosure, use, or reliance upon knowledge of the former employer’s trade secrets” (Chen et al. 2022, p. 136).
This ruling stems from the fact that employee mobility is the most prevalent form of trade secret misappropriation (see Islam et al. 2022).
Building on this legal backdrop, the study seeks to make three contributions: First, drawing on the attention-based view of the firm (ABV), we propose that IDD recognition increases the top management’s attention towards leveraging their trade secrets as they face higher capital market expectations for growth, leading to a significant increase in their advertising spending.
Consistent with this thesis, results show that IDD recognition increases advertising spending of a firm by almost 13.5%.
In support of the proposed mechanism, subsequent analyses show that IDD recognition leads to higher revenue expectations from financial analysts and greater discussion of brands by top management in 10-K reports.
By identifying the capital markets-based mechanism, the current study adds to extant work that predominantly focuses on reduced employee mobility as the underlying mechanism.
Post hoc analyses also show that increases in advertising spending following IDD recognition are associated with higher firm sales.
This study, therefore, offers insights into the mechanisms through which enhanced trade secret protection afforded by IDD recognition leads firms to increase advertising spending as a means of leveraging trade secrets and realizing higher sales.
<!-- para 6 -->
Second, consistent with the ABV, the study develops a contingency framework and finds that the effect of IDD recognition on advertising spending is weaker in the presence of CEO duality, but stronger for younger firms and firms operating in industries with higher peer advertising spending.
The findings, therefore, provide empirical support for the ABV proposition that the degree to which the top management of a firm pays attention to significant changes such as IDD recognition is likely to depend on individual, organizational, and contextual factors (Eklund and Mannor 2021).
Importantly, the contingency effects are economically salient, as the differences in the magnitude of the positive effect of IDD recognition on advertising spending across the low versus high levels of proposed contingency factors range from 53% to 100%.
These findings have direct implications for extant literature and policymakers.
<!-- para 7 -->
The weaker impact of IDD recognition on advertising spending for firms with CEO duality offers a nuanced view to recent research which suggests that CEO duality is likely to be beneficial for marketing actions (see Whitler, Krause, and Lehmann 2018).
The stronger positive impact of IDD recognition on advertising spending of younger firms is important from a policy perspective because it suggests that the trade secret protection policy is likely to lead these firms to invest more actively in leveraging their proprietary knowledge.
This implication is important given the central role young firms play in economic dynamism and job creation. [^1] Importantly, this finding offers an alternative perspective to concerns that stronger trade secret protection may suppress innovation (Marx, Strumsky, and Fleming 2009).
The significantly weaker impact of IDD recognition on advertising spending for firms in industries with lower peer advertising spending indicates the need for a closer look at how these firms evaluate opportunities afforded by greater trade secret protection.
<!-- para 8 -->
Third, robust empirical support for the economically significant positive effect of IDD recognition on advertising spending also has direct implications for extant empirical research that examines effects of trade secret protection laws and agreements.
Extant work concludes that trade secret protection laws and agreements, such as IDD, the Uniform Trade Secrets Act (UTSA), or non-compete agreements, either decrease firm actions to leverage trade secrets or have no significant effect on them (e.g., Klasa et al. 2018; Gao, Wang, and Yin 2023).
However, most extant work measures firm efforts to leverage trade secrets by using the sum of R&D, advertising, and SG&A as a metric.
Therefore, by adopting an explicit focus on advertising spending, the current study updates the conclusions reached by prior work.
<!-- para 10 -->
Consistent with the emphasis on restricting employee mobility, extant research on IDD recognition predominantly focuses on directly examining the implications of these restrictions in four domains (see Table 1).
First, studies examine how these laws influence internal firm actions, particularly financial management and employee policies.
Following enhanced trade secret protection, labor mobility is restricted, meaning managers cannot switch jobs easily, which causes them to become more risk-averse and leads firms to increase performance-based executive compensation to encourage risk-taking (Chen et al. 2022; Islam et al. 2022).
Second, research finds that firms reduce proprietary disclosures such as major customer identities (Li, Lin, and Zhang 2018) and redact commercially sensitive information (Glaeser 2018) under stronger trade secret protection in response to the potentially higher proprietary costs of making such disclosure. <!-- para 14 -->
Third, studies examine firms' innovation behavior and find that on one hand, firms increase investments in knowledge assets and research and development (R&D).
On the other hand, higher protection of trade secrets dampens patenting and inventor incentives leading to lower innovation output because, under reduced labor mobility, investors may have less incentives to invest in patents to signal their ability in the labor market (Gu et al. 2022).
<!-- para 15 -->
Finally, research assesses innovation outcomes, firm performance and valuation, and shows that although innovation output (i.e., patent quality and quantity) declines, firm valuation and acquisition activity improve (e.g., Chen, Gao and Ma 2021; Contigiani, Hsu, and Barankay 2018).
Across these domains, the proposed predominant mechanism is reduced employee mobility which in turn affects knowledge leakage, career concerns, and incentive structures (see Table 1).
A few studies that examine capital market mechanisms focus on financing frictions and information asymmetry through which trade secret protection operates (Castellaneta et al. 2016; Glaeser 2018; Guernsey, John, and Litov 2022).
<!-- para 16 -->
Extant research, however, seldom examines the effect of enhanced trade secret protection on how capital market expectations are likely to have an impact on firm actions to leverage trade secrets.
Adopting an aggregated measurement approach, a few studies conduct post hoc analyses to explore the impact of enhanced trade secret protection on firm actions to leverage it.
For example, Chen, Zhang, and Zhou (2018) find that higher enforceability of non-compete agreements leads firms to decrease their “discretionary spending” as measured by the sum of R&D, advertising, and selling, general, and administrative expense (SG&A).
Jeffers (2024), however, finds that higher enforceability of non-compete agreements increases firm investments in physical assets, but not in intangible assets, as reflected in R&D spending.
Focusing on IDD, Klasa et al. (2018) find that IDD recognition does not have an effect on firm actions to leverage their “new ideas” as reflected in the sum of R&D, advertising, and capital expenditures.
Similarly, Gao, Wang, and Yin (2023) find that whereas IDD recognition lowers cost elasticity, it does not have an impact on the sum of R&D and advertising spending.
In summary, from extant work, it is not clear if and how IDD recognition is likely to have an impact on firms' marketing decisions in general, and advertising spending in particular.
<!-- para 17 -->
Examining the impact of trade secret protection afforded by IDD specifically on advertising spending is important because advertising spending is the most direct reflection of a firm’s efforts in leveraging its trade secrets (Frennea, Han, and Mittal 2019).
Therefore, the current study examines the impact of trade secret protection afforded by IDD recognition on advertising spending and draws on the attention-based view (ABV) of firms.
ABV posits that managerial decisions are shaped by what captures their attention, defined as a “focus of time and effort with making sense of a firm’s environment and how to respond to it” (Umashankar, Bahadir, and Bharadwaj 2022, p. 70).
Importantly, attention is scarce, and managerial attention can determine resource allocation decisions (Ocasio 1997).
In particular, managers tend to pay close attention to capital market expectations (Washburn and Bromiley 2014).
For instance, earnings pressure, stemming from the likelihood that the firm’s stock price will decline if the firm fails to meet or beat securities analysts’ earnings forecasts, forces managers to selectively focus their scarce time and effort on prioritizing earnings over long-term strategic initiatives (Zhang and Gong 2018).
<!-- para 18 -->
Extant research shows that managerial attention to capital market expectations can significantly impact marketing outcomes.
For example, Umashankar, Bahadir, and Bharadwaj (2022) argue that in the post-merger and acquisition period, senior management prioritizes financial integration over customer concerns, leading to a decline in customer satisfaction.
More recently, Martin and Kushwaha (2024) find that managerial attention on earnings targets increases the likelihood of myopic advertising and R&D spending cuts.
Thus, examining how managers increase their attention in responding to capital market expectations following stronger trade secret protection may offer insights into firm behavior.
<!-- para 20 -->
Our central thesis is that the recognition of IDD in a state increases advertising spending by firms headquartered in that state because the stronger protection of trade secrets may lead managers to pay more attention to leveraging their trade secrets.
Higher managerial attention towards leveraging trade secrets following IDD recognition in a state is likely to occur for two key reasons.
First, IDD recognition is likely to lead to expectations of higher growth from capital markets because investors and analysts expect firms to be able to translate the enhanced protection of trade secrets into growth (see Chen, Gao, and Ma 2021).
Second, senior managers are more likely to pay attention to the capital market expectations for growth because by protecting against trade secrets leakage due to employee mobility (Nguyen, Pham, and Qiu 2023), IDD recognition may shift managerial attention previously devoted to the protection of trade secrets.
<!-- para 21 -->
As IDD recognition increases managers' attention toward growth expectations, they become more mindful of performance outcomes that signal their managerial ability to drive growth (Demerjian, Lev, and McVay 2012).
Greater senior management attention towards growth is likely to result in an increase in advertising spending because it is a critical budgetary lever that facilitates value appropriation (e.g., Frennea, Han, and Mittal 2019; Mizik and Jacobson 2003; Swaminathan, Murshed, and Hulland 2008).
<!-- para 22 -->
However, it can be argued that because IDD recognition enhances protection of trade secrets, it is likely to lower top management’s concerns about competitive pressures and result in a decline in advertising spending.
Consistent with this logic, research finds that IDD recognition is likely to increase firm financial leverage (see Klasa et al. 2018).
However, this is unlikely to be the case because expectations of growth from capital markets play a salient role in driving decisions of top management.
In fact, research in finance consistently finds that top management pays very close attention to investor and analyst expectations of firm performance and strives to meet these expectations (see Graham, Harvey, and Rajgopal 2005; Washburn and Bromiley 2014).
Indeed, research shows that advertising spending is likely to lead investors to value a firm based on its idiosyncratic attributes, as opposed to industry averages (see Cheong, Hoffmann, and Zurbruegg 2021).
Importantly, higher advertising spending is likely to enhance brand awareness (McAlister et al. 2016), improve consumer choice likelihood (Clark, Doraszelski, and Draganska 2009), and ultimately drive higher sales (Hanssens, Michelozzi, and Mizik 2023).
Therefore, we expect that IDD recognition in a state is likely to lead to a significant increase in advertising spending of firms that are headquartered in that state, as compared to firms headquartered in states that have not recognized IDD.
Formally:
<!-- para 23 -->
$H_{1}$: A firm in a state that recognizes IDD increases its advertising spending following the IDD recognition.
<!-- para 25 -->
As a significant legal development, IDD recognition is widely discussed in the media, with national and broadcast news services “detailing cases of employers bringing action to prevent a former employee from joining a rival company to protect trade secrets” (Dey and White 2021, p.19).
Because this extensive coverage signals a major change in the institutional environment, it is likely to draw the attention of top management and prompt firms to reconsider the effectiveness of their current strategies while evaluating newly emerging strategic opportunities (Chattopadhyay and Bercovitz 2020).
Research on the attention-based view of the firm, however, argues that because managerial attention is scarce, the degree to which the top management of a firm pays attention to the changes such as IDD recognition may depend on individual, organizational, and contextual factors (see for example, Nicolini and Korica 2021).
Accordingly, we examine three key factors that are likely to have a significant impact on the degree to which top management’s attention increases towards leveraging trade secrets following IDD recognition: CEO duality, firm age, and peer advertising spending.
<!-- para 26 -->
At the individual level, reflecting the importance of the CEO as the key decision maker of the firm, we examine CEO duality, wherein the firm’s CEO also serves as the board chair (Krause, Semadeni, and Cannella 2014).
CEO duality is a critical aspect because it directly influences managerial attention by centralizing decision-making authority and strategy implementation (Whitler, Krause, and Lehmann 2018).
At the organizational level, firm age affects managerial attention by shaping routines and strategic inertia.
Older firms may have entrenched attentional structures that limit their adaptability in capitalizing on new opportunities, whereas younger firms are more efficient and flexible and can prioritize their attention to overcome legitimacy challenges and resource constraints (Bhattacharya, Morgan, and Rego 2022; Saboo, Chakravarty, and Grewal 2016).
At the environmental level, peer firms’ advertising spending is likely to serve as an external reference point that signals competitive pressure.
Peer advertising spending levels are likely to significantly impact managerial attention and influence resource allocation decisions to overcome advertising interference effects (Gijsenberg and Nijs 2019; also see Shi, Grewal, and Sridhar 2021).
<!-- para 27 -->
Taken together, we expect that each of these factors shapes the degree to which top management is likely to pay attention to the capital market expectations of growth following IDD recognition and subsequently change their advertising spending to leverage trade secrets.
<!-- para 29 -->
CEO duality occurs when a CEO also serves as a chairperson of the board of directors (Boyd 1995).
This structural arrangement provides CEOs with substantial decision-making authority, increased autonomy, and greater job security (Finkelstein and D'Aveni 1994).
CEO duality also “represents a fundamental element in the structural arrangement of a board” (Tuggle et al. 2010, p. 947).
From a corporate governance perspective, this diminishes board oversight and the external accountability of CEOs (Ruigrok, Peck, and Keller 2006; Tuggle et al. 2010).
Consequently, dual-role CEOs often become entrenched and insulated from external stakeholder pressures, such as investors, analysts, or market expectations (see Krause, Semadeni, and Cannella 2014; Graham, Kim, and Leary 2020).
<!-- para 30 -->
IDD recognition changes the attentional landscape for CEOs by removing some of the attention required to protect trade secrets and creating space for focusing on how to leverage them.
Indeed, firms increase performance-based pay to encourage CEOs to take greater risks after IDD recognition (Chen et al. 2022; Islam et al. 2022).
As dual-role CEOs perceive lower personal risk associated with their decisions, performance-based pay adjustments designed specifically to mitigate CEOs' risk aversion following IDD recognition may have a weaker impact on CEOs with dual roles.
In fact, the ABV argues that such power concentration influences what issues managers prioritize through shaping how they interpret and respond to environmental cues (Ocasio 1997).
Thus, dual-role CEOs are less likely to redirect their attention from protection to leveraging trade secrets following IDD recognition because their positions already shield them from potential risks, such as termination and external accountability pressures (Lim 2015; Lim and Mccann 2013).
<!-- para 31 -->
Taken together, greater job security and lower perceived pressures from external factors for CEOs with dual roles suggest that they are less likely to shift their attention to leveraging trade secrets due to IDD recognition. [^3] Therefore, we expect:
<!-- para 32 -->
$H_{2}$: The positive effect of IDD recognition on advertising spending is weaker for firms with CEO duality.
<!-- para 34 -->
The impact of IDD recognition on advertising spending is likely to be stronger for younger firms because top management in these firms is likely to be more attentive to capital market expectations.
Younger firms often face greater resource constraints than older firms do, making them more reliant on external resources for growth and stability (Kotha, Zheng, and George 2011).
Indeed, younger firms have a greater need to signal to capital markets their ability to meet growth expectations (see Alimov and Mikkelson 2012; Li and Zhang 2015), due to relatively higher uncertainty surrounding their future performance in capital markets (Xiong and Bharadwaj 2011).
<!-- para 35 -->
The top management in younger firms may pay attention to IDD recognition, because it affects how securely they can translate their intellectual property into market-based results.
Importantly, younger firms are also more likely to respond to capital market expectations following IDD recognition, because they stand to gain more by leveraging trade secrets and converting them into visible market outcomes.
Indeed, research shows that investors respond more positively to younger firms beating revenue expectations (see for example, Zhao 2017; also see Zhang 2006).
Therefore, we expect:
<!-- para 36 -->
$H_{3}$: The positive effect of IDD recognition on advertising spending is stronger for younger firms.
<!-- para 38 -->
Firms pay close attention to the advertising spending of their peers because the effectiveness of their advertising spending is likely to depend on their peers' advertising spending (see Ozturan, Deleersnyder, and Ozsomer 2024).
For advertising spending to be effective, it must exceed a minimum threshold—defined as “the spending level necessary for a sales response” (Kim and McAlister 2011, p. 69).
This threshold rises when peer firms allocate more resources to advertising because competing messages reduce consumer responsiveness to advertising (Danaher 1995).
High peer advertising spending creates interference effects that diminish the advertising elasticity of the focal firm, weakening its ability to generate brand recall and conversion (Danaher, Bonfrer, and Dhar 2008).
In fact, recent studies estimate that such interference can lower the effectiveness of traditional media advertising by nearly 50%, and the reduction effect is even greater in online settings (Waisman, Nair, and Carrion 2025).
<!-- para 39 -->
The competitive sensitivity of advertising effectiveness, therefore, suggests that the degree to which top management pays attention to capital market expectations of growth is likely to be higher for firms in industries with higher peer advertising spending.
This is because the salience of advertising spending as an instrument to compete effectively in the marketplace to drive sales growth is significantly higher for firms operating in industries with higher peer advertising spending.
Indeed, according to ABV, managers pay closer attention to competitive signals that are salient and persistent in the external environment (Ocasio 1997).
Research shows that advertising spending by peer firms and disclosure of the same are competitive signals that are closely watched and followed by both top management and capital markets (see for example, Joshi and Hanssens 2010; Shi, Grewal and Sridhar 2021).
Therefore, we expect that firms in industries with higher peer advertising spending are more likely to increase their advertising spending in response to IDD recognition.
Formally:
<!-- para 40 -->
$H_{4}$: The positive effect of IDD recognition on advertising spending is stronger for firms in industries with higher peer advertising spending.

## methods
<!-- para 3 -->
To identify the effect of trade secret protection on advertising spending, we use IDD recognition by a state court as an exogenous shock.
Considering IDD recognition as an exogenous shock in our context rests on two assumptions.
First, senior managers should be aware of the precedent-setting case of IDD recognition, which becomes case law in that state.
Reassuringly, prior research shows that firms are likely to be aware of IDD recognition because it was covered in legal journals and local media (Li, Lin, and Zhang 2018).
Given that most public firms employ legal counsels who monitor trade secret rulings and that IDD-related cases have received substantial media attention, it is reasonable to assume that firms are informed about these judicial changes.
<!-- para 4 -->
Second, changes in court decisions regarding IDD recognition in a state are not influenced by individual firms.
Indeed, prior work shows that court rulings establishing IDD recognition depend on case-specific circumstances and judicial discretion and are thus largely independent of firm actions or lobbying efforts (see for example, Chen et al. 2022).
Therefore, considering the findings of prior work, IDD recognition can be viewed as an exogenous shock (also see Web Appendix A1 for a detailed discussion).
<!-- para 6 -->
To test the effect of IDD recognition on advertising spending of a firm, we draw on multiple datasets.
First, we collect data on advertising spending and firm financial metrics from COMPUSTAT.
Second, we use the Institutional Brokers' Estimate System (I/B/E/S) to obtain information about financial analysts' variables.
Third, we collect institutional ownership of publicly listed firms from Thomson Reuters.
Fourth, to obtain information about firm age, we use the date on which a firm is founded from Capital IQ.
Finally, we obtain information about the senior executives of a firm from the BoardEx database.
We exclude firms from the finance, insurance, and utilities industries because they are highly regulated by different financial reporting formats (see McAlister et al. 2016).
<!-- para 7 -->
Consistent with prior research, we construct our sample where the treated group comprises firms headquartered in states that do not recognize the IDD at the beginning of the sample period but recognize it later (e.g., Li, Lin, and Zhang 2018).
The control group comprises firms headquartered in states that never recognized IDD during the sample period.
<!-- para 8 -->
To identify specific states that recognize IDD, we build on the findings of Klasa et al. (2018) and identify precedent-setting cases that lead to IDD recognition in a state.
<!-- para 9 -->
To collect information about the state in which a firm is headquartered in a given year, we use the 10-K Headers Database constructed by Bill McDonald, which provides historical information about the states in which firms are headquartered each year. [^4] As the Securities and Exchange Commission (SEC) amended the disclosure rule for advertising spending in 1994, the starting point for our sample period is the calendar year 1995 (see Heitzman, Wasley, and Zimmerman 2010).
In addition, to allow for variation before the recognition of IDD, we consider cases that provide at least three years of pretreatment periods for the treated firms.
Using this approach, the treatment group consists of firms headquartered in Georgia (GA), Kansas (KS), Missouri (MO), Ohio (OH), and Utah (UT) (please see Web Appendix A2 for details).
As the state courts in GA and OH rejected IDD in 2013 and 2008 respectively, we include data until 2018 to allow for variation after IDD rejections.
<!-- para 10 -->
Recent research on staggered difference-in-differences (DiD) notes that always-treated units are a potential source of bias in identifying the treatment effect (Baker, Larcker, and Wang 2022).
The staggered DiD includes the unit and year fixed effects.
In this context, the DiD estimate is a variance-weighted average of the constituent $2 \times 2$ DiD estimates in the data, and one of the comparisons includes always-treated units as effective controls. [^5] As always-treated units reflect the treatment effect in the outcomes, the treatment effect estimated from the staggered DiD in this case may potentially introduce a negative weight and bias the estimate (Baker, Larcker, and Wang 2022).
To address this concern, we exclude firms headquartered in states that recognized IDD before 1995.
In addition, we do not include firms which only have observations after IDD was recognized in the state in which they are headquartered.
Using this approach, our sample consists of firms headquartered in states that recognized IDD during our sampling period as treatment groups.
The control group includes firms headquartered in states that never recognized IDD during our sampling period.
The final sample consists of 7,113 firm-year observations of 925 firms from 1995-2018 (i.e., 1,152 in the treatment group; 5,961 in the control group; see Web Appendix A2 for details). [^6]
<!-- para 12 -->
***Advertising spending.*** The dependent variable is advertising spending disclosed by a firm in its 10-K reports.
To limit the impact of extreme observations, we take the natural log of advertising spending (Lu et al. 2022). [^7]
<!-- para 13 -->
***The recognition of IDD.*** We measure IDD recognition as a binary variable, which equals 1 if the firm is headquartered in a state that has recognized the IDD and 0 if the firm is headquartered in a state that does not recognize the IDD in year t (Li, Lin, and Zhang 2018).
In addition, we follow Chen et al. (2022) and code IDD recognition as 0 for GA and OH from 2013 and 2008 because these states subsequently rejected IDD in those years respectively.
<!-- para 14 -->
***CEO duality.*** We measure CEO duality as a binary variable that is equal to 1 if the CEO of a firm also serves as the chairperson of the board and 0 otherwise (Ebbes, Germann, and Grewal 2025; Srinivasan, Wuyts, and Mallapragada 2018; Tuggle et al. 2010).
<!-- para 15 -->
***Firm age.*** We measure firm age as the natural log of the years since a firm was founded (Bhattacharya, Morgan, and Rego 2022; Srinivasan 2006).
<!-- para 16 -->
***Peer advertising spending.*** We measure peer advertising spending as the natural log of the average advertising spending of a firm’s industry peers in the four-digit North American Industry Classification System (NAICS4) industry.
<!-- para 17 -->
***Control variables.*** To identify the impact of IDD on the advertising spending of a firm, we incorporate a comprehensive set of firm-, industry-, and state-level covariates.
Specifically, firm-level control variables include financial factors that are likely to have an impact on the advertising spending of a firm, such as sales, financial leverage, and return on assets (ROA).
In addition, we include firm-level institutional variables that could also have an impact on advertising spending, i.e., the number of marketing executives in the top management of the firm, the size of the top management team, analyst forecast dispersion, analyst following, and institutional ownership of firms' outstanding shares.
We also include industry-level controls, i.e., industry competitive intensity and industry sales growth, which are known to drive firm advertising spending.
These industry-level variables are based on the NAICS4 industry classification.
Furthermore, to account for state-level factors that could drive advertising spending, we include state GDP growth and unemployment rate.
Web Appendix A3 outlines the control variables, measures, data sources, and the supporting literature, and Web Appendix A5 provides the rationale for including control variables.
To mitigate the impact of outliers, all continuous variables are winsorized at 1% and 99%.
Web Appendix A6 presents the summary statistics and correlations between the variables.
<!-- para 19 -->
To identify the effect of IDD recognition on firm advertising spending, we follow prior studies in this domain and use a staggered DiD model (e.g., Chen et al. 2022; Kim et al. 2021).
Note that the timing of IDD recognition by the state courts in the treated groups varies within the year of IDD recognition (e.g., some recognized IDD in the middle of the year and others almost at the end of the year).
In addition, firms may not be able to change their behaviors immediately following IDD recognition and may need time to respond to it (Seo and Somaya 2022).
As such, to identify the effect of IDD recognition on advertising spending, we lag the recognition of IDD and covariates and estimate the following model: (1)
<!-- para 20 -->
$$ \mathrm{Advertising}_{\mathrm{i,j,g,t}}=\beta_{1}\mathrm{IDD}_{\mathrm{g,t-1}}+\Delta^{\prime}\mathbf{X}_{\mathrm{i,j,g,t-1}}+\mu_{\mathrm{i}}+\tau_{\mathrm{t}}+\varepsilon_{\mathrm{i,j,g,t}} $$
<!-- para 21 -->
Advertising $_{i,j,g,t}$ = natural log of advertising spending for firm i in industry j that is headquartered at state g during year t,
<!-- para 22 -->
$IDD_{g,t-1}$ = 1 if IDD recognition is in effect in state g where firm i is headquartered in year t-1 and 0 otherwise,
<!-- para 23 -->
$\Delta'X_{i,j,g,t-1}$ = firm, industry, and state-level controls,
<!-- para 24 -->
$\mu_{i}$ = firm fixed effects,
<!-- para 25 -->
$\tau_{t}$ = year fixed effects,
<!-- para 26 -->
$\varepsilon_{i,j,g,t}$ = the random error term.
<!-- para 27 -->
The firm and year fixed effects in Equation 1 subsume the main effects of the treated firms and the posttreatment period such that $\beta_{1}$ captures the treatment effect, i.e., the extent to which firms headquartered in the states with IDD recognition change their advertising spending (Baker, Larcker, and Wang 2022).
Because of its flexibility, this approach is widely applied in empirical contexts similar to the state-wide recognition of IDD, such as the staggered adoption of descriptive analytics by online retailers (Berman and Israeli 2022) and the staggered IPOs of brokerage firms (Bradshaw et al. 2023).
<!-- para 28 -->
Since we use advertising spending data disclosed in firm 10-K reports, the availability of this data depends on the disclosure of this information by publicly listed firms.
This reliance on a firm’s disclosure practices can lead to a potential selection bias because there could be a systematic difference between firms that disclose their advertising spending and those that do not (Heitzman, Wasley, and Zimmerman 2010).
To address this potential selection bias, we use a two-stage approach where in the first stage, we use a probit model to outline factors that are likely to drive the firm’s disclosure of advertising spending: (2) $\Pr(AD_{i,j,g,t}=1)$
<!-- para 29 -->
$=\Phi(\alpha_0 + \alpha_1 \text{Sector Peer Disclosure}_{i,j,g,t-1} + \alpha_2 \text{Auditor Peer Disclosure}_{i,j,g,t-1} + \Phi' \mathbf{X}_{i,j,g,t-1} + \varphi_t)$
<!-- para 30 -->
$AD_{i,j,g,t}$
<!-- para 31 -->
= disclosure of advertising spending by firm i in industry j that is headquartered at state g in year t, Sector Peer Disclosure $_{i,j,g,t-1}$ = weighted proportion of sector peers, other than industry peers, that disclose advertising spending in year t-1, Auditor Peer Disclosure $_{i,j,g,t-1}$ = weighted proportion of auditor peers, other than industry peers, that disclose advertising spending, and $\varphi_{t}$= year fixed effects.
<!-- para 32 -->
In this model, we use the weighted proportion of the disclosures of advertising spending by the firm’s sector and auditor peers as exclusion restrictions. [^8] We use the nature of the NAICS industry classification system to identify sector peers that belong to the same sector as the focal firm but exclude industry peers.
In addition, we use auditor information to identify auditor peers that share the same auditor with the focal firm but exclude industry peers.
Web Appendix A7 outlines the details of the exclusion restrictions and the rationale.
We estimate Equation 2, generate the Inverse Mills Ratio (IMR), and include it as an additional covariate in the second stage model (i.e., Equation 1) to account for the potential selection bias.
We cluster the standard errors at the firm level, and to account for the fact that the IMR is an estimated covariate, we use 200 bootstrapping replications to generate the standard errors.
<!-- para 33 -->
To test the moderating effect hypotheses $H_{2}-H_{4}$, we incorporate the interaction terms, and estimate the following model: (3)
<!-- para 34 -->
$$ \begin{aligned}Advertising_{i,j,g,t}&=\beta_{1}IDD_{g,t-1}+\beta_{2}IDD_{g,t-1}\times CEO Duality_{i,j,g,t-1}\\&+\beta_{3}IDD_{g,t-1}\times Firm Age_{i,j,g,t-1}+\beta_{4}IDD_{g,t-1}\times Peer Adv Spending_{i,j,g,t-1}\\&+\beta_{5}CEO Duality_{i,j,g,t-1}+\beta_{6}Firm Age_{i,j,g,t-1}+\beta_{7}Peer Adv Spending_{i,j,g,t-1}\\&+\beta_{8}IMR_{i,j,g,t}+\Delta^{\prime}X_{i,j,g,t-1}+\mu_{i}+\tau_{t}+\varepsilon_{i,j,g,t}.\\ \end{aligned} $$

The validity of the DiD approach rests on the parallel trends assumption that the advertising spending of the treated and control firms follows parallel trends in the absence of IDD recognition.
While it is critical to assess whether the parallel trends assumption is likely to be satisfied, there is no formal test for this assumption.
Therefore, we examine whether the pretreatment trends for the treated and control firms are significantly different (Angrist and Pischke 2009).
Specifically, we incorporate the indicators of three, two, and one year before IDD recognition and those of one, two, and three or more years after IDD recognition to estimate the following model (see Hoffmann et al. 2024; Klasa et al. 2018): (4)

$$ \begin{aligned}Advertising_{i,j,g,t}&=\beta_{11}IDD^{Recognition-3}_{g,t}+\beta_{12}IDD^{Recognition-2}_{g,t}+\beta_{13}IDD^{Recognition-1}_{g,t}\\&\quad+\beta_{14}IDD^{Recognition0}_{g,t}+\beta_{15}IDD^{Recognition+1}_{g,t}+\beta_{16}IDD^{Recognition+2}_{g,t}\\&\quad+\beta_{17}IDD^{Recognition3+}_{g,t}+\Delta^{\prime}X_{i,j,g,t-1}+\mu_{i}+\tau_{t}+\varepsilon_{i,j,g,t}\end{aligned} $$

<!-- para 10 -->

<!-- para 11 -->
$IDD^{Recognition-1}_{g,t} = 1$ if the firm is headquartered in the state g, one year before IDD recognition,
<!-- para 12 -->

<!-- para 13 -->

<!-- para 14 -->

<!-- para 15 -->

Results also show that the coefficients of the three terms of IDD in the posttreatment periods are positive and significant (i.e., $\beta_{15} = .108$, $p = .044$; $\beta_{16} = .117$, $p = .039$; $\beta_{17} = .160$, $p = .000$).

Counterfactual analysis: IDD rejection.
In the treated groups of our sample, the state courts of GA and OH first recognized IDD in 1998 and 2000 and then rejected it later in 2013 and 2008.
This means that the trade secret protection provided by IDD is no longer available for firms in GA and OH after IDD rejection.
These rulings that reject IDD by state courts in GA and OH offer a natural empirical setting for counterfactual analysis.
If IDD recognition increases advertising spending by firms headquartered in GA and OH, IDD rejection is expected to have the opposite effect on advertising spending by those firms.
To test this expectation, we focus on firms headquartered in the states that recognized IDD (i.e., GA, KS, MO, OH, and UT) in the post-IDD recognition period.
Specifically, we use firms headquartered in GA and OH as the treated groups and those in KS, MO, and UT as the control groups and estimate the following model: (5)
<!-- para 25 -->

IDD Rejection $_{g,t-1}$ = 1 if IDD rejection is in effect in the state g where firm i is headquartered at year t-1 and 0 otherwise,
<!-- para 27 -->

Augmented diff-in-diff.
The augmented difference-in-differences (ADiD) estimation proposed by Li and Van den Bulte (2023) allows for a more flexible estimation approach regarding the parallel trends assumption.
ADiD better accounts for heterogeneity between the treated and control groups than DiD and requires a less stringent parallel trends assumption.
Specifically, the ADiD controls for not only the intercept but also the slope between the treated groups and control groups in the pre-treatment period by calculating the counterfactuals in the posttreatment period of the treated firms.
Therefore, following Li and Van den Bulte (2023), we use the pretreatment period data and estimate the intercept and slope to calculate the counterfactuals in the posttreatment period of the treated firms and estimate the following model: (6)
<!-- para 33 -->
$$ \mathrm{Advertising}_{\mathrm{lt}}=\delta_{1}+\delta_{2}\mathrm{Advertising}_{\mathrm{co},\mathrm{t}}+\varepsilon_{\mathrm{lt}} $$
<!-- para 34 -->
Advertising $_{lt}$ = natural log of advertising spending for treated firms in year t in the pretreatment period and Advertising $_{co,t}$ = the average of natural log of advertising spending for control firms in year t in the pretreatment period.
<!-- para 35 -->
After obtaining $\hat{\delta}_1$ and $\hat{\delta}_2$, we calculate the counterfactuals (i.e., $\widehat{\text{Advertising}_{1t, ADiD}} = \hat{\delta}_1 + \hat{\delta}_2 \text{Advertising}_{co,t}$).
Then, we use the counterfactuals for the control groups and estimate the models.
As shown in Model 3 of Table 2, we continue to find support for the positive effect of IDD recognition on advertising spending (see Table 2).

Alternative control groups and treated groups.
Our sample incorporates 28 states as control groups that never recognized IDD during the sample period.
Interestingly, California (CA) accounts for almost 55% of the observations in the control groups (see Web Appendix A2).
Therefore, we examine the effect of dropping CA in the construction of the control groups.
As the results of Model 4 in Table 2 show, we find that our substantive conclusions are robust to excluding CA from the control group (see Table 2).
<!-- para 37 -->
The focal model examines the impact of IDD recognition on advertising spending in five treated states.
A potential critique is that our conclusions are driven by variations in a particular state.
Therefore, we construct alternative treated groups that exclude one treated state at a time from our sample and re-estimate the models using five different samples (see Guernsey, John, and Litov 2022).
Consistent with our expectations, we continue to find that IDD recognition has a positive effect on advertising spending (see Web Appendix A10).
<!-- para 38 -->
Placebo tests.
A potential alternative explanation for the observed positive effect of IDD recognition on advertising spending could be that it reflects a general increase in overall operating costs following IDD recognition.
Because IDD recognition offers trade secret protection, managers may be optimistic that the operating environment is likely to be more favorable to them following IDD recognition.
As such, the positive impact of IDD recognition on advertising spending is likely to reflect an overall increase in firm spending.
In addition, it is reasonable to argue that a legal change is likely to impose additional costs for firms to adapt to a new business environment.
For example, IDD recognition restricts labor mobility and may increase the potential costs for firms to hire employees.
<!-- para 39 -->
Therefore, we conduct an additional analysis using alternative dependent variables that reflect general business expenses.
Specifically, we use the natural log of selling, general, and administrative expenses (SG&A) after deducting advertising spending and the natural log of the cost of goods sold (COGS) as the alternative dependent variables.
As shown in Model 5 & 6 of Table 2, we do not find empirical support for the alternative argument that IDD recognition is likely to lead to a general increase in business expenses (i.e., the effect of IDD recognition on SG&A = .002, p = .885; on COGS = .017, p = .238).
<!-- para 40 -->
In addition, we conduct an additional placebo test to see if our results are driven by unobservable state level factors other than IDD recognition.
To this end, we construct placebo treatment groups by assigning fictitious IDD recognition years to some of the control groups and re-estimate the models.
If our results are driven by the actual implementation of IDD recognition, not unobservable state level factors, we should find the null effect of fictitious IDD recognition on advertising spending in placebo treatment groups.
Reassuringly, we find no empirical evidence for the effect of the placebo IDD recognition on advertising spending (please see Web Appendix A11 for details).
<!-- para 41 -->
Stacked diff-in-diff.
In examining the impact of treatments that are staggered across time (e.g., IDD recognition in our context), a potential alternative estimation strategy would be to employ a stacked DiD model (see Baker, Larcker, and Wang 2022; Guo and Liu 2023).
To this end, we construct an event specific sample with firms in one IDD recognition state as treated groups and those that never recognize IDD as control groups, which forms a cohort, and stack all cohorts to construct the stacked dataset.
The stacked DiD model incorporates the cohort-specific firm and year fixed effects and enables us to use relative time dummies, which allows for potentially cleaner comparison between the treated and control groups and alleviates the potential concern about the staggered treatment design (see Cengiz et al. 2019; Sun and Abraham 2021).
As Model 7 in Table 2 shows, we consistently find support for the positive effect of IDD recognition on advertising spending in the stacked DiD models (see Web Appendix A12 and Table A12 for more details).

Accounting for the CEO compensation structure.
As IDD recognition lowers employee mobility, it increases the cost of job loss, lowers rewards for risk-taking, and can lead managers to underinvest in risky projects (see Chen et al. 2022).

Accounting for sector-year fixed effects.
An alternative fixed effects specification for the proposed model is to include time-varying industry fixed effects, as compared to the traditional specification of firm and year fixed effects.
Incorporating these fixed effects allows the model to take into account the potential time-varying unobservable shocks specific to industries, which, in turn, could be correlated with firm advertising spending and related to IDD recognition.
However, incorporating time-varying industry dummies significantly raises the number of fixed effects that need to be estimated. [^10] Accordingly, we use fixed effects estimators instead of including dummies to efficiently handle high-dimensional fixed effects (Gormley and Matsa 2014; Guimaraes and Portugal 2010).
In addition, extant literature cautions researchers about incorporating too many fixed effects because it may cause the incidental parameters problem and remove potentially meaningful variation in the data (i.e., attenuation bias).
The smaller the number of observations per group for the fixed effects, the more pronounced attenuation bias would be (see Gormley and Matsa 2014).
In addition, the incidental parameters problem is a particularly important concern for the first stage probit model in our empirical context (see Fernández-Val 2009).
As such, to minimize the concern about the incidental parameters problem and secure meaningful variations in the model with high-dimensional fixed effects, we use sector-year fixed effects in Equation 1 and 3. [^11] As Table 4 shows, we find that our substantive conclusions remain largely unchanged if we adopt this alternative specification.
That said, it is important to note that the result of this alternative specification implies that there is an 11% increase in advertising spending following IDD recognition ( $\beta_{1} = .108$, p = .000), as compared to the focal analyses which conclude that IDD recognition leads to a 13.5% increase in advertising.
As such, we need to be cautious about interpreting the economic impact of IDD recognition as the effect size is attenuated by about 20% after accounting for time-varying industry factors.

## discussion
<!-- para 2 -->
Protecting trade secrets allows firms to invest in creating trade secrets and leveraging them (Andreicovici, Bormann, and Hombach 2025).
Yet, little is known about how firms adjust their advertising spending once changes in the legal environment provide stronger trade secret protection.
Against this background, the current study presents theoretical arguments and empirical results that offer four key implications for extant research.
<!-- para 3 -->
First, the robust and economically significant impact of IDD recognition on advertising spending of a firm (i.e., estimated to be 13.5%) suggests that research on the consequences of trade secret protection laws and legislation, such as IDD and UTSA, should examine not only whether such laws protect proprietary information but also how they influence firms' propensity to leverage their trade secrets.
By directly examining the impact of IDD recognition on advertising spending, a critical marketing action, the study also complements recent work that examines the impact of legal protections such as trademark rights on firm actions to increase their efforts to safeguard their intangible assets by filing legal applications (Krasnikov and Jayachandran 2022).
<!-- para 4 -->
Second, by proposing managerial attention towards leveraging trade secrets due to higher expectations of capital markets as the underlying mechanism, we complement extant work that predominantly focuses on the impact of IDD recognition on employee mobility as the underlying mechanism (see Table 1).
The focus on the managerial attention mechanism, in turn, allows us to provide a nuanced view about the implications of trade secrets protection.
This is important because extant statutory language and policy discourse on trade secret protection focus primarily on deterring misappropriation by competitors (Boot and Vladimirov 2025).
While this protective function remains significant, the original intent of trade secret laws was to facilitate leveraging proprietary knowledge rather than serve solely as anti-theft mechanisms.
Indeed, growing litigation suggests that many firms use these laws defensively (Mcelroy and Fisher 2024).
Our findings suggest that stronger trade secret protection can redirect managerial attention toward market-oriented uses of proprietary knowledge, encouraging investment in advertising and brand building to drive growth.
<!-- para 5 -->
Given the salience of the proposed mechanism, we conduct two additional empirical tests that focus on two key elements of the proposed mechanism, top management attention towards a key marketing asset, i.e., brands, and capital market expectations of growth.
To directly examine managers' attention towards marketing actions, we draw on recent research and measure the degree to which managers discuss a key marketing asset, i.e., their brands in 10-K reports (see Boustanifar and Kang 2025 and Web Appendix A16 & A17).
Consistent with the proposed mechanism, we find that IDD recognition significantly increases (= .142, p=.001) the degree to which managers discuss their brands in their 10-K reports (please see Model 1 in Table 5).
In addition, our findings may suggest that the predominant underlying mechanism in the prior literature (i.e., reduced employee mobility) and our proposed mechanism (i.e., managerial attention) may be complementary instead of substitutive.
Specifically, under reduced mobility, top marketing managers are likely to focus on brand-building activities in the long term.
Building on our findings, future research may consider exploring this effect as a valuable avenue for follow-up studies.

We also examine the capital market expectations of growth, by estimating the impact of IDD recognition on analysts' revenue forecasts (see Web Appendix A16).
We focus on analysts' revenue forecasts because they are direct measures of financial market expectations of growth, exhibit lower prediction errors, are viewed as primary drivers of earnings and earnings growth, and are less vulnerable to poor disclosure practices (Bradshaw, Lee, and Peterson 2016). [^12] As shown in Model 2 of Table 5, IDD recognition leads to a significant increase in revenue forecasts by financial analysts (= .024, p = .014).
Importantly, by documenting the positive effect of financial market expectations on advertising spending, we complement extant work that predominantly outlines the negative impact of financial market expectations on marketing investments (e.g., Martin and Kushwaha 2024).
These results suggest that stronger legal protection can align analyst expectations and managerial incentives, creating an institutional environment more conducive to marketing investment.
Because senior marketing managers are typically evaluated based on revenue growth (Huang, Marquardt, and Zhang 2023), these findings suggest that stronger trade secret protection may indirectly empower marketing managers to defend advertising budgets.
<!-- para 10 -->
Third, by adopting a direct focus on advertising spending, the current effort updates conclusions in extant empirical research.
Prior studies show that trade secret protection increases investments in physical assets but does not significantly affect spending on R&D (Jeffers 2024) or on aggregated intangible assets such as the sum of R&D, capital expenditures, and advertising spending (Klasa et al. 2018), or the sum of R&D and advertising spending (Gao, Wang, and Yin 2023).
Our analysis distinguishes advertising from these aggregated measures and shows that aggregating advertising with R&D or capital expenditures obscures an important managerial choice.
Advertising reflects a value appropriation-oriented investment (see Mizik and Jacobson 2003), that is, one through which firms convert proprietary knowledge into market-based outcomes.
Because advertising spending builds brand equity (see McAlister, Srinivasan, and Kim 2007), stronger trade secret protection appears to facilitate firm investment in brand-related assets that sustain market advantage.

As shown in Figure 2, Panel A, the moderating effect of CEO duality is economically significant as the positive effect of IDD recognition on advertising spending is almost double in magnitude for firms without CEO duality ( $b_{noCEODuality} = .265$) compared to firms with CEO duality ( $b_{withCEODuality} = .132$).
The significant difference in the magnitude becomes more salient given that almost 50% of firms in our sample have CEOs that are also chair of the board of directors, similar to the S&P 500 firms (The Conference Board 2021).
Interestingly, recent research finds that CEO duality is likely to be helpful for firms with board members with marketing experience to improve revenue growth (see Whitler, Krause and Lehmann 2018).
The results from the current study, therefore, suggest the need for a more balanced view to understand the effect of CEO duality on marketing actions and outcomes.
Indeed, recent research shows that duality enhances CEO control over the attention of board of directors and can mute critical evaluation of their decisions (Tuggle et al. 2024).

This suggests that the legal protection of trade secrets afforded by IDD is more salient for younger firms as it provides greater impetus for them to invest in advertising spending.
Because market-based assets are critical for growth and survival among young firms, stronger trade secret protection may provide them with a legal foundation to invest in visibility and credibility through advertising (Joshi and Hanssens 2010; Xiong and Bharadwaj 2011).
The moderating effect of firm age also informs policymakers of the positive effects of trade secret protection by IDD recognition on broader economic benefits via the potential job creation and growth of younger firms.
Indeed, while startups constitute only 10% of U.S. firms, they account for over 20% of job creation at the firm level (Haltiwanger et al. 2013).

By bringing to the fore the moderating effect of peer advertising spending levels on managers' responsiveness to IDD recognition, we add to the nascent body of work that examines the impact of economic conditions on firms' responsiveness to peer advertising spending (Ozturan, Deleersnyder, and Ozsomer 2024).
Future research, therefore, could build on our work to explore whether firm responsiveness to peer advertising decisions is likely to vary with the degree of trade secret protection afforded to the firm.

Performance Implications.
The primary goal of policymakers when establishing trade secret protection laws is to enhance the competitive advantage of firms (WIPO 2024).
In addition, from a managerial perspective, it is important to examine whether the advertising spending increases due to IDD recognition are associated with performance outcomes.
Therefore, we adapt the model outlined in Equation 1 to examine the impact of IDD recognition on sales.
As shown in Table 6, we find that IDD recognition has a significant positive effect on sales (= .042, p = .008) in Model 1.
Importantly, the effect of IDD recognition on sales is no longer significant (= .014, p = .349) in Model 2, which includes advertising spending as a covariate that has a positive impact on sales (= .188, p = .000).
Further, results of Model 3 show that the effect of IDD recognition is positive and significant only for firms that increase advertising following IDD recognition (= .014, p = .027). [^13] That is, the stronger trade secret protection afforded by IDD recognition is associated with higher sales only when firms invest more in advertising.
From a managerial perspective, these results suggest that while stronger protection of trade secrets provides firms with an opportunity for higher sales, to realize this opportunity they need to invest in advertising.
A direct managerial implication, therefore, is that firms need to pay greater attention towards evaluating the marketplace opportunities afforded by legal protections of trade secrets.

For policymakers, these results indicate that the benefits of trade secret protection materialize only when firms act on the new legal environment through spending on advertising.
Importantly, by identifying the impact of IDD recognition on sales, the results provide an alternative perspective to the view that legal restrictions on labor mobility such as IDD suppress innovation and regional growth (Marx, Strumsky, and Fleming 2009) and harm workers (Johnson, Lavetti, and Lipsitz 2025).
<!-- para 25 -->
State Perspective.
Given that IDD recognition is a state-level ruling, it is also important to assess whether it has any impact at the state level.
This is a potentially important consideration because both the Bureau of Labor Statistics and the Bureau of Economic Analysis consistently underscore the critical role of advertising in job creation and GDP growth in the U.S. economy.
Specifically, advertising expenditures contribute to 28.5 million jobs in the U.S. in 2020 (Association of National Advertisers 2022; IHS Markit 2021).
Accordingly, in post hoc analyses, we use state-level data to assess if the impact of IDD recognition on firm advertising and sales also translates into state-level advertising spending, sales growth, and growth in advertising-related jobs (see Web Appendix A19).
Consistent with the firm-level analyses, we find that IDD recognition in a state is positively associated with advertising spending (= 2.594, p = .000), sales (= 1.641, p = .000), and growth in advertising jobs (= .748, p = .000).
Therefore, from a public policy perspective, stronger trade secret protection by IDD recognition may offset the constraints of labor mobility restrictions through facilitating investment in advertising, economic growth, and job creation.
<!-- para 27 -->
The current study draws on the attention-based view of the firm to propose that the legal protection of trade secrets afforded by IDD recognition is likely to shift managerial attention to leverage their trade secrets, leading to higher advertising spending.
Empirical analyses not only provide robust support for the central thesis, but also bring to the fore the heterogeneity in the impact of IDD recognition on firm advertising spending.
The current study, therefore, seeks to serve as a starting point for future research to examine the impact of increases in trade secret protection on firms' marketing actions and their effectiveness.

<!-- para 37 -->
For IDD recognition to be an exogenous shock that has an impact on firm action, it is important that firms are aware of the precedent-setting cases that lead to IDD recognition in a state (Li, Lin, and Zhang 2018).
A precedent-setting case in a state becomes case law and courts in that state subsequently follow this ruling on the applicability of IDD (Klasa et al. 2018).
In this regard, Li, Lin, and Zhang (2018) find that all the precedent-setting cases pertaining to IDD were covered in the legal journals and the corresponding state courts cited the precedent-setting case within a year.
Since most public firms have legal counsels that are responsible for legal matters related to employees, it is reasonable to expect that firms closely track and monitor the amendment of trade secret laws and related cases.
In addition, IDD-related cases received media coverage and gain sufficient public awareness (e.g., PepsiCo v.
Redmond 1995; Procter & Gamble Co. v.
Stoneham 2000).
Consistent with these expectations, recent research shows that firms and employees are aware of the precedent-setting cases associated with IDD.
For example, Chen et al. (2022) find that CEO mobility declines and the likelihood of hiring an insider CEO through internal promotion increases after IDD recognition.
Therefore, it is reasonable to expect that firms have sufficient awareness of the precedent-setting cases that recognize the IDD in the states where they are headquartered.
<!-- para 38 -->
The second identifying assumption is that changes in the views of the state courts on the IDD are unlikely to be influenced by firm decisions.
IDD is a judicial decision that focuses on the protection of trade secrets by restricting employees' ability to join a rival firm.
Unlike state laws, judicial decisions in state courts are based on the merits of specific cases and are made by judges who are largely independent of lobbying efforts by firms (see Li, Lin, and Zhang 2018).
In addition, in a state court, a new precedent for the IDD applicability is determined by the idiosyncrasy of the particular case related to the protection of trade secrets and the disposition of the justices, and the court's decision on the IDD is unlikely to be anticipated by firms (Klasa et al. 2018; Qiu and Wang 2018).
Court rulings of individual precedent-setting cases, therefore, are unlike other approaches to establishing a state legislation, which may be influenced by lobbying by special interest groups and political pressure brought on by certain economic situations (Chen, Gao, and Ma 2021).
As such, rulings that recognize IDD in a state are largely exogenous to firm decisions (see Nguyen, Pham, and Qiu 2023).
