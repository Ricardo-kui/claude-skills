# mukherjee_et_al_2021_hiding_in_the_herd_the_pr 句子库存

## introduction
<!-- para 2 -->
Automobile recalls are occurring at record-breaking rates (Spector 2016); but to the dismay of consumers and regulators, recalls seem to be announced after inexplicable delays.
Toyota's unintended acceleration recall and General Motors' (GM) ignition switch recall are two noteworthy examples, which were associated with 37 and 124 deaths, respectively (Healey 2010, Korosec 2015).
In both cases, consumers' lives were put at risk while firms hesitated to announce a recall, even after they were aware of the serious product defects (Greimel 2014).
This study offers one potential explanation for recall delays: recall clustering.
We define recall clusters as a collection of recalls within close temporal proximity in which a leading recall (the first recall in a cluster) by one firm excites following recalls (subsequent recalls in a cluster) by competitor firms.
Research that examines causes of recalls and treats recalls as the dependent variable (Haunschild and Rhee 2004, Thirumalai and Sinha 2011, Shah et al. 2017) presumes product recalls to be independent across firms.
That is, although there may be an underlying cause to the problem, such as inadequate learning from past mistakes (Haunschild and Rhee 2004), investments in risky innovation (Thirumalai and Sinha 2011), or overworking employees (Shah et al. 2017), recalls by one firm are assumed to be independent of recalls by competitors.
<!-- para 3 -->
Anecdotally, however, auto firms appear to deviate from this assumption.
The popular press hints at the existence of recall clusters using the term “recall tidal waves” and noting that when an auto recall is announced by one firm, other firms seem to quickly follow suit regardless of whether the recalls are related to similar defects (Stephens 2014).
For instance, on August 21, 2017, Ford announced a recall due to leaky fuel tank valves, which was quickly followed in the subsequent days by a fuel tank recall from Honda and an oil hose recall from Chrysler (NHTSA 2020, Schmitz 2017).
Similar groupings of recall announcements by competitors in close temporal proximity have been covered extensively by the popular press (JD Power 2018, Hirsch 2014).
This suggests that recall announcements may not be triggered solely by individual firms’ product quality defect awareness but may also be influenced by competitor recalls, a phenomenon that, to our knowledge, no prior research has investigated.
If true, recall clusters would likely form.
More specifically, managers may consciously or unconsciously delay announcing recalls in order to cluster them behind a competitor’s recall or to hide in the “herd” (Scharfstein and Stein 1990).
Herding behavior is exhibited when firms cluster poor performance in groups, also known as herds (Scharfstein and Stein 1990).
Herding theory was developed by accounting scholars to explain the temporal clustering of various accounting phenomena (Tse and Tucker 2010); but few empirical studies have demonstrated herding behavior in an operational setting, and none to our knowledge have associated herding behavior with recall announcements.
Demonstrating evidence of herding behavior and the resulting formation of recall clusters could remove a layer of opaqueness in recall delays for regulators and consumers, resulting in more timely recall decisions.
As such, the first research question in this study is the following: Do firms cluster recalls?
<!-- para 4 -->
We also explore an important consequence of recall clusters by examining whether and how the stock market penalizes firms differently depending on a recall's position within a cluster.
If, for example, leading recalls result in a larger market penalty than following recalls, that may offer at least one reason why firms delay recall announcements until a competitor announces one.
Anecdotal evidence indicates that the penalty firms realize after announcing recalls may not be uniform and could depend upon the timing of the recall in relation to their competitors.
For instance, Chrysler, GM, and Honda all issued recalls within a few days of each other during October 2015 (NHTSA 2020), yet the stock market's reactions to those recalls appeared to vary significantly.
Chrysler, the first to initiate a recall in this cluster, realized the largest stock market penalty, followed by GM and Honda, respectively (Bloomberg 2016).
Attribution theory may help explain such behavior as it supports the contention that a firm may be attributed more blame for uniquely negative outcomes (Kelley and Michela 1980).
As such, we build on attribution theory to answer the second research question: Does the stock market penalty attributed to a recall depend upon its position within a cluster?
<!-- para 5 -->
To answer our research questions, we develop hypotheses based on analytical results derived from a dynamic game theoretic model.
In this model, we use attribution theory as the underlying mechanism to develop a set of results related to the stock market penalty faced by firms issuing leading and following recalls.
These results serve as the foundation for a dynamic game, where we establish insights regarding firm recall strategies including the optimality of clustering following recalls after a competitor initiates a leading recall.
We then examine support for our hypotheses related to stock market penalties and recall clustering using empirical data on all auto recalls announced in the United States over a 48-year period.
<!-- para 6 -->
In our clustering empirical tests, we leverage a self-excited Hawkes point process model.
Self-excited point process models are well suited for studies such as ours that examine self-excitation of events across time (Hawkes 1971a, b; Xu et al. 2014).
We estimate our Hawkes process model using an expectation-maximization algorithm to categorize auto recalls into leading recalls, following recalls, or neither.
We find robust evidence that a leading recall by one firm excites following recalls by other firms.
Of the 3,117 recalls across the six major U.S. auto firms, 2,273 (73%) exist in a cluster, forming 266 clusters across the 48-year period from 1966–2013.
On average, a cluster forms after a 16-day gap in which no recalls are announced, persist for 34 days, and consist of a leading recall and 7.6 following recalls.
We conduct four clustering robustness checks that lend support to our findings.
<!-- para 7 -->
To answer our second research question, we use an event study to explore the stock market impact of a recall announcement.
We find that leading recalls are associated with as high as a 67% larger stock market penalty than following recalls, revealing a significant financial incentive for firms to delay announcing a recall until a competitor does.
We also find that the stock market penalty for a following recall increases as the time elapsed since the leading recall increases.
Further, our analysis indicates that the stock market penalty for a leading recall increases with each additional day that elapses after the end of the previous cluster.
Collectively, these results support our contention that the market attributes higher blame and thus penalizes a firm more heavily when their recall is viewed as being more unique compared with competitor recalls.
We also report the results of four robustness checks related to the event study that support our findings.
<!-- para 8 -->
This research makes two important contributions to theory and offers one key practical implication.
First, we contribute to the literature on herding theory (Scharfstein and Stein 1990) in the context of product recall timing (Hora et al. 2011, Ball et al. 2018, Wowak et al. 2020) by finding evidence of recall clusters, suggesting that firms may herd recall announcements to minimize stock market penalties.
We also contribute to the literature that has examined how the impact of a recall on a firm's financial performance varies depending on contextual factors, such as firm reputation (Rhee and Haunschild 2006) or recall strategy (Chen et al. 2009).
Specifically, we extend attribution theory (Kelley and Michela 1980) to an operational setting by finding that the stock market consequences experienced by firms due to recalls vary depending on when they announce recalls relative to their competitors.
The most prescient practical implication emerging from our study is the need for regulators to take proactive steps to discourage the formation of recall clusters.
Consequently, we propose a cost-neutral policy recommendation for the National Highway Traffic and Safety Administration (NHTSA), which may improve the speed and transparency of recall decision making and consequently limit recall clustering.
<!-- para 10 -->
To obtain a comprehensive understanding of the recall decision-making process and the timing of recalls in the auto industry, we interviewed three senior executives at one of the big three U.S. automakers (GM, Ford, and Chrysler).
The first interviewee was a vice chairman of the firm who reported directly to the chief executive officer; the second was a vice president of product reliability who was personally responsible for overseeing dozens of auto recalls for more than a decade; and the third was an assistant general counsel who litigated numerous auto recalls for the firm over a 20-year period.
<!-- para 11 -->
These interviews revealed that auto manufacturers establish product quality analysis teams responsible for continuously monitoring quality signals using multiple internal and external data sources, such as internal product failure analyses from manufacturing or research and development (R&D), defect reports from customers and dealers, and competitor defect and recall information reported in several data sets on the NHTSA's website.
The objective of these teams is to monitor potential product quality issues and alert senior management when recalls need to be considered.
When it is determined that a recall may be warranted, a recall decision-making meeting is convened, comprised of senior management from various functions, such as manufacturing, quality, R&D, and legal.
The purpose of these meetings is to make an official recall recommendation, which is submitted to the individual with recall decision-making authority at the firm, usually the chief executive of the division or the vice president of quality.
Two important aspects of this process relevant to our study are (1) firms maintain a pool of product quality issues that are under recall consideration and (2) firms scan their internal and external environment for new quality issues arising across the industry and for new signals related to ongoing product quality issues under recall consideration.
In other words, firms are constantly trying to determine if product quality issues are "recall-worthy" or if they should remain in the "wait-and-see" category.
This process described to us is consistent with popular press articles that detail how recall decisions are made at auto firms (Golson 2014).
We build upon this anecdotal recall decision-making understanding in the next section as we develop a theoretical model that motivates our recall clustering and stock market hypotheses.

## theory
<!-- para 2 -->
Consider two firms, labeled 1 and 2, that sell similar products and operate in the same market.
Products produced by both firms have quality problems; in each time period t, firms face the following choices: (1) leave the product on the market and incur a continuous market cost that includes product failure and warranty claims (Liu et al. 2016), lost sales (Berman 1999), and regulatory fines (Hirsch 2014) or (2) recall the product and face a stock market penalty (Davidson and Worrell 1992).
We denote the continuous market cost associated with leaving the product on the market in period t as $C_t^i$, $i = 1, 2$ and $t = 1, 2, \ldots, T$.
Here T could be thought of as the maximum number of time periods after which both firms might find it in their best interest to no longer keep the product on the market.
Consistent with practice, we assume that ex ante, a firm may not be able to estimate the potential costs associated with leaving the product on the market with certainty.
The probability density function and the cumulative distribution function of $C_t^i$ are given by $f_t^i$ and $F_t^i$, respectively.
We also assume that the continuous market cost stochastically increases over time (i.e., $C_{t+1}^i$ stochastically dominates $C_t^i$ in the first-order sense).
This assumption is reasonable because the negative impact of waiting to recall, in the form of product failure and warranty claims, is likely to increase over time.
<!-- para 3 -->
At the beginning of period t and prior to making the decision to recall or to leave the product on the market, each firm privately observes its own continuous market cost.
Before presenting results regarding how the realized continuous market cost influences the optimal recall strategy, we discuss details of the stock market penalty that a recalling firm faces.
One frequently used measure to capture the stock market penalty is cumulative abnormal returns (CAR), defined as a firm's abnormal market returns over a period of time relative to the predicted market returns over the same period of time.
We model the stock market return for firm i in the absence of any recalls as follows:
<!-- para 4 -->
$$ \frac{d S_{t}^{i}}{S_{t}^{i}}=\mu_{i}d t+\sigma_{i}d B(t),i=1,2, $$
<!-- para 5 -->
where $S_t^i$ is the stock price of firm $i$ at time $t$, $\mu_i$ and $\sigma_i$ are the drift (mean) and the volatility (standard deviation) of a Brownian motion, and $B(t)$ is a standard Brownian motion.
We use the index $l$ to denote the firm issuing the leading recall and index $f$ to denote the firm issuing the following recall.
After issuing a leading recall, firm $l$ experiences a one-time, negative “shock” to its stock price.
We capture the impact of the shock on the future stock market returns of firm $l$ as follows:
<!-- para 6 -->
$$ \frac{d S_{t}^{l}}{S_{t}^{l}}=\mu_{l}d t+\sigma_{l}d B(t)+[\varphi_{l}(\tau_{l})+\gamma_{l}(t-\tau_{l})]d t. \tag{1} $$
Equation (1) is applicable for time $t > \tau_l$, where $\tau_l$ is the time at which the leader announces a recall.
The term $\varphi_l(\tau_l) < 0$ captures the one-time negative shock to the stock market return of the firm issuing the leading recall.
According to attribution theory, when a subject's behavior is unique or differs from the behavior of others, external observers are more likely to attribute the underlying cause of the behavior to something specific about that entity and thus the entity is attributed more blame (Weiner 1985).
In contrast, when a subject's behavior is similar to those exhibited by others, external observers are more likely to attribute the underlying cause of the behavior to something that is not unique to that entity or to factors beyond its control and thus the entity is attributed less blame (Bettman and Weitz 1983).
This suggests that as firm $l$ waits longer to issue a leading recall, creating a larger gap in time from the end of the previous recall cluster, the market may place a higher attribution of blame on the firm issuing the leading recall.
We capture this by assuming that the magnitude of the one-time negative shock experienced by the leader increases with $\tau_l$.
The term $\gamma_l(t - \tau_l) > 0$ represents the correction to the market returns over time, with $\frac{\partial \gamma_l(t - \tau_l)}{\delta t} \geq 0$ (i.e., the stock market penalty attributed to the recall announcement decreases over time).
<!-- para 7 -->
Next, we discuss the dynamics related to the stock market returns of the follower.
Similar to the leader, the follower also experiences a one-time negative shock $\varphi_f(\tau_l, \tau_f) < 0$ because of the recall, where $\tau_f > \tau_l$ is the recall time of the follower.
However, we expect the market to attribute less blame to the following recall after the leading recall is announced, resulting in the follower experiencing a lower stock market penalty relative to the leader.
In general, we expect the benefit enjoyed by the follower, in terms of a reduced stock market penalty, to attenuate over time because the follower might be perceived as more unique the longer it has been since the leader issued a recall.
This implies that for any given $\tau_l$, $|\varphi_f(\tau_l, \tau_f)|$ increases with $\tau_f$.
Combining the two assumptions regarding $\varphi_f$, there exists a constant $K$ such that $|\varphi_f(\tau_l, \tau_f)| < |\varphi_l(\tau_l)|$ for all $\tau_f \leq \tau_l + K$.
This condition implies that the follower experiences a lower stock market penalty than the leader as long as the delay in announcing the following recall, relative to the leading recall, is not excessive, as captured by the threshold $K$.
Finally, consistent with our discussions for the leader, we assume that the follower's stock market returns also experience a correction $\gamma_f(t - \tau_f) > 0$ over time after issuing a recall, with $\frac{\partial \gamma_f(t - \tau_f)}{\delta t} \geq 0$.
Thus, we have Equation (2) that governs the stock market return of the follower after issuing a recall.
<!-- para 8 -->
$$ \frac{d S_{t}^{f}}{S_{t}^{f}}=\mu_{f}d t+\sigma_{f}d B(t)+[\varphi_{f}(\tau_{l},\tau_{f})+\gamma_{f}(t-\tau_{f})]d t \tag{2} $$
We also note that the stock market penalty of the follower depends on the recall time of both the leader and the follower.
More specifically, we assume that $\varphi_f(\tau_l, \tau_f)$ increases with $\tau_l$ for any given value of $\tau_f - \tau_l$.
For example, suppose that the follower issues a recall two days after a leading recall announcement is made.
Consider two scenarios where the leader announces a recall on day 5 or day 10, respectively.
The above condition would imply that the follower faces a lower stock market penalty under the first scenario, when it recalls on day 7, compared with the second scenario, when it recalls on day 12.
<!-- para 9 -->
We now present results regarding the stock market penalty faced by the leader and the follower, where we capture the stock market penalty in terms of the CARs over a fixed period of time after issuing the recall.
In interpreting the results for CAR, it is important to note that a CAR of larger magnitude corresponds to a greater stock market penalty.
Both the results presented below (i.e., Lemmas 1 and 2) follow from the stock market dynamics and the related assumptions that we have discussed so far in this section.
<!-- para 10 -->
Lemma 1 (Within-Cluster Effects) a.
The cumulative abnormal returns experienced by the leader over $\delta$ time periods after announcing a recall (i.e., $t = \tau_l$ to $\tau_l + \delta$) is larger in magnitude than the cumulative abnormal returns experienced by the follower over the same $\delta$ time periods after announcing a recall (i.e., $t = \tau_f$ to $\tau_f + \delta$) b.
For any given recall time of the leader $\tau_l$, the cumulative abnormal returns experienced by the follower after issuing a recall increases in magnitude as the time difference between the leading and following recalls (i.e., $\tau_f - \tau_l$) increases.
<!-- para 11 -->
The following result establishes how the stock market penalty experienced by the leader changes based on the time elapsed between the end of the previous cluster and the beginning of the next cluster (captured by $\tau_{l}$ in our model).
<!-- para 12 -->
Lemma 2 (Between-Cluster Effects).
The cumulative abnormal returns experienced by the leader after announcing a recall increases in magnitude with $\tau_{l}$.
<!-- para 13 -->
The above results offer qualitative insights regarding the stock market penalties experienced by the leader and the follower.
They serve as a foundation to establish key results regarding the optimal recall strategy of both firms, presented in the next section.
<!-- para 15 -->
As mentioned above, in each time period $t$, both firms face the following choices when neither firm has already issued a recall: (1) leave the product on the market and incur a continuous market cost or (2) recall the product and face a stock market penalty.
Lemma 1a demonstrates that the stock market penalty associated with a leading recall is larger than that of a following recall.
As such, each firm has an incentive to wait for the other firm to issue a recall first, but waiting entails leaving the product on the market and incurring an increasing continuous market cost.
Given these tradeoffs, the optimal recall strategy for each firm in period $t$ is determined by the solution to the following dynamic programming problem.
<!-- para 16 -->
$$ \begin{aligned}&V_{t}^{i}(R^{j}=0,e^{j}=0)\\=&\min\left\{P_{t}^{i}(l)\atop C_{t}^{i}+r_{t}^{j}E V_{t+1}^{i}(R^{j}=1,e^{j}=1)+(1-r_{t}^{j})\mathbb{E}V_{t+1}^{i}(R^{j}=0,e^{j}=0)\right\},\end{aligned} $$
<!-- para 17 -->
(3) where $V_t^i$ represents the cost-to-go for firm $i$ ( $i = 1, 2$), given that firm $j$ ( $\neq i$) has not yet recalled (captured by $R^j = 0$).
Consequently, the time elapsed since firm $j$ issued a recall, denoted by $e^j$, is also 0.
The first term on the right-hand side, $P_t^i(l)$, represents the stock market penalty faced by firm $i$ for issuing a leading recall in period $t$, assuming that firm $j$ has not yet issued a recall.
The second term is the cost incurred if firm $i$ does not issue a recall in period $t$: it is the sum of the continuous market cost in period $t$ and future expected cost-to-go from period $t+1$ onwards.
In the second term, $r_t^j$ is the probability that firm $j$ will issue a recall in period $t$.
In our analysis, we assume that when deciding whether to issue a recall, firm $i$ has already observed the continuous market cost $C_t^i$ it will incur if the product is left on the market.
However, firm $i$ does not observe firm $j$'s continuous market cost and, as a result, develops an estimate of the probability of firm $j$ recalling (i.e., $r_t^j$) based on the distribution of $C_t^j$, which we assume is common knowledge.
<!-- para 18 -->
Before presenting the equilibrium recall strategy of the firms, we first analyze the optimal timing for a firm to issue a following recall, given that the other firm has already announced a leading recall.
For illustration purposes, we assume that firm 1 makes the leading recall announcement at time $\tau_1$.
Then, in any given time period $t > \tau_1$, firm 2's decision regarding whether to issue a recall in that period is determined by the solution to the following problem.
<!-- para 19 -->
$$ \begin{align*}V_{t}^{2}(R^{1}&=1,e^{1}=t-\tau_{1})\\&=\min\left\{P_{t}^{2}(f)\atop C_{t}^{2}+E V_{t+1}^{2}(R^{1}=1,e^{1}=t+1-\tau_{1})\right\},\end{align*} $$
<!-- para 20 -->
where $P_{t}^{2}(f)$ is the stock market penalty faced by firm 2 for issuing a following recall in period t.
The result below establishes the optimal strategy for a firm evaluating a potential recall decision, knowing that the other firm in the market has already issued a leading recall.
<!-- para 21 -->
Proposition 1.
Suppose that firm 1 announces a leading recall at time $\tau_{1}$.
Then, a.
In every time period t such that $\tau_{1} < t < T$, it is better for firm 2 to issue a following recall than to keep the product on the market b.
Given that firm 1 issued a leading recall in period $\tau_{1}$, the optimal response for firm 2 is to issue a following recall right away in the next time period (i.e., $\tau_{1} + 1$).
<!-- para 22 -->
Having established the optimal recall timing for the follower, we now look at the equilibrium recall strategies for the two firms.
Earlier, we mentioned that each firm develops an estimate of the probability of the other firm recalling, based on the distribution of the continuous market cost.
This estimate forms the basis for each firm's recall decision in any given period; hence, the equilibrium recall strategy involves a set of beliefs about when the other firm might issue a recall, that is, it is a Bayes-Nash equilibrium.
The following result provides insights into the structure of the equilibrium recall strategy.
<!-- para 23 -->
Proposition 2.
The equilibrium recall strategy in every period is threshold-type.
More specifically, in any given period t, there exists thresholds $TC_t^i$, $i = 1, 2$ such that it is optimal for firm i to issue a recall if the continuous market cost $C_t^i$ is greater than $TC_t^i$ and keep the product on the market otherwise.
<!-- para 25 -->
To develop testable hypotheses, we combine insights from the relevant literature and the analytical results presented in Section 3.
They help us postulate both the existence of recall clusters and the differential stock market penalty experienced by firms contingent upon a recall's position within a cluster.
<!-- para 27 -->
Herding behavior occurs when firms mimic each other's actions (Devenow and Welch 1996).
This behavior is often exhibited when managers are concerned about their reputation and thus, imitate the actions of their peers to avoid being exposed as low performers.
Researchers often refer to this action as hiding in the herd or "following the herd" (Scharfstein and Stein 1990, pp. 465, Devenow and Welch 1996, pp. 608), which is when entities glean information by observing the actions of others and use that information to determine how to behave (Hirshleifer and Hong Teoh 2003).
For example, Chang et al. (1997) found that banks mimic the actions of their peers by opening branches close to other existing branches.
Trueman (1994) also found that analysts imitate the actions of others by releasing forecasts similar to those previously released by other analysts.
In the context of product recalls, firms may extract information from a competitor's recall announcement to inform their recall decision-making process.
Because the automobile industry is highly concentrated, firms closely observe their rivals' actions (Desir 2012) and thus could herd recalls for related or unrelated issues.
For example, GM may be monitoring a potential brake problem in one of their cars.
A decision by Toyota to announce a recall for a brake problem that is similar in nature may cause GM to modify their view on their own potential brake problem.
In other words, instead of viewing the problem as a defect that does not warrant a recall, a decision by Toyota to recall might prompt GM to move their brake problem from the "wait-and-see" category to the "recall-worthy" category, culminating in GM announcing a following recall shortly after Toyota.
Alternatively, even when quality problems are unrelated, firms may perceive a new cluster that has formed as an ideal time to recall a defective product they have been monitoring.
For example, anecdotal evidence presented earlier indicates that Ford's recall in August 2017 for fuel tank valves was quickly followed by a recall from Chrysler for a non fuel-tank related issue.
Combining the analytical foundation of recall clustering established in Proposition 1 in Section 3 with the herding literature discussion above, we hypothesize:
<!-- para 28 -->
Hypothesis 1.
Product recalls occur in clusters such that a leading recall by one firm excites following recalls by other firms.
<!-- para 30 -->
Although past scholarship has examined the stock price impact of recall announcements (Davidson and Worrell 1992), little is known about how the timing of a firm's recall announcement in relation to its competitors affects shareholder value.
Examining whether and how shareholder value is impacted by when firms announce a recall in relation to competitor recall timing may shed light on a consequence of, and an underlying motivation for, herding behavior.
<!-- para 31 -->
For this set of hypotheses, we build on attribution theory (Kelley and Michela 1980), which explains how entities interpret and attribute blame for events to various causes.
As mentioned in Section 3, when a firm's behavior is unique, external observers are more likely to attribute the underlying cause of the behavior to something specific about that entity and, hence, the entity is assigned more blame (Weiner 1985).
In contrast, when a subject's behavior is similar to others, external observers are more likely to attribute the underlying cause of the behavior to something that is beyond the entity's control and, thus, that entity is assigned less blame (Bettman and Weitz 1983).
Because past scholarship suggests that people feel the need to assign causality for negative events (Weiner 1985), it is reasonable to assume that the market will attribute the underlying cause of a recall either to something specific about a particular firm or to factors beyond a firm's control, depending on the degree of uniqueness of an event (Corgnet et al. 2010).
<!-- para 33 -->
Within a cluster, there is likely to be a stock market penalty difference between leading and following recalls.
This follows from Bettman and Weitz (1983), Weiner (1985), and Corgnet et al. (2010) who demonstrate that as the unique attribution of responsibility for a negative event increases, so does the penalty ascribed to that negative event.
In our context, if multiple firms announce product recalls in temporal proximity, external observers such as investors may attribute blame differently across the firms in accordance with the sequence of events.
The first recall in a cluster may be attributed higher blame due to a greater degree of perceived uniqueness.
In comparison, the following recalls may appear less unique because they follow a leading recall.
Lemma 1a in Section 3 offers theoretical insights into how the stock market penalty faced by the leader and follower compare against one another.
Building on this result and the attribution theory-related discussion above, we hypothesize:
<!-- para 34 -->
Hypothesis 2a.
The market penalty for leading recalls will be larger than the penalty for following recalls.
<!-- para 35 -->
Similarly, we propose that following recalls that occur immediately after the leading recall will fare better in terms of the stock market than following recalls that are more distant from the leading recall.
The explanation for this is grounded in the idea of greater blame attribution based upon uniqueness, as captured by the timing of a negative event (Kelley and Michela 1980).
Accordingly, the market is likely to assign greater blame to a following recall that is toward the end of a cluster and thus more removed from the leading recall than those that are announced closer in time to the leading recall.
We established this result analytically in Lemma 1b in Section 3.
Based on this result and the discussion above, we hypothesize the following:
<!-- para 36 -->
Hypothesis 2b.
The market penalty for a following recall will increase as the time since the leading recall increases
<!-- para 38 -->
In addition to exploring how the stock market effects of recall announcements vary based on the position of a recall within a cluster, we also analyze how differences in blame attribution could lead to differences in stock market effects between clusters.
Because it is the leading recall that establishes a cluster, our hypothesis in this section is focused on the factors that could explain differences in the stock market penalty associated with leading recalls.
Building on our arguments above, as more time elapses between the last following recall of one cluster and the leading recall of the subsequent cluster, the greater the perceived uniqueness and attribution of blame to the leading recall, which in turn may translate into a larger stock market penalty.
Using attribution theory as a building block, we demonstrated this result analytically in Lemma 2.
Following this result and the above discussion, we hypothesize the following:
<!-- para 39 -->
Hypothesis 2c.
The market penalty for a leading recall will increase as the time between clusters increases.

## methods
<!-- para 3 -->
We selected the auto industry as our research setting for three reasons.
First, the majority of recalls in this industry are voluntarily issued by automakers (Haunschild and Rhee 2004, Shah et al. 2017).
With voluntary recalls, firms control the timing of the recall announcement, which is an important aspect of this study.
Involuntary recalls, on the other hand, are mandated and directed by the NHTSA and thus firms have little discretion about when to issue them. [^1] Second, firms in the auto industry announce a large number of recalls, making it feasible to investigate the possibility of recall clustering.
Third, data regarding product recall announcements are carefully recorded by the NHTSA dating back to 1966, allowing us to assemble a large recall data set to answer our research questions.
<!-- para 5 -->
We collected data for all auto recalls recorded by the NHTSA spanning 48 years (1966–2013).
Recall data include firm, model, recall date, manufacture date, number of vehicles affected, the component associated with the recall, and recall initiator (firm or NHTSA).
Recall data were combined with COMPUSTAT and Center for Research in Security Prices databases for firm-level controls and stock market prices.
Consistent with recall research, we also collected auto model variety, volume, and age measures from Ward's Automotive (Shah et al. 2017).
Using the NHTSA campaign numbers assigned to each recall, we identified 3,792 recalls for all publicly traded firms that sell vehicles in the United States (Chrysler, Ford, GM, Honda, Nissan, and Toyota). [^2] Our clustering analyses focus on these six firms because they are publicly traded and are the top firms by market share in the United States representing 80% of the U.S. auto market. [^3] Because clusters may be artificially created by firms using the same supplier that has a quality problem, we used defect and noncompliance reports provided by the NHTSA to determine whether a recall was due to quality problems at the supplier (Shah et al. 2017) and removed 675 supplier-related recalls.
Our final sample consists of 3,117 recalls (2,408 voluntary and 709 involuntary) across the six publicly traded auto firms.
<!-- para 8 -->
We selected the Hawkes process model to examine support for clustering for three reasons.
First, recall clustering, by definition, contradicts an assumption of Poisson process models that events are random and independent across time (Wooldridge 2010).
Using a Poisson process to model auto recall occurrences would be suboptimal if a recall by one firm excites recalls by other firms.
Recent research has demonstrated the benefit of using self-excited point process models, such as the Hawkes process, as opposed to Poisson processes, to test for self-excitation of events in data structured similarly to ours (Xu et al. 2014).
Second, we leverage the Hawkes process model because of a valuable output derived from the model at convergence.
As described below, the Hawkes process model uses a two-step expectation-maximization (EM) algorithm for parameter estimation and convergence.
The EM algorithm is a Bayesian method used to estimate models where a portion of the data is latent, or unobserved (Wooldridge 2010).
In our model, the leading and following recall designations are latent.
However, this recall designation assigned at model convergence allows us to comprehensively and transparently describe the prevalence of recall clustering.
Third, the leading and following recall designations are necessary inputs to our event study models used to test Hypotheses 2a–2c.
Without a means to reliably categorize recalls as leading or following in a cluster, we would not be able to examine how the stock market reacts differently to recalls based on their position within a cluster.
<!-- para 10 -->
The Hawkes process model is a self-excited point process model that takes into account dependence, or clustering, among events (Hawkes 1971a).
Scholars have recently used similar models to study problems in other settings, including exploring how consumer clicks on ads may be self-excited events (Xu et al. 2014).
We adopt a similar approach in the context of auto recalls.
Let $f(m)$ denote the firm that manufactures auto model m.
The Hawkes process recall rate for model m on day t, given all recalls in the time window l days prior to day t, can be written as follows:
<!-- para 11 -->
$$ \begin{aligned}\lambda_{m,t}=&\exp\left(X_{m,t}^{^{\prime}}\beta\right)\\&+\sum_{\substack{t-l\leq t^{^{\prime}}\leq t\\ m^{^{\prime}}:f(m^{^{\prime}})\neq f(m)}}\theta\times e^{-\omega\times(t-t^{^{\prime}})}\mathbb{I}_{[RecallSize_{m^{^{\prime}},t^{^{\prime}}}>0]}\end{aligned} \tag{4} $$
In Equation (4), the recall rate $\lambda_{m,t}$ consists of two terms.
The first term, $\exp(X_{m,t}^{\prime}\beta)$, represents the background recall rate for model m on day t and is not influenced by prior recalls.
We allow this background recall rate to vary depending on the covariates described in Section 5.4.
The second term represents the excitation of the recall rate due to prior recalls.
This term differentiates a Hawkes process model from a Poisson process model and is used to examine evidence of self-excitation, or recall clustering.
Parameter $\ell$ represents the time window prior to day t.
Starting with $\ell = 0$, we re-estimate the model iteratively using increasing values of $\ell$ until the model converges.
Parameter $\theta$ captures the excitation (Hawkes 1971b) of the recall of model m produced by firm $f(m)$ on day t because of a recall by another firm at a prior day $t'$.
If the excitation parameter $\theta$ is positive and statistically significant, then the recall rate $\lambda_{m,t}$ is statistically higher than the background recall rate, $\exp(X_{m,t}^{\prime}\beta)$, indicating support for recall clustering and Hypothesis 1.
An additional feature of the Hawkes process model is the excitation decay.
That is, the excitation of future recalls is likely to decay as the time from the leading recall increases.
The exponential function is most commonly used to model this decay (Ait-Sahalia et al. 2015) and is represented by $e^{-\omega \times (t - t')}$, where $\omega$ is the decay parameter.
If this parameter is positive and significant, we can conclude that the excitation of future recalls decays with the time since the leading recall.
Finally, the term $\mathbb{I}_{[RecallSize_{m',t'} > 0]}$ is an indicator function that captures whether there was a recall by firm $f(m')$ on day $t'$.
<!-- para 13 -->
The Hawkes process model uses a two-step EM algorithm that is executed iteratively until the model converges (Veen and Schoenberg 2008).
In the first step, called the expectation (E) step, recalls are classified as leading, following, or neither based upon the time elapsed since the most recent recall. [^4] This classification is done by varying the number of days prior to a recall when no other recall was announced (parameter $\ell$ in Equation (4)).
More specifically, the algorithm begins with a zero day gap in recalls by all firms (i.e., $\ell=0$) and increases this gap in one-day increments.
After each $\ell$ day gap in recalls, the model assigns the next recall as leading and subsequent recalls from competitor firms as following until another similar gap in time is observed. [^5] This process continues until the end of the time-series and is defined as the branching structure of the Hawkes process model (Xu et al. 2014).
In the second step, called the maximization (M) step, the log-likelihood value is computed using the recall classification from the previous step.
For example, for each value of $\ell$ chosen in the first step (e.g., one day, two days, etc.), the algorithm computes the value and significance of the estimated parameters in Equation (4).
The two steps are executed iteratively until convergence, when the log-likelihood stops improving.
We use bootstrapping to calculate robust standard errors (Piatti and Trojani 2020) in the estimation of the maximum likelihood in the second step of the EM algorithm.
The final parameter significance is computed using the standard errors obtained after model convergence.
Please refer to the online appendix for a detailed description of the EM algorithm and the bootstrapping method.

To further interpret the results of the Hawkes process model, we compute the average marginal effects of the clustering parameters $\hat{\theta}$ and $\hat{\omega}$ by examining how the Poisson rate of a recall changes for each additional day after the leading recall.
The computation of the average marginal effects involves two steps: First, for each observation $(m, t)$ in our data set, where $m$ is an auto model and $t$ is the recall date, we estimate the decrease in excitation using the Hawkes process model (Equation (4)) by moving one additional day away from the leading recall.
In the second step, we average the estimates obtained in the first step across all the recalls in our data set.
Figure 1 displays the average marginal effect on the Poisson rate of a following recall, and the 95% confidence intervals of this marginal effect, moving one additional day away from the leading recall.
As an example, we see from Figure 1 that the average marginal effect of a leading recall on the Poisson rate of a following recall the next day (day one after the leading recall) is 0.22 with a 95% confidence interval of (0.16, 0.28).
However, as we move to the end of the excitation period within a cluster (day 34 after the leading recall), the marginal effect on the Poisson rate declines to a minimum of 0.02 with a 95% confidence interval of (0.01, 0.04).

The second robustness check examines differences in discretion between leading and following recalls, which is valuable to explore because our theoretical explanation for clustering is that firms may wait to recall until they observe a leading recall from one of their competitors.
Although the NHTSA does not categorize recalls by severity (Shah et al. 2017), they do report the number of units associated with a recall.
We use the number of units recalled as a proxy for recall severity.
If clustering is a real phenomenon, then following recalls should be more discretionary than leading recalls.
Hence, we would expect following recalls to be associated with fewer units recalled than leading recalls, which is what we find.
Specifically, we use the leading and following designations from the EM algorithm and test the difference between the number of units recalled in each category.
On average, leading recalls are associated with 296,274 units recalled, whereas the corresponding number for following recalls is 133,682 units.
These two numbers are statistically different with a t-test value of 4.23 (p < 0.001).

The third robustness check is an alternate modeling approach to explore clustering.

We now turn to our event study model to examine Hypotheses 2a–2c.
We follow a similar approach to other event studies that have investigated the impact of recall announcements on stock market returns (Thirumalai and Sinha 2011).
Specifically, we perform this analysis in three steps.
The first step is to estimate the expected stock market return for a firm when it does not announce a recall.
The second step is to test the significance of abnormal returns for the firm after announcing a recall.
One nuance in our setting is that we are not only examining if a leading or a following recall leads to a significant abnormal return but, more importantly, if the abnormal returns attributed to these two types of recalls are significantly different from each other.
The third step is to incorporate appropriate covariates to ensure that the stock market differences between the recall types hold after including other financial measures that may explain abnormal stock market returns.
In our analyses, we further control for endogeneity of events because recall clustering inherently assumes that recalls across firms are not exogenous.
<!-- para 32 -->
To estimate the expected stock market return in the absence of a recall, we use a market model (Thirumalai and Sinha 2011), which is a linear function of the overall market index of the S&P 500.
We use the linear market model as specified below for firm f on day t:
<!-- para 33 -->
$$ R_{ft}=\alpha_{0f}+\alpha_{1f}\times RM(t)+\epsilon_{ft}. \tag{5} $$
Consistent with prior research (MacKinlay 1997), we use the observed daily market returns, $R_{ft}$, of the recalling firm over a 30-day period prior to the event window (discussed in Section 6.2) as the dependent variable in our estimation of $\alpha_{0f}$ and $\alpha_{1f}$.
The independent variable, $RM(t)$, is the daily market index for the S&P 500 during the same time period.
The estimated coefficient $\hat{\alpha}_{1f}$ captures the slope of the relationship between the daily stock market return for the firm and the overall market index, whereas the estimated coefficient $\hat{\alpha}_{0f}$ represents the intercept of the linear model.
Equation (5) is consistent with prior event studies related to the impact of recall announcements on stock market returns but with the main difference being that we also account for event clustering when estimating the errors.
Market models with heteroscedastic error structures were introduced by Engle (1982) and Bollerslev (1986) and have been used in past studies that model endogenous, dependent events in financial markets (Nelson 1991).
Specifically, Bollerslev (1986) proposed using a generalized auto-regressive conditional heteroskedastic (GARCH) error structure for modeling market returns in cases where events are endogenous.
Accordingly, we use a GARCH model to estimate the error term in Equation (5) as specified below.
<!-- para 34 -->
$$ Error Distribution:\epsilon_{ft}\mid RM(t)\sim N(0,\sigma_{ft}^{2}) \tag{6} $$
$$ GARCH(1,1)Variance Structure:\sigma_{ft}^{2} $$
<!-- para 35 -->
$$ =\delta_{0f}+\delta_{1f}\epsilon_{f(t-1)}^{2}+\delta_{2f}\sigma_{f(t-1)}^{2}, $$
<!-- para 36 -->
(7) where $\sigma_{ft}^{2}$ specifies the error variance and $\delta_{0f}$, $\delta_{1f}$, and $\delta_{2f}$ specify the heteroskedastic error structure.
<!-- para 38 -->
The second step of the event study is to test the significance of abnormal returns for the firm announcing a recall.
Abnormal returns are calculated as the difference between the observed firm returns and the estimated firm returns, as shown below.
<!-- para 39 -->
$$ AR_{ft}=R_{ft}-(\hat{\alpha}_{0f}+\hat{\alpha}_{1f}\times RM(t)) \tag{8} $$
In Equation (8), $R_{ft}$ is the actual return and $\hat{\alpha}_{0f} + \hat{\alpha}_{1f} \times RM(t)$ is the expected return in the absence of a recall announcement.
Consistent with prior event studies, we aggregate the daily abnormal returns over multiple days to create the CAR measure (Thirumalai and Sinha 2011).
We considered several event windows in our analysis when calculating CARs, allowing us to observe the abnormal market returns over varying time periods that include days before and after the event.
We consider the following values of $t_1$ and $t_2$ used in the calculation of CAR as shown below in Equation (9): $(t_1, t_2) = (-1, 0), (-1, +1), (-1, +5)$ and $(-5, t_2)$, where $t_2 \in \{+5, +10, +15, +20, +25, +30\}$.
Note that in the above event windows, time “0” refers to the day of the recall announcement.
<!-- para 40 -->
$$ CAR_{f[t_{1},t_{2}]}=\sum_{t=t_{1}}^{t=t_{2}}AR_{ft} \tag{9} $$
When examining whether the abnormal returns (i.e., CARs) are statistically significant, we again take steps to control for dependence among recall events.
Related event studies have used a Patell t-test (Patell 1976) to examine the statistical significance of abnormal market returns over multiple time windows in a market model (Thirumalai and Sinha 2011).
This test, however, is only appropriate for independent events.
To correct for event dependence, Kolari and Pynnönen (2010) introduced the adjusted Patell t-test for clustered events in a market model.
This test accounts for the correlation of abnormal returns across events within a cluster. [^7] We incorporate this test to both examine if the leading and following recalls are individually associated with abnormal returns and, more importantly, if the abnormal returns are different from each other.
Specifically, consistent with prior event studies (Thirumalai and Sinha 2011), we analyze the leading and following recall CAR as a percentage change relative to each firm's own price, as it helps to normalize the stock market penalties across firms. [^8] CAR estimates for the leading and following recalls as well as the adjusted Patell t-test statistics are presented in Table A3 in the online appendix.
In the table, we show the different event windows, the mean CAR and Patell t-statistic for the leading and following recalls, the difference in CAR between the two, and the Patell t-statistic for the difference.
We make several observations from Table A3.
First, we see that leading and following recalls, individually, lead to a negative and statistically significant CAR in a majority of the event windows.
However, more pertinent to our study, we see from columns (6) and (7) that the leading recall CARs are significantly more negative than the following recall CARs for four event windows, ranging from 23% in event window (-5, 15) to 67% in event window (-1, 5).
Hence, although both types of recalls lead to abnormal negative stock market returns, we find that leading recalls, on average, have significantly more negative stock market consequences than following recalls.

The third step of the event study is to incorporate appropriate covariates.
In addition to the covariates included in the Hawkes process model to control for recall count or timing, we added several covariates that may influence the financial performance of firms following negative events (Thirumalai and Sinha 2011).
Ln_Revenue: The stock market may react differently to recalls by large firms compared with smaller ones.
To control for this, we include the natural log of the firm's revenue in the year of the event (Thirumalai and Sinha 2011).
Ln_Recall Size: The stock market may penalize firms more for larger recalls than smaller recalls.
To control for this possibility, we include the natural log of the number of units recalled (Chen et al. 2009).
Ln_Past Recalls: Firms that recall frequently may be penalized more than firms that issue fewer recalls.
As such, we control for the natural log of the number of past recalls by the firm in the last 12 months (Thirumalai and Sinha 2011).
Ln_Market Share: Firms that have a large market presence may be penalized less than firms with a small market presence because of reputational effects (Chen et al. 2009).
We control for annual market share by using the natural log of the ratio of annual sales of the focal firm to the total annual sales of the other five firms in our data set.
Ln_Market Cap: We control for the effect of a firm's market value on the stock market's reaction to a recall by incorporating the natural log of a firm's market capitalization when the recall is announced (Hendricks et al. 2009).
Component: We control for the component recalled to ensure that different components involved in the recall are not the reason for our CARs.
The NHTSA groups each recall into one of 15 broadly defined categories (e.g., brake, engine, steering).
We use 14 component fixed effects associated with the recalls in our data set and treat airbags as the reference category.
Finally, our main variable of interest in the event study is Leading Recall, which is a binary variable that takes the value of one if a particular recall is a leading recall and zero otherwise.
The magnitude and significance of Leading Recall enables us to conclude whether our hypothesis that leading recalls lead to more negative stock market reactions than following recalls holds after controlling for the large set of covariates described above.
Descriptive statistics for these covariates are presented in Table A4 and their correlations are included in Table A5 in the online appendix.

Because recalls are clustered, we utilize a generalized estimating equation (GEE) model in our analysis because GEE models can accommodate clustered recalls across firms and can correct for serial correlation across time (Shah et al. 2017).
We use an exchangeable working correlation structure to correct for serial correlation and cluster robust standard errors.
In the GEE equation (Equation (10)), we model abnormal market returns experienced by firm f on day t due to recalls related to model m and component j:

$$
CAR_{ft}=\beta_{0}+\beta_{1}\mathrm{LeadingRecall}_{ft}+\beta_{2}\mathrm{LNRevenue}_{ft}+\beta_{3}\mathrm{LNModelAge}_{mt}+\beta_{4}\mathrm{LNRecallSize}_{mt}+\beta_{5}\mathrm{LNPastRecalls}_{ft}+\beta_{6}\mathrm{LNModelVariety}_{mt}+\beta_{7}\mathrm{LNModelVolume}_{mt}+\beta_{8}\mathrm{TimeSinceLastCluster}_{ft}+\epsilon_{ft} \tag{10}
$$

The fourth robustness check investigates if component similarity between the leading and following recalls might explain our event study results.
We conduct this test because we theorize that the market will penalize following recalls differently based upon how uniquely the market views it in comparison with the leading recall.
Thus far, we have demonstrated that recall timing serves as a proxy for event uniqueness in the context of auto recalls.
However, it is plausible that differences in the underlying component involved in the leading and following recalls could also explain the differential stock market penalty experienced by the two types of recalls.
To test if this is indeed the case, we replicate the event study reported in Table 5 but designate each following recall in a cluster as either Same Component Following the leading recall or Different Component Following the leading recall.
We compare the difference in stock market penalty between leading and following recalls with the same component versus the difference in stock market penalty between leading and following recalls with different components.

We find no significant differences (at $p < 0.10$) between the two sets of coefficients.
This suggests that the stock market views recall uniqueness more from the perspective of recall timing than the component recalled, providing additional support for our theorizing.

## discussion
<!-- para 2 -->
Leveraging game theoretic model insights as well as literature on herding and attribution theories, we posit that product recalls occur in clusters and that the stock market penalty attributed to a recall varies depending upon its position within a cluster.
Analyzing 48 years of automotive recall data using a Hawkes process model, we find robust evidence that firms cluster recalls, with $73\%$ of auto recalls appearing in clusters that last on average 34 days and consist of 7.6 following recalls.
<!-- para 3 -->
These clustering findings contribute to the recall and herding literature streams.
Recall literature has been predominantly silent on the factors that may influence recall timing, both in the context of the auto industry as well as in other recall-intensive industries, with few exceptions (Hora et al. 2011, Ball et al. 2018, Wowak et al. 2020).
Our study lays important groundwork in this burgeoning area of research and highlights the need for future work to account for competitor recall endogeneity, in particular competitor recall timing, when studying recalls.
Our findings also make important contributions to the literature on herding theory.
Although herding has been used to explain various accounting and finance-related phenomena, to our knowledge, herding behavior has not yet been demonstrated in an operational setting.
We extend this important theoretical viewpoint to operations management by showing that managers are susceptible to herding when deciding when to recall potentially defective products, both when the recalled component is similar to, and when it is different from, the leading recall component.
Additionally, we contribute to literature on herding theory by demonstrating that firm reputation may play a key role in creating a herd (Hirshleifer and Hong Teoh 2003).
More specifically, we see that Toyota stands out in Table 3 with $31\%$ of its recalls acting as a leading recall within a cluster, which is a significantly larger proportion than all other firms.
This is likely due to Toyota's stellar quality reputation (Rhee and Haunschild 2006).
In particular, competitor firms may move quickly to rid themselves of quality problems that have been festering when a quality stalwart such as Toyota announces a recall.

We also find that leading recalls suffer as high as a $67\%$ larger stock market penalty than following recalls, indicating a meaningful financial incentive for auto firms to cluster following recalls behind a leading recall announcement.
This stock market penalty difference dissipates over time within a cluster.
Additionally, across clusters, the stock market penalty faced by the leading recall amplifies as the time since the last cluster increases.
These stock market findings contribute to the recall and attribution literature streams.
<!-- para 5 -->
Past event studies within this body of work have explored how contextual factors may influence the stock market consequences faced by firms after making recall announcements (Rhee and Haunschild 2006, Chen et al. 2009).
Our study contributes to this literature by extending attribution theory (Weiner 1985) to a recall setting and demonstrating that the market reaction to a recall varies depending on the recall's position within a cluster.
Specifically, we find that the more unique a recall appears to be in comparison with the competitors, the greater the market attribution of blame to the firm and the larger the stock market penalty.
Furthermore, this recall uniqueness appears to be explained most clearly by the timing of the recall relative to the competition.

We narrow the above findings and contributions to one parsimonious practical implication.
Because our study demonstrates that auto firms consciously or unconsciously time their recalls to minimize stock price penalties, any implication of substance should be directed toward increasing recall decision-making transparency.
Making auto recall decisions transparent should make them more timely, reducing the prevalence of clustering, which creates unnecessary delays in removing harmful products from the market.
To this end, we examined how the NHTSA manages recalls compared with another major U.S. regulatory agency that oversees recalls, the Food and Drug Administration (FDA).
Through Freedom of Information Act requests, we obtained FDA recall data for drug and medical device recalls from 2003–2012.
That data include a mandatory, firm-provided, defect awareness date, along with a recall initiation date.
This allows the FDA to measure the actual time to recall, making it harder for firms to delay a recall decision.
The NHTSA does not require firms to provide defect awareness dates.
However, requiring auto firms to report the date that they first became aware of a defect may discourage them from hiding in the herd and prompt them to make more timely and transparent recall decisions.
We have proposed this solution to the NHTSA.

Our research is not without limitations.
As with all secondary data studies, there could be unobserved variables and endogeneity that may bias our results.
However, we have conducted a number of robustness checks and incorporated numerous covariates to mitigate this concern.
Additionally, to remove potential biases in the stock market analysis that could be introduced by having recalls in the pre-event window, we used the pre-event window of the leading recall in a cluster as the pre-event window for both leading and following recalls within that cluster.
Doing so, however, could introduce another source of bias as the pre-event window for following recalls may be several days removed from the following recall announcement.
Despite these caveats, the findings from our research can serve as a catalyst for future research in two directions.
First, the automobile industry is highly concentrated; as a result, firms may be able to extract more information about when to issue a product recall by monitoring their competitors' actions than in other industries that are less concentrated.
Second, auto manufacturers produce similar products and thus inherently have comparable manufacturing processes.
This might allow firms to extract more accurate information about when to issue a recall by observing the competitors' actions than in other industries with greater product diversity.
The high degree of product similarity may also create an environment in which herding behavior is more likely because when many similar recalls occur in close temporal proximity, the NHTSA may not be able to closely scrutinize each recall.
If so, this would create an additional explanation for firms to cluster recalls in the auto industry.
Consequently, we encourage future researchers to explore recall clustering in industries that are less concentrated and have more diverse products.
