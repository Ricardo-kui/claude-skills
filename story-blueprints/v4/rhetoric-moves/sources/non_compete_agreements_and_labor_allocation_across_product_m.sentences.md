# non_compete_agreements_and_labor_allocation_across_product_m 句子库存

## introduction
<!-- para 2 -->
Non-compete agreements (NCA) are covenants that restrict employees from working for competitors during and after employment.
Employers commonly use NCAs to retain valuable human capital within-firm boundaries and to protect trade secrets.
On the one hand, NCAs can benefit employees, because of increased incentives for employers to retain and invest in employees' human capital (Garmaise 2011; Johnson, Lipsitz, and Pei 2023).
However, the cost is reduced wages (Lipsitz and Starr 2022) as well as lower labor mobility (Marx, Strumsky, and Fleming 2009; Johnson, Lavetti, and Lipsitz 2025).
There is an ongoing debate in economics and finance about further benefits and drawbacks of these agreements (Garmaise 2011; Samila and Sorenson 2011; Marx and Fleming 2012; Starr 2019; Johnson, Lipsitz, and Pei 2023; Reinmuth and Rockall 2023; Shi 2023; He 2025).
On the policy side, the Federal Trade Commission (FTC) announced a ban on NCAs on April 23, 2024 in a 3–2 vote [^1], which was later blocked by a federal court order.
<!-- para 3 -->
In this article, I add a novel and likely first-order dimension to the finance literature and policy debate on NCAs: product markets.
NCAs effectively constrain the within-product market employment choice set of inventors.
Inventors bound by an NCA who want to move to a new employer thus face the trade-off to risk litigation and move to a closely related competitor or to "bypass" the NCA and work for a new employer in a more distant product market.
The evidence provided in this article supports the existence of this trade-off and documents evidence consistent with detrimental consequences of NCA-constrained employment options.
<!-- para 4 -->
To conduct the analysis, I use the universe of corporate patenting data in the United States from 1976 to 2018, which include around 5.3 million inventor-year observations.
Patent data provide a suitable laboratory to study NCAs and allocation of labor for several reasons.
First, because patent ownership rights are assigned to their employers, patent data provide detailed employment histories of inventors.
Second, corporate employers provide measures of industry affiliation.
Third, inventors are highly skilled individuals and, as such, are likely affected by NCAs.
Fourth, patent data provide measures of the technologies used, as well as a granular measure of productivity (e.g., citations received and the economic value based on employers' market reactions to patent grants).
Lastly, patent data provide the precise location on a state-level of each individual inventor listed on a patent, thus making location-based variation feasible.
<!-- para 5 -->
Changes in NCA enforceability across US states provide variation for estimating causal effects.
In a staggered difference-in-differences event time regression, increases in NCA enforcement are positively related to the probability that an inventor moves to a more distant product market.
In terms of economic magnitude, on average two out of hundred inventors move to another industry per year, an increase of 42 percent.
The baseline regression uses inven
		\tor and year fixed effects, and thus exploits the staggered timing of nine NCA enforcement increases across states either in the form of precedent-setting court cases or state laws.
<!-- para 6 -->
Recent econometric theory provides guidance on the event study design: I compare treated inventors (i.e., those exposed to an increase in NCA enforceability) to never-treated in an event time framework and use the imputation-based estimator by Borusyak, Jaravel, and Spiess (2024) to account for heterogeneous treatment effects.
Consistent with a causal interpretation of the results, there are no pre-trends.
These results are either based on standard two-way fixed effects panel regressions or a matched sample comparing never-treated control inventors who are otherwise similar to treated inventors.
The results hold in unmatched samples, are robust to using various industry definitions such as SIC and NAICS codes, as well as continuous textual-based definitions of product markets, and different levels of clustering standard errors.
<!-- para 7 -->
It would be problematic if the introduction of state-level legislation is correlated with other policy interventions that affect industry mobility.
I address potential concerns of relying on state-level shocks by using within state-year variation in the intensity in treatment.
Specifically, I construct a firm-level proxy, based on executives' contracts filed in 10-K and 10-Q statements, whether an employer likely relies on NCAs.
If inventors indeed bypass their NCA and move to more distant product markets, then inventors employed at firms that rely on such agreements should be more affected.
I include state-year fixed effects, and show that primarily those inventors move to more distant product markets whose employers are more likely to rely on NCAs.
In line with economic theory, industry mobility is particularly pronounced for inventors in highly concentrated industries, in which they have only a few closely related firms to choose from.
<!-- para 8 -->
However, apart from the conditional probability of inventors moving, I also analyze the question of whether total levels, measured as departure rates, increase following NCA enforcement increases.
I aggregate all labor flows on a firm-state level and, indeed, show that this is the case.
Crucially, within-industry mobility declines consistent with earlier literature such as Jeffers (2023).
We also see increased levels of across-industry mobility when NCAs are more enforceable.
<!-- para 9 -->
The natural follow-up question to ask is: What is the effect of NCA-constrained reallocation on the productivity of inventors?
I estimate intent-to-treat regressions, which do not rely on inventors' choices.
Using inventor and state-year fixed effects, these regressions show evidence of lower subsequent productivity.
Inventors most affected by the labor restrictions are less likely to patent, produce fewer patents, and produce patents with fewer citations.
<!-- para 10 -->
To shed some light on negative productivity effects, I analyze what characterizes observed industry mobility.
I calculate a measure for labor market matching quality between inventors and their new employers based on how similar the patent technologies of the inventor and the new employer are.
NCA-constrained industry moves are associated with 10 percent lower technology similarity, a significant decline in labor market matching quality.
The subset of reallocated inventors also subsequently patents in what to them are new, unfamiliar patent technologies.
<!-- para 11 -->
The results emphasize an important distinction between ex-ante and ex-post effect of labor market regulation.
Ex-ante, NCAs are designed to incentivize employers to invest in their employees.
Ex-post however, NCAs create a hold-up problem and shift bargaining power to employers.
Inventors cannot credibly threaten to move to another firm and retain their industry-specific human capital.
It might thus be optimal for them to leave and retain a higher share of their productivity output.
<!-- para 12 -->
This article contributes to several strands of literature.
First, it contributes to the literature on the real effects of labor market frictions (Shen 2021; Bena, Ortiz-Molina, and Simintzi 2022).
Previous research documents that NCAs lead to lower labor mobility.
Specifically, Fallick, Fleischman, and Rebitzer (2006); Garmaise (2011) and Lipsitz and Starr (2022) document lower within-industry mobility, whereas Marx, Strumsky, and Fleming (2009) document lower within-state mobility.
Jeffers (2023), using comprehensive data on labor flows across the United States, finds lower across-industry, but highest departure rates for within-industry.
The evidence presented in this article is broadly consistent with these findings.
One notable difference is that among inventors, compared to other skilled labor, I document relatively higher baseline rates of across-industry mobility.
One possible explanation is that inventors face fewer opportunities for within-industry moves compared to other skilled workers.
Lastly, Marx, Singh, and Fleming (2015) also establish higher mobility, specifically a brain drain of skilled human capital from NCA enforcing to non-enforcing states.
By focusing on narrow product market dimensions, I document higher labor market mobility for inventors.
The article is thus closely related to Marx (2011), who provides survey evidence consistent with the empirical results presented in this article.
Furthermore, my setting allows to analyze long-run employment outcomes and an important outcome for society: productivity measured as innovation output.
This article is closely related to two theoretical papers on NCAs.
Chen et al. (2026) theoretically and empirically argue that (pre-FTC ban) NCA regulation is near optimal for growth.
Shi (2023) on the other hand suggests that a complete ban on NCAs might be the optimal policy.
This article does not provide full equilibrium evidence on efficiency and social welfare, but rather deep dives into product markets as a first-order consideration for NCAs.
<!-- para 13 -->
I also add to the allocation of labor literature across firms and specifically across product markets (Hombert and Matray 2017; Hombert and Matray 2018; Babina 2020; Hacamo and Kleiner 2022; Babina, Ouimet, and Zarutskie 2025).
I show how labor market frictions can lead to some reallocation of labor across product markets, which is likely an unintended consequence in the context of NCAs.
I show that NCAs have profound impact on career choices of employees, and affect inventor productivity.

## methods
<!-- para 3 -->
The first step is to obtain data on individual inventors and their patents from the United States Patent and Trademark Office (USPTO).
Under US patent law, patents are initially granted to individual inventors rather than their employers.
In practice, however, inventors typically waive their intellectual property rights to their employers, allowing patents to be assigned to firms.
This institutional feature makes it possible to infer inventors' employment histories by observing the assignees to whom patent rights are transferred.
The USPTO provides data on granted patents including who invented each patent, the location of each inventor, and the application year which is used for a timely measure of innovation generation.
The USPTO provides disambiguated inventor-level data, [^2] which allows researchers to track individual inventors over time.
Additionally, the data provide citations to and from all patents granted until the end of 2024.
The data are obtained from patentsview.org.
<!-- para 4 -->
Data on corporate innovation are obtained from two primary sources, Kogan et al. (2017) and Arora, Belenzon, and Sheer (2021). [^3] The combination of these two data sources results in a list of patent numbers assigned to unique corporate identifiers (assignees) and identifiers who produced the innovation (inventors).
<!-- para 6 -->
An NCA typically restricts an employee's post-employment opportunities by limiting the industries in which they may work, the duration of the restriction—often one to two years after separation—and, less frequently, the geographic scope.
As a result, NCAs constrain the set of feasible employment choices available to affected individuals.
<!-- para 7 -->
Regulation of NCAs is largely determined at the state level.
California prohibits the enforcement of NCAs, whereas Florida exhibits among the strongest enforcement regimes.
Despite this variation in enforceability, evidence from Shi (2023) indicates that a substantial share of executives continue to sign NCAs even in states where such agreements are not legally enforceable.
One possible explanation is that employers hedge against the possibility that employees later relocate to jurisdictions in which NCAs are enforceable.
I summarize changes in state-level NCA enforcement in Table 1.
I rely on Ewens and Marx (2018), who provide an extensive discussion on court rulings and legislative changes from 1985 to 2016. [^4] The second data source is drawn from Kini, Williams, and Yin (2021).
The article extends a score of NCA enforceability across states originally developed by Garmaise (2011) to the years 1992–2014.

What happens when NCAs become more enforceable?
Often, NCAs are enforceable conditional on passing a “reasonableness” test.
After a 1996 legislative change, NCAs in Florida need to protect “legitimate business interests” in order to be enforceable.
This clarified previous uncertainty and shifted power towards employers.
This is one example of an event used in this article.
Another example is the Ohio Supreme Court decision in 2004 which stated that a sufficient consideration to uphold an NCA was continued employment.
Another example is Idaho, which changed to a so-called “blue pencil” rule where a judge can modify the contract to make it more reasonable whereas in other states one invalid part of an NCA renders the whole agreement void. [^5]
<!-- para 13 -->
For some specifications, I use data on firm-level reliance on NCAs.
I proceed in similar fashion as Kini, Williams, and Yin (2021) and Acikalin et al. (2022).
First, I obtain form 10-K and form 10-Q filings from EDGAR.
I obtain identifiers from historical Compustat from WRDS servers, as well as a historical CIK-CUSIP mapping. [^6] Form 10-K and form 10-Q filings commonly include NCAs of senior employees at a firm.
I use the information to construct a panel of US corporations with an indicator variable equal to 1 if the corporate employer mentions the use of an NCA either in an executive/board contract or mentions the reliance on NCAs in the annual statement.
I screen for instances of “NCA” or “covenant not to compete”.
I construct a dummy variable equal to 1 if a firm relies on NCAs and then transform this variable to a firm-year panel, whether the firm in the past has mentioned the use of NCAs.
This panel is comprehensive from year 1996 onward.
<!-- para 14 -->
I compare the frequency of NCA use based on EDGAR filings with the academic literature.
In my sample, 54 percent of firms rely on NCAs. [^7] This is close to previous survey and empirical evidence.
To compare, Starr, Prescott, and Bishara (2021) find that almost one-fifth of all employees in the United States are bound by NCAs.
The share of NCAs for technical workers is around 50 percent (Marx 2011), 62.5 percent for CEOs with employment contracts (Kini, Williams, and Yin 2021), and 70 percent for corporate executives (Garmaise 2011).
<!-- para 15 -->
The Appendix lists some examples of NCAs obtained from firms' 10-K or 10-Q.
In its annual statement, Microvision emphasizes its heavy reliance on NCAs.
In an Appendix to a 10-Q, Nuance Communications explicitly mentions that the firm prohibits employees "from working for an employer who is engaged in activities or offers products that are competitive with the activities and products of the company."
<!-- para 17 -->
I start with all patents matched to US firms from Kogan et al. (2017) and Arora, Belenzon, and Sheer (2021).
Next, I merge all corporate-owned patents with the disambiguated inventor data.
The sample starts in the year 1976, the first year with disambiguated digitized inventor-level data.
The sample of granted patents based on USPTO data ends at the end of 2024; however, to allow some time for the patent grant process, all regressions only use data on patent application until the year 2018.
<!-- para 18 -->
I identify industry employment changes as follows: The inventor files two subsequent patent applications for a different employer with a different industry affiliation.
I follow the previous literature (Song, Almeida, and Wu 2003; Marx, Singh, and Fleming 2015) and use the yearly midpoint between two subsequent patent applications to proxy for the year of employment change. [^8] The application year rather than the grant year is used in order to have a more timely measure of innovation creation [^9] and employment changes.
Inventors with only a single patent during the sample period are excluded.
All regressions include inventor fixed-effects, and other specification use identified mobility events.
Thus, inventors with only one patent would not provide any meaningful variation on labor market mobility.
As an additional data cleaning step, I exclude inventors who assign the rights to their patents to more than five entities or those who move employers more than ten times in their life time, which are unlikely to be real employment mobility events.
The resulting data set is a panel at the inventor-year level.
<!-- para 19 -->
Innovation is an ideal laboratory for several reasons: First, the universe of corporate patenting in the last 42 years provides tractable employment histories of inventors based on granted patents.
In the context of this article, it also seems plausible that highly skilled human capital such as inventors, are likely to be affected by NCAs.
<!-- para 20 -->
Second, patent documents capture the location (proxied by the state level) of each inventor listed on a patent.
This improves measurement for empirical research that uses location-based variation in treatment.
<!-- para 21 -->
Third, corporate innovation data allow to look at two distinct but related dimensions: measures of product and technology similarity.
Product markets for employers are readily available as SIC and NAICS industry codes, as well as text-based industry classifications following Hoberg and Phillips (2010) and Hoberg and Phillips (2016).
The latter is a measure with desirable econometric properties which can be used to measure the similarity between the old and the new employers of inventors.
Patent data provide technology classifications of every patent (e.g., CPC, WIPO, IPC).
This is useful as it allows researchers to compute technology similarities between the patents of inventors and their employers.
<!-- para 22 -->
Fourth, and lastly, patent data provide a useful metric on a patent basis to measure the productivity of an inventor over time.
A researcher can thus observe the number of patents, the number of citations received [^10] (Lerner and Seru 2022), and the economic value of patents (Kogan et al. 2017).
The latter is comprised of a USD value on a patent basis, calculated using stock market reactions of listed patent assignees on the grant day of a patent.
<!-- para 23 -->
There are also drawbacks of patent data.
Even though they capture the universe of patenting activity in the United States, they do not allow to track individuals who stop patenting.
When constructing employment histories, I thus capture a lower bound of individuals' mobility events.
In a robustness exercise, treatment and control inventors do not differ in their attrition.
Nevertheless, sample composition remains a potential concern.

To provide readers with an overview, I briefly describe the different panels used in the empirical analysis.
The baseline panel, on which most of the empirical analyses rely, is described in Table 2.
This panel includes all inventor-year observations.
To control for possible covariate imbalance, some analyses are on a matched sample.
If the analysis relies on whether the inventor is likely bound by an NCA, or uses product market similarities, the sample only includes Compustat employers with available EDGAR data from 1996 onward.
This panel includes around 3.2 million inventor-year observations.
Some regressions are conditional on inventors who move across SIC 4 digit industries.
These regressions are cross-sectional in nature and rely on a sample of around 125,000 across-industry employment mobility events.
One regression is on a state panel, using years from 2000 to 2025.
Lastly, the aggregated panel estimating levels of departures computes departure rates on a firm-state level, resulting in a panel of around 540,000 firm-state observations.
<!-- para 34 -->
To start the analysis, I estimate the following standard, TWFE panel difference-in-differences regression:
<!-- para 35 -->
$$
IndustrySwitch_{i,t}=\delta D_{i,t}+\theta_{i}+\phi_{t}+\varepsilon_{i,t} \tag{1}
$$
<!-- para 36 -->
where i represents inventor i, in year t.
D is the treatment dummy variable switching to one for an inventor who experiences an increase in NCA enforcement.
The dependent variable IndustrySwitch is equal to 1 if an inventor moves between two firms with different 4-digit SIC industry codes.
The variables  $\theta$ and  $\phi$ are inventor and year fixed-effects, respectively.
Year fixed-effects account for year-specific shocks to inventor mobility.
Inventor fixed-effects control for time-invariant unobserved factors on the inventor level.
The coefficient of interest is  $\delta$ which captures the treatment effect in a standard panel two-way fixed effect regression.
I cluster standard errors on the source of variation, which is the state level and additionally on the inventor level.
<!-- para 37 -->
Potential problems are heterogeneous treatment effects as well as negative weighting when we compare early-treated to late-treated inventors (Goodman-Bacon 2021; Baker, Larcker, and Wang 2022).
Recent econometric theory guides the setup of the regression using state-level variation in treatment of Table 1.
I compare treated with never-treated inventors.
Thus, I compare inventors based in states that experienced increased enforcement of NCAs with clean controls: those inventors that did not experience any NCA enforcement changes during the sample period.
The baseline regression therefore only defines treatment as inventors in states which experience one increase in NCA enforceability in the time frame.
<!-- para 38 -->
The goal of the empirical analysis is to tease out long-run impacts in product market mobility of inventors.
The analysis is thus confined to increases in NCA enforcement that are not reversed shortly after.
Panel A in Table 1 shows the variation used in the main analysis.
Panel B shows decreases in NCA enforceability.
Panel C includes states with multiple increases and decreases.
The analysis is not sensitive whether NCA enforcement decreases are excluded or included, or to the inclusion of states with more than one increase and decrease in NCA enforcement.
A likely explanation for this is the existence of a large group of never-treated control inventors.
<!-- para 39 -->
In a second specification, I analyze dynamic effects in the following matched sample event study regression:
<!-- para 40 -->
$$
IndustrySwitch_{i,t}=\sum_{k=-5}^{k=+9}\beta_{k}\times D_{k}\times NCAIncrease_{i}+\theta_{i}+\phi_{t}+\varepsilon_{i,t} \tag{2}
$$
<!-- para 41 -->
where  $D_k$ are time dummies relative to the NCA enforcement increase, where  $i$ represents inventor  $i$ in year  $t$.
The dependent variable IndustrySwitch is equal to 1 if an inventor moves between two firms with different 4-digit SIC industry codes.
The variables  $\theta$ and  $\phi$ are, as before, inventor and year fixed-effects, respectively.
<!-- para 42 -->
The coefficients of interest are now  $\beta_{k}$ which capture the treatment indicator interacted with five pretreatment dummies and ten posttreatment dummies.
To account for possible treatment effect heterogeneity, the equation is estimated using the imputation-based approach of Borusyak, Jaravel, and Spiess (2024).
<!-- para 43 -->
The specification is based on a matched sample.
I match the nearest three neighbors based on the same year, the same patent technology class, and based on similar quality measured by the cumulative number of patents and citations received so far.
Different matching criteria do not affect the results.
<!-- para 44 -->
The previous analyses rely on standard, fixed industry classifications such as SIC codes.
In the following, I analyze whether the results generalize to a continuous version of industry similarity between two firms.
For this, I use the textual based industry similarity scores of Hoberg and Phillips (2016), which are calculated as the similarity between the product description of the old and the new employer. [^11] Using textual product market similarities provides several benefits, such as (1) the industry definitions are not fixed over time and the measure varies between two identical firms across time.
(2) The measure captures product market proximity continuously regardless of whether two firms are in the same industry or not.
Standard classifications are binary in nature: two firms are either in the same industry or not.
A continuous measure might provide additional variation.
<!-- para 45 -->
Conceptually, this analysis differs from the previous one in one more crucial way.
It is conditional on moving to another employer.
The previous regression was unconditional in nature, and the industry switch dummy was either zero or one.
The following regression compares only inventors who move among which some are choice set constrained due to increases in NCA enforceability.
An alternative way of phrasing is that the following analysis analyzes the intensive margin (e.g., where in the product market space inventors move to, conditional on moving) versus the extensive margin (whether inventors move).
<!-- para 46 -->
I analyze whether inventors move to employers further away in a product market dimension from their old employers after an increase in NCA enforcement.
Formally, I run the following regression:
<!-- para 47 -->
$$
Similarity_{i}=\beta\times D_{i}+\phi_{t}+\varepsilon_{i} \tag{3}
$$
<!-- para 48 -->
where Similarity $_{i}$ is the product market similarity between the previous and the new employer obtained from Hoberg and Phillips (2016).
The subscript i now refers to an inventor mobility event and the sample is thus cross-sectional in nature.
D is the treatment dummy, NCA increase, which is equal to 1 if the inventor is exposed to an increase in NCA enforcement.
The regression includes year fixed effects and I cluster standard errors on the state level, as this specification is cross-sectional in nature.
<!-- para 49 -->
If NCA enforcement increases indeed lead to increased across-industry mobility of inventors, then we would expect this effect to be stronger for inventors that are in fact bound to an NCA.
Unfortunately, individual level NCAs of inventors are unobserved.
However, employers might differ on how much they rely on NCAs.
I compute a proxy on a firm level as follows: First, I obtain form 10-K and form 10-Q filings of the employers in the sample from 1996 to 2018.
These filings often include contract information and NCAs of executives and other senior employees.
I compute a dummy equal to 1 if a firm relies on NCAs.
The assumption is that to some extent, this firm-level dummy is a proxy for the presence of NCAs on an inventor level.
<!-- para 50 -->
I formally test whether increased enforcement of NCAs leads to more industry mobility especially for those inventors employed at firms that use NCAs.
For this purpose, I run a triple difference-in-differences regression as follows:
<!-- para 51 -->
$$
Y_{i,t}=\beta\times D_{i,t}\times EmployerNCA_{j,t}+\theta_{i}+\phi_{st}+\varepsilon_{i,t} \tag{4}
$$
<!-- para 52 -->
where EmployerNCA is an indicator variable equal to 1 if employer j relies on NCAs.
The parameter of interest is  $\beta$ which captures the interaction term of the previous treatment indicator  $D \times$ the dummy variable Employer NCA.
The variable is equal to 1 only for inventors in years after an increase in NCA enforcement, and additionally employed at firms who rely on NCAs.
Importantly, the regression includes Inventor fixed effects as well as  $\phi$, which is a State-Year fixed effect.
These fixed effects absorb the previous treatment dummy, which varies on a state-year level.
Thus, the specification measures the relative increase in industry mobility comparing inventors in treated states likely bound by NCA contracts to those that are less likely bound.
The standard errors in this regression are clustered on the state and inventor level.
<!-- para 53 -->
Since this specification includes State-Year fixed effects, the effect of state NCA adoption is fully absorbed by the fixed effects.
Hence, the regression does not allow to interpret the average effect of NCA adoption.
However, it is of interest to understand the baseline effects of enforcement increases of NCA on labor mobility.
To accommodate these effects, I rerun the specification excluding State-Year fixed effects which allows to include all possible interactions.
<!-- para 54 -->
In the previous sections, the unit of analysis is inventors.
While it might be relevant for policy makers whether individuals move across product markets, ultimately it is crucial to know whether the levels of employee departures outside and within product markets are affected.
For this purpose, I construct an aggregated unit of analysis, specifically on a firm–state level.
Specifically, I apportion inventors based on the state information listed on patent documents.
This means that all IBM inventors located in Idaho might be affected by an increase in NCA enforcement, whereas IBM inventors located in California or New York are never treated.
Given that the source of variation is on a state-level, this means that aggregated units of observations cleanly map into treated and non-treated status.
To do so, I analyze dynamic effects in the following event study regression:
<!-- para 55 -->
$$
DepartureRate_{f,t}=\sum_{k=-5}^{k=+5}\beta_{k}\times D_{k}\times NCAIncrease_{s,t}+\theta_{f}+\phi_{t}+\varepsilon_{f,t} \tag{5}
$$
<!-- para 56 -->
where  $D_k$ are time dummies relative to the NCA enforcement increase.
Subscript  $f$ represents firm–states in year  $t$.
The dependent variable DepartureRate is the aggregation of all mobility events divided by the number of unique inventors who patented in the last 10 years for a given firm–state.
I divide the variable DepartureRate into within- and across-industry mobility.
Within-industry mobility is defined as all total inventor mobility events moving to employers in the same SIC4 industry.
Outside-industry is defined as all total inventor mobility events to employers in a different SIC4 industry.
The denominator is measured as the number of unique inventors who patented for each firm–state in the last ten years.
The variables  $\theta$  and  $\phi$  are firm–state and year fixed-effects, respectively.
<!-- para 57 -->
The coefficients of interest are $\beta_{k}$ which capture the treatment indicator interacted with five pretreatment dummies and six posttreatment dummies.
To account for possible treatment effect heterogeneity, the equation is estimated using the imputation-based approach by Borusyak, Jaravel, and Spiess (2024).

It is ex-ante unclear whether to expect immediate or delayed employment choice effects.
However, a number of reasons point to a delayed response.
For example, the Florida law change in 1996 was explicitly only applicable to contracts signed after July 1, 1996. [^12] This would mean that only employees that start working after this date are exposed to increased NCA enforcement.
To increase the chances of legal protection, Ewens and Marx (2018) note that employers commonly require their employees to sign updated employment contracts.
This is supported (for the Georgia 2010 case) by Ewens and Marx (2018) who interviewed an employment attorney, who stated: “when the new law went into effect (including our firm), many employers revised their employment and restrictive covenant agreements to take advantage of the law.”
<!-- para 95 -->
Setting the legal point of view aside, there are additional considerations for a delayed response from the point of view of employees.
Inventors willing to move might not be well aware of the details of their NCA.
Employees might learn about the increased enforcement of NCAs years after.
There is no reason we should expect sudden effects, but rather an increase over time which leads to a new equilibrium in the labor market.
<!-- para 96 -->
Bishara (2011) extensively analyzes the legal background on the enforceability on NCAs.
He notes that it can be difficult to predict the consequences for a departing employee when she joins an out-of-state competitor.
It is thus often an open legal question to what extent individual NCAs are in fact enforceable.
It is important to note that thus, employees' reactions to NCA restrictions might well capture subjective choice set constraints, regardless of true legal restrictions.
The observed effects are thus best interpreted as the effects of subjective employee behavior rather than clear-cut labor regulatory constraints.
<!-- para 97 -->
Overall, the findings are consistent with interview evidence of Marx (2011), where employees admit to taking career detours given that their NCA prohibited them from working in similar industries for the next 1–2 years.
Marx (2011) interviewed one speech recognition professional who left the industry after being fired by his co-founder.
"Well, if I'm ever gonna leave, what would I do for 2 years if I couldn't do speech recognition?"

The dummy is equal to 0 if the industry is not concentrated, for example, there are many closely related firms.
The economic intuition behind this test is that employees in concentrated industries are more constrained and are forced to move to further distant product markets since employees in concentrated industries only have a few closely related employers to choose from.
In order to do so, I estimate equation (1) with an additional interaction term measuring industry concentration.

Second, using a so-called donut hole approach, I eliminate the five closest competitors and calculate a dummy based on the median average similarity of the next five closest competitors.

What are the effects on productivity if inventors move across industries in response to NCA enforcement increases?
On one hand, it might be beneficial for inventors' productivity if increased across-industry mobility leads to more idea recombination, and thus higher or more high-quality innovation output.
On the other hand, inventors might perform worse after an NCA-constrained industry move.
<!-- para 108 -->
To test these hypotheses, I use inventor-level measures of productivity.
I first construct a dummy variable, patent, equal to 1 if the inventor files for an eventually granted patent this year.
The second variable is the number of patents filed.
Third, I use the economic value of patents, and fourth, citation-weighted patents.
<!-- para 109 -->
Simply comparing inventors who move to another industry to those who do not would rely on a revealed choice.
Treatment and control inventors might not be comparable to each other and differ in meaningful ways whether observed or unobserved in ways other than whether they move to another industry.
To circumvent this problem, I abstract from the individual choice of inventors and estimate causal effects on future productivity based on an intent-to-treat (ITT) regression.
<!-- para 110 -->
Formally, the regression estimates equation (4).
The dependent variables are inventor productivity, measured whether the inventor patents, the number of patents, the economic value of patents, or citation-weighted patents.
The regression includes inventor fixed effects to control for time-invariant quality of the inventor.
The regression also includes State-Year fixed effects.
This is crucial, as the enforcement of NCAs might have negative effects on state-wide innovation output (Johnson, Lipsitz, and Pei 2023; Reinmuth and Rockall 2023).
<!-- para 111 -->
The regression is of an intent-to-treat form, as it does not use variation whether the inventor moves to another industry or not.
Rather it solely relies on high exposure to treatment.
High exposure is defined as inventors affected by increases in NCA enforcement interacted with a dummy for whether the inventor is likely bound by an NCA.
Based on the previous evidence, these inventors are most likely to move to another industry.

The increased number of across-industry mobility events following increased NCA enforcement might be involuntary.
In this case, NCA enforcement might lead to more dismissals and affected inventors are forced to find employment in further distant product markets.
If NCA enforcement leads to redundancies in the workforce, one would attribute lower future productivity wrongly to across industry mobility.
This alternative hypothesis is difficult to test, as inventor dismissals are not detected from patent data.
On the one hand, inventors are characterized by high human capital and possess bargaining power.
Such individuals are unlikely to face dismissal risk caused by redundancy.
Also, inventors in the sample continue to patent in their future employment; thus, they continue to innovate and are productive individuals.
To assess the likelihood of this alternative hypothesis, I test whether NCA enforcement is correlated with layoffs and discharges.
I obtain data from the Job Openings and Labor Turnover Survey, so-called JOLTS, provided by the U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics.
I obtain data on layoffs and discharges, which are available on a state level from 2000 to 2025.

To measure labor market matching quality, I compute characteristics of inventors as well as their new employers using patent data.
I analyze whether inventors move to firms that are less similar to them not just in a product market dimension, but in a technology dimension.
Patent data are valuable because each patent is classified into separate patent technologies, depending on the technology used.
Specifically, I calculate the following measure on technological similarity using patent data between inventor and her new employer:
<!-- para 133 -->
$$
\operatorname{techsimilarity}(i,f)=\frac{\mathbf{i}^{\top}\mathbf{f}}{||\mathbf{i}||\cdot||\mathbf{f}||}. \tag{6}
$$
<!-- para 134 -->

## discussion
<!-- para 2 -->
Inventors evade their NCAs by moving to new employers in more distant product markets.
NCA enforcement increases have a positive causal effect on the probability that an inventor moves across industries.
This effect holds in unconditional and conditional labor mobility regressions using a variety of industry definitions such as SIC, NAICS, or text-based industry classifications.
Inventors working for employers which likely rely on NCAs are most affected.
Overall, stronger NCA enforcement leads to some reallocation of human capital across product markets.
Such NCA-constrained industry switches have detrimental effects on future productivity of inventors.
I highlight how product market choice set constraints can have substantial effects reallocating human capital to more distant product markets.
