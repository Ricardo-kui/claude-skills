## introduction
<!-- para 1 -->
---
<!-- para 3 -->
On January 28, 1986, the National Aeronautics and Space Administration (NASA) experienced a tragic accident when the space shuttle _Challenger_ exploded soon after takeoff.
Seventeen years later, on February 1, 2003, NASA experienced the loss of a second space shuttle as the _Columbia_ disintegrated during reentry after a successful mission.
In spite of the dangers inherent to space exploration, the official investigations into both accidents concluded that they could have been prevented (U.S. Congress, House Committee on Science 1986, Columbia Accident Investigation Board (CAIB) 2003).
What is particularly notable, however, is not just that both accidents were preventable but that they were preventable for similar reasons.
<!-- para 4 -->
Both accidents, for example, originated from flaws in components (i.e., faulty O-rings and foam debris) that were known to individuals at NASA who were either ignored or felt unable to voice their concerns (CAIB 2003).
Also, in both cases, decreasing attention to anomalies in the components occurred (Vaughan 2005), while at the same time critical decisions were based on information generated by a flawed organizational structure and culture (CAIB 2003, Vaughan 2005).
In other words, beyond their distinct technical causes, both disasters resulted from a series of similarly flawed
<!-- para 5 -->
organizational processes.
Intriguingly, these processes were corrected in the immediate aftermath of the _Challenger_ accident, only to resurface and ultimately cause the _Columbia_ accident.
How could this happen to an organization, NASA, that employs some of the country’s best minds?
More specifically, how could the _Columbia_ tragedy occur when the organizational problems that significantly contributed to the _Challenger_ accident had been identified and were thought to have been fixed (Mahler and Hogan Casamayou 2009)?
<!-- para 6 -->
We believe that an important reason was NASA’s ability to learn, followed by its tendency to forget.
Although intuitive, this is a sequential phenomenon that learning theory has not dealt with in a systematic way.
In spite of providing many insights into the factors that facilitate or inhibit organizational learning (e.g., Argote 2011, 2013), existing theory is less clear about how learning and forgetting relate to each other, and also how they might combine to create serial errors.
That is, although we have good theory about organizations learning and good theory about organizations forgetting, we know little about why they might exhibit both tendencies in succession and how such cycles might be repeated.
<!-- para 7 -->
To understand why organizations cycle between learning and forgetting, we must identify the underlying factors that trigger shifts between these states.
We theo rize _serious errors_, defined as “organizational processes that result in a failure with significantly adverse outcomes,” as one source.
In particular, we identify a dual role for these adverse events that push organizations toward a focus on safety while pulling them away from competing foci, such as efficiency or innovation.
These alternative foci vary, depending on an organization’s core activities in a particular context.
As we will explicate in more detail, at NASA, the shift away from safety meant a shift toward efficiency (i.e., launching more shuttles at less expense).
In the pharmaceutical industry, the shift away from safety means a shift toward innovation (i.e., creating new drugs).
In both cases, the result of a serious error is a shift in emphasis to more safety-oriented behaviors.
Then, over time, organizational processes that promote forgetting, such as a false sense of security and employee turnover, cause a gradual shift away from safety toward other foci.
The ultimate result is another serious error, which refocuses the organization on safety, and the cycle begins again.
Although existing learning research recognizes organizational errors as disruptive (Madsen 2009) and deserving of study (Goodman et al. 2011), this dual effect has not previously been theorized.
<!-- para 8 -->
We argue that this oscillation occurs because, fundamentally, resources devoted to one activity cannot be devoted to another (Perrow 1984, Heimann 2005, Vaughan 2005), which creates tensions for any organization with limited resources.
In NASA’s case, choosing to focus on safety indicates a commitment to a set of behaviors that will deliver its product error-free (or nearly so).
In deciding to launch a shuttle, this constitutes support for the statement “we will not launch until proved safe to do so.” Choosing to focus on an alternative behavior, such as efficiency, however, indicates a commitment to operating in a productive fashion, adopting a set of behaviors that ensure that the product is delivered at maximum volume with minimum costs.
In terms of a shuttle launch, such a focus constitutes support for the statement “we will launch unless it is proved unsafe to do so.”1 Although we do not suggest that safety and efficiency are diametrically opposed and mutually exclusive, we do argue that an organization’s relative emphases on safety versus other foci are related and will shift over time.
<!-- para 9 -->
The result of this shift among foci is a pattern of organizational oscillation that this study seeks to model and test.
We do so by drawing on two very different contexts.
First, we use the example of NASA’s _Challenger_ and _Columbia_ accidents to develop theory about how mechanisms of learning and forgetting cause an organization to oscillate between safety and efficiency.
We then test this model empirically in the context of U.S. pharmaceutical firms that oscillated between safety and innovation as a result of serious drug errors.
We also test the consequences of oscillation for subsequent innovation in these firms.
<!-- para 10 -->
Our study therefore draws on both qualitative data to build theory and quantitative data to test that theory.
Following prior research, we rely on extreme examples (e.g., shuttle disasters) to inform our theoretical model because it is in such cases that the underlying dynamics and processes of our theory become more apparent (Eisenhardt 1989, Pettigrew 1990).
In particular, the process of organizational learning is stronger in the presence of serious errors than in more moderate ones (Madsen and Desai 2010).
The resulting model then serves as the basis for hypothesis generation and empirical testing in a different context, the pharmaceutical industry.
The advantage of this study design is that it provides a more comprehensive, grounded, and generalized test of new theory than either case study or deductive empirical work alone can provide (cf.
Eisenhardt and Graebner 2007).
<!-- para 11 -->
As a result, if we find that firms do oscillate between learning (toward safety) and forgetting (away from safety), our model will provide the foundation for a phenomenon that is not well explained by existing theory.
Predicting shifts between competing foci as a function of serious errors extends our knowledge of how organizations learn and then forget.
Showing that these shifts in foci subsequently affect innovation and future organizational errors indicates the consequential nature of this phenomenon.
<!-- para 13 -->
The literature on organizational learning is large and quite diverse in theoretical emphasis (Levitt and March 1988; Argote 2011, 2013).
The learning-curve phenomenon is one area that has received attention, with early work documenting that cumulative experience tends to generate efficiency gains in the form of increased production and diminished costs (e.g., Argote et al. 1990).
Yet learning curves show a substantial amount of variation, even across organizations performing the same task, with some learning quickly, some slowly, and some not at all (Lapré and Nembhard 2011).
<!-- para 14 -->
This is unsurprising if one considers the substantial research documenting the difficulties that organizations have with learning.
These problems include insufficient or flawed data (March et al. 1991, Beckman and Haunschild 2002), a tendency to search locally rather than more broadly (Levinthal and March 1993), issues in the distribution of success versus failure (Madsen and Desai 2010), and other factors that reduce the efficacy of experiential (Denrell 2003) or vicarious (Chandler and Hwang 2015) learning, such as the complex factors that inhibit learning from rare events (Lampel et al. 2009).
<!-- para 15 -->
Not only is knowledge hard to acquire, but organizations sometimes lose knowledge through processes of forgetting (e.g., Argote 2013).
In our study, “forgetting” is a term we use to represent processes that cause a shift away from safety focus, which may be caused by the absence of knowledge previously acquired (Martin de Holan and Nelson 2004) or by a shift in attention and emphasis (Ocasio 1997).
In other words, activities associated with an avoidance of serious errors are discarded or deemphasized, and a focus on production or innovation takes over at least some of these activities.
<!-- para 16 -->
**Organizational Learning from Serious Errors**
<!-- para 17 -->
In spite of the great strides that have been made in understanding what facilitates learning, what hinders it, and how knowledge decays, we know little about how organizations systematically cycle through periods of learning and forgetting.
Why does such oscillation occur?
We propose serious errors as a possible trigger that causes the organization to shift from a period of forgetting to a period of learning.
The role of errors as a disruptive influence is becoming well established within the organizational learning literature.
To this end, more recent work has moved the field away from a focus on cost reduction and productivity improvement to identify other outcomes of learning, including error reduction and accident avoidance (Haunschild and Sullivan 2002, Baum and Dahlin 2007, Madsen 2009).
Yet we know little about the causes of serial errors.
<!-- para 18 -->
Cases of serial errors include BP’s 2005 Texas oil refinery explosion that killed 15 people and injured 180 others.
Following this incident, the Chemical Safety and Hazard Investigation Board issued a caution to BP to avoid further cost cutting on safety and conducted a thorough two-year investigation of the company.
Despite these strong external pressures, five years later, BP had an explosion at the _Deepwater Horizon_ oil rig that claimed 11 lives and became one of the largest oil spills in history.
Revealingly, BP’s 2009 annual report suggests that this tragedy was preceded by a period when the firm prioritized operational efficiency and growth, focusing on oil exploration and production.
Further, a subsequent court ruling that BP was “grossly negligent” in the disaster states that the oil spill was caused by cost cutting despite safety risks (Gilbert and Scheck 2014).
Other instances of serial errors have been shown to occur in such diverse settings as hospitals (Twedt 1993), European telecommunications firms (Baumard and Starbuck 2005), and orbital launches (Madsen and Desai 2010).
<!-- para 19 -->
**NASA: Learning from _Challenger_ and _Columbia_**
<!-- para 20 -->
NASA’s space-shuttle program started in the early 1980s with a heavy focus on safety.
Then, a history of successful launches combined with political pressures to cut costs and increase productivity, reductions in safety personnel, and competition from the European Space Agency generated efficiency pressures that gradually weakened the safety focus.
This shift from safety to efficiency was identified as the primary cause of the _Challenger_ accident in 1986 (U.S. Congress, House Committee on Science 1986, Vaughan 1997).
Problems with
<!-- para 21 -->
shuttle launches, including specific technical components such as the O-rings, were “normalized” (Vaughan 1997), while NASA persisted with aggressive launch schedules, and cost cutting became paramount.
<!-- para 22 -->
After the _Challenger_ accident, NASA shifted its focus back to safety.
It implemented several resource-intensive programs that were designed to minimize the threat of future accidents and address the cultural and structural shortcomings identified by the accident investigations.
These programs focused on increasing both the number and the status of safety personnel in the organization, as well as strengthening safety operating procedures.
In particular, NASA focused on overcoming structural impediments to communication internally, especially concerning potential shuttle problems (U.S. Congress, House Committee on Science 1986).
Over time, however, NASA returned to a strong “culture of production” (Vaughan 2005, p. 43), emphasizing deadlines, schedules, and the avoidance of launch delays.
This shift in emphasis was manifested in specific changes, such as a reduction in the number of safety personnel (U.S. Congress, House Committee on Science 2003, CAIB 2003, Vaughan 2005), a decline in the status of those safety jobs that remained (Vaughan 2005), a return to the decentralized structure that was implicated as a direct cause of the _Challenger_ accident (Mahler and Hogan Casamayou 2009), and general turnover and loss of organizational knowledge associated with cost-cutting efforts (Vaughan 2005).
All of these factors are related to organizational forgetting (Argote et al. 2003).
In short, both external pressures (largely from public and regulatory agencies) and internal pressures (largely from concerns about reputation and the loss of lives) waned and were replaced with an attitude that the problems had been solved and that aggressive launch schedules and cost-cutting measures could resume.
For NASA, launching more shuttles had several advantages, including meeting commitments to the international space station, reducing costs per mission, and responding to political demands from Congress.
<!-- para 23 -->
Then, in 2003, the _Columbia_ shuttle disintegrated on reentry to the Earth’s atmosphere.
The technical cause was a piece of foam that broke off during launch, causing a hole in the left wing.
Although this was suspected shortly after launch, further investigation was severely discouraged within NASA (CAIB 2003, Bohmer et al. 2004, Vaughan 2005).
In fact, a request for additional information that would have helped to determine whether the foam strike had compromised the wing was cancelled.
Following _Columbia_, a focus on safety was reestablished, causing the international space station deadline to be adjusted and the establishment of major organizational changes based on recommendations from the CAIB report (Beck and Plowman 2014).
Yet even then, the “continuing pressures of the culture of production” returned as the memory of _Columbia_ receded (Vaughan 2005, p. 52).
<!-- para 24 -->
It is apparent that throughout the history of the shuttle program, NASA has cycled through periods where safety was of paramount importance and periods where efficiency was of paramount importance.
We suggest that this was due largely to the difficulties inherent in maximizing both safety and efficiency, together with the occurrence of errors that produced significantly adverse and salient outcomes.
What is fascinating about this story, which only emerges in retrospect, is the extent of the organizational memory loss that caused NASA to return to processes and structures that had been directly implicated in earlier problems, and which then contributed to a subsequent disaster.
In theorizing these cycles, therefore, we focus on the complex processes of organizational learning and forgetting that were triggered by these serious errors.
Beyond the distinct technical causes of the two shuttle crashes, it is the repeating organizational shifts in foci that produced them that are central to the oscillating patterns we observe.
<!-- para 26 -->
Oscillation between foci (such as safety and efficiency, or safety and innovation) occurs as a response to the management of competing tensions.
Anthropologists have long discussed oscillation between states as a natural outcome of trying to manage incommensurates: when two desirable states exist and an actor cannot be in both at the same time, oscillation tends to occur (e.g., Maybury-Lewis and Almagor 1989).
Documented examples of oscillation include Barley and Kunda’s (1992) work showing that managerial thought shifts over time between the ontologically incompatible normative and rational ideologies of control.
In addition, Adler et al. (1999) discuss the trade-off between efficiency and flexibility in production systems, and how one firm dealt with this by having its employees switch continuously between routine and nonroutine tasks.
Similarly, Tushman and Romanelli (1985) propose that, because firms tend toward exploiting current competencies and face challenges in achieving ambidexterity, organizational and technological evolution is marked by alternating periods of stasis and radical restructuring as a result of disruptive changes.
And Nickerson and Zenger (2002) discuss how firms shift between centralized and decentralized forms of control.
They do this because each form has significant advantages and disadvantages that are almost completely in opposition to each other.
Being in one state tends to highlight the costs of that state and the benefits of the unoccupied state.
<!-- para 27 -->
Within the learning and innovation literatures, prior work has also demonstrated that firms can shift from one focus of attention to another.
March (1991, p. 71), for example, famously notes that firms can move between “the exploration of new possibilities and the exploitation of old certainties,” and that, although a balance is
<!-- para 28 -->
ideal, in reality, firms “make explicit and implicit choices between the two.” Similarly, Abernathy and Utterback (1978) argue that firms shift from product innovation to process innovation as they mature, although their theory is one of linear progression rather than cyclical oscillation.
We add to this work by theorizing that firms not only shift from safety to a competing focus but also shift back again.
More importantly, we investigate the mechanisms underlying these changes.
We theorize serious errors as one source of behavioral switching that acts as a trigger to move the organization from a period of forgetting to a period of learning.
More specifically, we identify a dual role for serious errors that pushes the firm toward safety behaviors while pulling it away from the competing foci.
<!-- para 29 -->
NASA’s experiences with _Challenger_ and _Columbia_ constitute one example of an oscillation between efficiency and safety foci.
As noted, the two accidents had much in common.
They both involved faulty components (i.e., O-rings and foam), an issue that was initially taken quite seriously but, over time, was downgraded and “normalized” (Vaughan 1997, 2005).
This was particularly notable concerning foam incidents prior to _Columbia_ as the incident classification shifted from a “safety-offlight” issue to an “accepted risk” to “in-family.” In fact, the flight prior to _Columbia_ had a major foam strike that was supposed to be investigated before _Columbia_ was allowed to launch, but it was delayed (Bohmer et al. 2004, Vaughan 2005).
Although in both cases some NASA employees were aware of these issues, structural, cultural, and decision-making processes inhibited such concerns from being taken seriously (CAIB 2003, Bohmer et al. 2004).
In short, a close study of the effect of these accidents on the culture at NASA, combined with the previously mentioned oscillation theories, suggests the model of organizational oscillation shown in Figure 1.
<!-- para 30 -->
The model in Figure 1 suggests that organizations oscillate between _safety focus_, which is associated with avoiding Type I errors (i.e., avoiding doing something that might result in a serious error, such as launching a mission that might fail) and _nonsafety focus_, which is associated with avoiding Type II errors (i.e., avoiding not doing something that is indeed appropriate, such as not launching a mission that would be safe).
This oscillation is due to the tensions inherent when choosing among competing goals.
We predict that the underlying incompatibilities and resource constraints involving cognitive, structural, and human resources produce this oscillation pattern, with the pivot occurring in response to a serious error.
<!-- para 31 -->
The role of serious errors in the process is to stimulate organizations to move between periods of safety and periods when other motivating forces come to the fore.
The nonsafety force that fills the void when an organization shifts away from safety will vary, depending on the core activities of a particular context.
For NASA, a fundamental tension exists between safety and efficiency; in the pharmaceutical industry, the counterpoint to periods of safety is new drug discovery and development.
Again, it is worth emphasizing that the alternative behaviors that are encouraged when the firm moves away from safety are not assumed to be interchangeable with safety.
In reality, these are different behaviors that require different practices, leadership styles, and cultural values (Lewis et al. 2002, Miron et al. 2004, Naveh and Erez 2004).
<!-- para 32 -->
Similarly, it is not an assumption of our model that the firm’s safety and nonsafety behaviors are mutually exclusive.
Indeed, organizations may seek to emphasize both simultaneously and will likely exhibit a mix at any given point.
Having said this, we argue that there is an inherent trade-off between these foci such that, following a serious error, an organization cannot _maximize_ performance in both simultaneously.
In other words, we do not mean to imply that these organizational foci are dichotomous (i.e., either one or the other); rather, there are times when safety is prominent and times when other foci are prominent, and that the shift between these states reflects complex organizational processes of learning and forgetting.

## theory
<!-- para 2 -->
After a serious accident or operational error, safety is likely to dominate—both internal and external pressures will cause an increase in safety behavior relative to that before the accident.
External pressures likely stem from regulators, investigative boards (especially in public agencies), consumer watchdog groups, and the media who pressure firms to devote resources to discover what happened and prevent future occurrences (Carroll 1995).
The Rogers Commission (in the case of _Challenger_) and the CAIB (in the case of _Columbia_) both put tremendous pressure on NASA to examine and fix the problems that were identified in conjunction with agency personnel.
The _Deepwater Horizon_ oil spill in the Gulf of Mexico in 2010 offers another example of the scrutiny to which external constituents, such as
<!-- para 3 -->
the media and politicians, subject organizations following serious errors (Chandler 2015).
Similarly, Merck’s 2004 market withdrawal of Vioxx (a nonsteroidal antiinflammatory drug), as a result of increased risk of heart attack and stroke, received extensive media coverage and regulatory attention.
<!-- para 4 -->
Internal pressures post error are equally intense.
Firms can partially mitigate reputation harm through repair (Rhee and Valdez 2009).
Demonstrated increases in attention to safety aid this process.
In addition, internal learning can shift the focus from production to safety and error prevention.
Organizations learn more from failure than they do from success (Madsen and Desai 2010) because failure motivates search (Cyert and March 1963) and increases reflection (Morris and Moore 2000).
Organizations may enact procedures and hire new safety personnel in an effort to avoid future accidents.
These and other cases of increased attention to safety, however, are expensive.
As such, they come largely at the expense of investments in competing foci, such as efficiency and innovation.
<!-- para 5 -->
The insight that low performance results in greater attention by the firm and its top managers (Ocasio 1997), which leads to a change in behavior (Greve 1998), is well established in the learning literature.
Similarly, there has been some work looking at how organizations learn from rare events in terms of specific corrections (Lampel et al. 2009).
Moving beyond these insights, we propose that the behavioral change (i.e., a shift to safety focus) following a serious error is not proximal to the source of the error (as might be predicted by work on impression management or responses to institutional pressures) but rather is distributed firmwide.
In other words, we identify a broad effect that results in organizations making a full-scale, resource-intensive shift from one focus to another.
<!-- para 6 -->
Hypothesis 1 (H1). _Serious errors cause firms to increase safety focus._
<!-- para 7 -->
A firm’s safety focus, however, is likely to decline gradually over time, post error.
A move away from safety and the return to a prior focus (what we term “forgetting”) could result from various endogenous processes, such as the tendency to interpret error-free periods as success, a difficulty maintaining vigilance, and pressures to abandon expensive safety measures.
The NASA case provides examples of all of these.
<!-- para 8 -->
In general, error-free periods tend to be interpreted as an indication that a problem has been solved and resources can be reallocated away from safety (Heimann 2005).
This occurred during the post-_Challenger_ period, for example, a number of years when no serious accidents occurred.
As a result, incidents such as foam loss, which happened frequently in the run-up to the _Columbia_ launch, were dismissed as potential problems (Vaughan 2005).
In actuality, NASA was fortunate that foam strikes never hit a critical part of a shuttle before _Columbia_, yet the role of luck in error-free periods tends to be systematically underestimated (Dillon and Tinsley 2008).
As periods of error-free success accumulate, assessment updates gradually reallocate resources from safety to operations.
Perceived success also tends to lead to inertia and insularity (Miller 1994), which can prevent learning and further discourage investments in safety.
Additional evidence for this effect can be found in the cyclicality of bank-lending practices, where riskier loans are made at the peak of an economic cycle and more conservative loans at the trough (Berger and Udell 2004).
<!-- para 9 -->
Another explanation for a decline in safety behavior over time involves the difficulty in maintaining vigilance—especially when error prevention is a nonevent and production results are more salient (Weick 1987, Perrow 1994).
Sustaining attention is difficult and success can decrease attention to warning signals (Rerup 2009).
Strong safety buffers, therefore, can actually increase the likelihood of errors, either by distancing operators from the problems as they unfold or by inciting boredom on the job (Perrow 1984, 1994).
Related ideas from the learning literature include organizational memory (Walsh and Ungson 1991) and the dissipation of organizational knowledge (Argote et al. 1990, Martin de Holan and Nelson 2004).
Memory loss can occur because of turnover (Argote and Miron-Spektor 2011, Argote 2013) or the passage of time combined with faulty or inadequate retention systems (Day 1994).
At NASA, we know that substantial turnover in safety personnel occurred while the organization was being reorganized between the two accidents (Vaughan 1997).
We also know that case evidence about NASA’s activities during this period shows that failures in learning led to a period of “safety drift” (Farjoun 2005).
This resulted in a reallocation of resources to a focus on efficiency, which, in turn, led to a further erosion of safety (Woods 2005).
<!-- para 10 -->
And finally, a decline in safety focus post error can occur because of the abandonment of safety measures established after a previous error.
Abandonment may occur for several reasons, including efficiency and costcutting pressures.
After the _Challenger_ accident, for example, the Rogers Commission recommended that NASA shift to a more centralized launch-decision structure coordinated by headquarters personnel (Mahler and Hogan Casamayou 2009).
The Rogers Commission had noted that NASA’s decentralized structure was generating poor coordination and information flow across centers (all of whom had launch-critical information), a communication problem that was exacerbated by intercenter rivalry and cultural differences.
Despite those problems, NASA reverted to decentralization during a wave of downsizing in the mid-1990s.
This meant that poor coordination returned, accompanied by poor information flows (CAIB 2003, Mahler and Hogan Casamayou 2009).
It was this decentralization that, at
<!-- para 11 -->
least partially, prevented further investigation into the damage caused by the foam break on _Columbia_.
<!-- para 12 -->
Hypothesis 2 (H2). _The positive effect of serious errors on safety focus declines over time, post error._
<!-- para 13 -->
Central to our theory is the idea that organizations face a fundamental tension when selecting among behaviors that compete for the same scarce resources.
Instead of merely assuming that the increase in safety behavior following a serious error automatically leads to a decrease in nonsafety behavior, however, we use our remaining hypotheses to examine the underlying mechanisms by which this shift might occur.
Although external and internal pressures prompted NASA to oscillate between efficiency and safety, in the pharmaceutical industry (and, we suspect, other high-tech, research and development (R&D)-oriented industries), the predominant focus competing with safety is new product innovation, an activity requiring massive, long-term investments.
<!-- para 14 -->
Firms’ attempts to introduce new drugs that are both efficacious and safe entail an inherent trade-off that is characterized by high uncertainty.
Despite extensive evidence that firms garner in clinical trials,2 the intrinsic quality of new drugs is often not fully observable at the time of U.S. Food and Drug Administration (FDA) review.
Some adverse effects associated with a new drug have very low incidence rates, for example, whereas some populations are not well represented in standard clinical trials.
As such, it is widely believed that more trials and more testing will result in more problem discovery and the correction of problems pre- and postlaunch (Maor 2011).
Thus, there is an inherent trade-off between safety (devoting resources to more testing to make sure that a new drug is safe) and innovation (devoting resources to exploring new drugs and applications).
We suggest that this firmwide trade-off is affected by the occurrence of serious errors related to specific drugs in a firm’s portfolio.
Such a trade-off occurs not only because more resources allocated to one focus implies fewer resources allocated to the other but also because the activities associated with a stronger safety focus may lead the firm to hold back on activities that can lead to potential innovations.
<!-- para 15 -->
As argued in motivating H1, serious errors cause an increase in safety focus, which will likely lead to a decrease in innovation.
This decrease occurs because of the associated resource trade-off.
A strong system of routines and structures that support new drug development does not adjust readily to safety processes that add costs, delay time to market, and reduce a firm’s tolerance for risk (Nelson 1991, McGrath 1997).
Regulatory and public attention means that firms must devote resources to investigating current errors and preventing future errors, which means fewer resources devoted to exploring new drugs.
We do not expect this effect to be confined locally, however.
In response to a serious error, we anticipate that a return to a safety focus will cause pharmaceutical firms to investigate the safety of all the drugs currently in their pipeline, running additional checks in an effort to prevent another error.
<!-- para 16 -->
Evidence collected from firms’ annual reports confirms this tension between periods when firms’ priorities lie in pushing innovation and periods when ensuring drug safety becomes the primary concern.
In 2005, for example, Amgen’s executives reported that the firm was “in the process of launching several new potential blockbuster products” (Amgen 2005, p. 2).
The following year, however, following “setbacks in clinical trials” that “raised some complex questions about drug safety,” the company was “working with medical experts and regulatory authorities to help ensure that treatment decisions are made based on sound science” (Amgen 2006, p. 3).
<!-- para 17 -->
Hypothesis 3 (H3). _Serious errors cause firms to decrease innovation focus._
<!-- para 18 -->
In H2, we propose that the positive effect of serious errors on safety focus declines over time, post error.
If that is true, we should expect to see the negative effect of serious errors on innovation focus also decline over this same period.
As time passes, regulatory and public attention to the error are likely to decline.
This means that, gradually, the firm will feel free to take greater risks, refocusing resources away from safety and toward product innovation.
This process is likely facilitated by some of the earlier-mentioned endogenous processes that occur after an error: the tendency to see problems as having been solved, the normalization of deviance, and the abandonment of practices geared toward safety.
<!-- para 19 -->
Hypothesis 4 (H4). _The negative effect of serious errors on innovation focus declines over time, post error._
<!-- para 20 -->
As we elaborated in H2 and H4, the role of errors in pushing an organization toward and away from safety fades over time.
Simply extending these arguments to their logical conclusion, we arrive at the corollary that, as a firm’s safety focus (which is stronger following a serious error) gradually fades, that firm becomes more vulnerable to a subsequent serious error.
Concurrently, as a firm’s nonsafety focus (which is attenuated in response to a serious error) recovers its prior impetus, the firm is likely to become more susceptible to another serious error.
This gradual weakening of the effects of serious errors thus contributes to completing our oscillation model, in which an organization initially sets out to ensure greater safety but ultimately experiences another serious error, as in the NASA case.
<!-- para 22 -->
We test the propositions derived from our theory of oscillation between learning (safety) and forgetting (nonsafety; in this case, innovation) using an extensive data set from the pharmaceutical industry, where these two foci are highly salient.
Maintaining a focus on creating pharmaceutical innovations is vital to the survival of firms in this setting (Henderson and Cockburn 1994, Cockburn et al. 2000).
Attention to innovation results from the fact that the introduction of generic versions of drugs following the expiration of corresponding patents drastically erodes a firm’s competitive advantage, inflicting as much as a US$60 billion loss for the top pharmaceutical firms, from 2007 to 2012 (DeRuiter and Holston 2012).
This imperative compels firms to replenish their R&D pipelines constantly with innovations (Caves et al. 1991)—an approach to business that is resource intensive since the costs associated with the development of each new drug are estimated to amount to more than US$800 million (DiMasi et al. 2003).
<!-- para 23 -->
Safety is also critical to the success of pharmaceutical firms.
Because drugs are required to be tested before being introduced to market, firms must balance the incentive to innovate with the recognition that dangerous drugs will not succeed in the marketplace.
As such, an important focus of firms’ development activities has rested on clinical trials to gather scientific evidence of each drug’s efficacy and safety.
Another feature of the pharmaceutical industry relevant to our study is the ability to observe serious drug errors, thus enabling investigation of how their occurrence exerts a dual influence (pushing a firm toward and away from safety).
These drug errors comprise cases in which evidence about a new or existing drug raises safety-related concerns about adverse effects that outweigh its potential merits, thus resulting in its withdrawal from the market or significantly limiting the scope of potential users.
In some cases, evidence about a drug’s side effects results in the addition of a “boxed warning” to that drug’s label highlighting safety concerns.
In other cases, evidence about a drug’s effects can result in it being “contraindicated” for use by a particular type of patients.

<!-- para 24 -->
The pharmaceutical context therefore represents a good test of our theory because, by examining serious drug errors, we can go beyond the qualitative evidence from a single organization (i.e., NASA) to conduct large-scale quantitative analyses across a whole industry.3 An important consideration here is that drug errors, although potentially very serious, occur more frequently than shuttle accidents.
This enables us to collect fine-grained, longitudinal data to examine the dual effect of drug errors pushing firms toward safety and away from innovation, and the fading of this effect over time.
<!-- para 26 -->
Our sample comprises all pharmaceutical firms that introduced new drugs under FDA oversight and that were at hazard of experiencing drug-error events from 1997 to 2004, totaling 146 firms.
The analysis begins in 1997 because that is when data about clinical trials started to be reported in a systematic fashion.
A Freedom of Information Act request submitted in 2005 was the means for collecting data on new FDA-approved drugs (Polidoro 2013).
In its reply, the FDA noted that the agency does not release data on new drug applications that failed to receive regulatory approval.
To collect data on those instances in which the FDA denied approval to a new drug, we followed prior studies (e.g., Bosch and Lee 1994, Sharma and Lacey 2004) and searched LexisNexis for news on the voluntary announcement of nonapproval letters issued by the FDA to firms whose drug had received regulatory approval.
The Center for Drug Evaluation and Research Report to the Nation was the source of data on safety-based withdrawals.
The source of data on serious adverse effects associated with drugs that resulted in a revision of the corresponding labels was MedWatch, the FDA Safety Information and Adverse Event Reporting Program.
<!-- para 27 -->
Following the FDA Modernization Act of 1997, the National Library of Medicine of the U.S. National Institutes of Health developed a website, in collaboration with the FDA, containing information on clinical trials, including those sponsored by private industry.
This was the source of data on the clinical trials sponsored by sample firms.
Web of Science provided data on scientific articles by researchers affiliated with sample firms.
<!-- para 28 -->
For each firm, we collected all references that contained the firm name in the author-address field, thus indicating that at least one author was affiliated with that firm.
The databases of the U.S. Patent and Trademark Office (USPTO) were the source for information on the firms’ patents.
The _Pharmacy Times_ furnished annual data on the top 200 best-selling drugs.
Finally, we collected data about each firm’s mergers and acquisitions (M&A) activity from the Securities Data Company data set, LexisNexis, and the firms’ websites.
All variables were corrected to account for M&A.
The final sample in our longitudinal analysis comprises 923 firm-year observations.
<!-- para 30 -->
_Safety Focus_ (H1 and H2)_._ To measure a firm’s safety focus, we used data on the clinical trials a firm conducts.
Clinical trials constitute the firm’s main efforts to determine drug safety and require a significant investment of resources, including the time devoted to cultivating relationships with the research institutes, hospitals, and physicians who facilitate access to a pool of patients that is appropriate to the scope of the investigation.4 Importantly, a firm has discretion in the emphasis it places on the conduct of clinical trials and, as such, can adjust this emphasis over time according to its operational priorities.
This ability to prioritize is indicated by the news media, which recently reported, for example, that the chief executive officer of Boston Scientific “plans to better focus on growth initiatives, and is done spending on big studies that benefit competitors” (Kamp 2010).
<!-- para 31 -->
To measure a firm’s safety focus in a given year, we created a variable containing the number of clinical trials that a firm started in that year.
Importantly, this variable considers trials about any of a firm’s drugs.
In other words, it captures the firm’s generalized concern with safety-related issues, as opposed to a focused response to errors affecting any particular drug.
As such, our analysis examines whether, following the occurrence of a specific safety-related issue with _one drug_, a firm extends its increased safety focus to _all drugs_.
As we discuss later, our analyses control for a firm’s number of new drug applications in order to account for the possibility that a firm with more drugs in the pipeline conducts more trials.
Also, as we report later, the findings are robust to an alternative measure containing the average number of patients enrolled in a firm’s clinical trials, while controlling for the total number of trials.
<!-- para 32 -->
We also ran models using an alternative measure of safety focus.
Sometimes a firm’s concern with investigating the safety of its drug goes beyond clinical trials to the publication of the corresponding results in scientific articles.
Such activity entails costs in addition to the resources devoted to encourage its researchers to publish in scientific journals (Henderson and Cockburn 1994) because such publications can result in the leakage of knowledge that may facilitate rivals’ attempts to create imitative drugs (Danzon 2000) or to refine drugs available in their library of chemical components (Thomke and Kuemmerle 2002).
Even when rivals already have generic knowledge about the pharmacological mechanism underlying a drug, they may benefit from novel information about a drug’s specific features, such as its interaction effects with other drugs or the scope of applicability that scientific articles about clinical trials typically contain (Polidoro and Theeke 2011).
A firm’s decision to publicly disclose information about the safety of its drug in scientific journals, as opposed to privately communicating such information to the relevant entities, thus likely reflects the extent to which the firm emphasizes drug safety.
As such, we also ran our analyses using scientific articles about clinical trials as a measure of safety focus.
This alternative variable contains the number of scientific articles whose references mentioned clinical trials in the title or abstract fields and which were published by authors affiliated with the focal firm in the observation year.
<!-- para 33 -->
_Innovation Focus_ (H3 and H4)_._ To measure a firm’s innovation focus, we followed prior research that uses patent counts as an indicator of innovation output (e.g., Griliches 1990, Patel and Pavitt 1995).
We created a measure with the number of a firm’s successful patent applications in the observation year.
The advantage of using
<!-- para 34 -->
this measure is that, as noted in motivating our hypotheses, rather than assume that safety and innovation foci are diametrically opposite and mutually exclusive, we can observe them independently and examine empirically the dual effect that errors exert on them.
Moreover, drawing on patent applications as an alternative test of our theory is instructive because the focus of USPTO assessments is distinct from more nuanced considerations about the efficacy/safety trade-off that guides FDA assessments, such that a setback with the FDA review of a new drug may not necessarily inform how USPTO officers evaluate whether an invention meets the requirements for a patent to be issued.
Additionally, the pharmaceutical industry is an innovation-driven context where firms rely heavily on patents to protect their innovations (Cohen et al. 2000).
As such, the exclusionary rights associated with patents are valued by firms in light of the competitive pressures that lead rivals to produce imitative drugs (Danzon 2000).
Failure to prosecute a patent in a timely fashion can thus lead to difficulties in obtaining a patent subsequently, given that rivals’ related patents raise the hurdles to the requirement of novelty.
Besides their effects on blocking imitation and preventing infringement lawsuits, patents in the pharmaceutical industry also provide a means for licensing agreements and enhancing a company’s reputation (Cohen et al. 2000).
<!-- para 36 -->
_Serious Drug Error._ To observe the occurrence of significantly adverse errors in the pharmaceutical context (see Table 1), we used multiple measures.
First, safetybased drug withdrawals are arguably the most serious events and occur when “there is an intrinsic property of a drug that makes it necessary to withdraw the drug from the market for safety reasons” (Center for Drug Evaluation and Research 2005, p. 43).
A drug withdrawal causes a firm to suffer reputational damage and revenue loss since it can no longer commercialize that drug.
As such, we propose that these events engender the internal and external pressures that result in the firm increasing its safety focus.
<!-- para 37 -->
A second type of safety-related drug error occurs when a firm submits a new drug for FDA approval but does not receive approval for market introduction.
The nonapproval of a new drug by the FDA has clear financial consequences, given the firm’s inability to commercialize the innovation in spite of a significant investment in its discovery and development.
Moreover, the FDA may subject subsequent innovations by the same firm to tougher scrutiny.
Prior research demonstrates that these costs result in a significant loss of stockholders’ wealth (Bosch and Lee 1994, Sharma and Lacey 2004).
<!-- para 38 -->
A third class of events that we consider when measuring drug errors is boxed warnings and contraindications that are added to a drug’s label.
Besides impairing the firm’s ability to capture economic value by diminishing the scope of potential users, these events can also tarnish the firm’s reputation for providing safe therapies, which can indirectly affect the success of other drugs.
<!-- para 39 -->
As discussed earlier, a relevant feature of the pharma context is that we can observe errors that occur more frequently than the shuttle disasters that occurred at NASA.
Although serious drug errors are relatively more frequent, however, they still constitute a rather discrete outcome—only about 10% of the observations in the final sample (92 of 923) were affected by a serious drug error, and only about 2% of those observations (19 of 923) exhibited the incidence of more than one serious drug error.
As these figures suggest, the relevant variation we can observe empirically is the one between observations with no serious drug error and those affected by such errors.
Given the discrete nature of these events and the fact that these errors are quite serious and, thus, the incidence of an error is likely to determine the response (rather than the number of errors), we measured serious drug error as a dummy variable set to 1 if a firm experienced one of these serious safety-related events in a given year and to 0 otherwise.
<!-- para 41 -->
The analysis controls for potential sources of heterogeneity across observations that might influence both the independent and dependent variables.
In models estimating a firm’s safety focus, we controlled for its innovation; conversely, in models estimating a firm’s innovation focus, we controlled for its safety.
To account for the possibility that a firm introducing a greater number of new drugs is more likely to attend to safety and also more likely to experience a serious drug error, we added a variable with the number of new drugs that a firm submitted to FDA approval in a given year.
To address the concern that firms vary the extent to which they focus on exploitation or exploration, and that such variation could be confounded with the oscillation between safety and innovation foci, we added a variable to our models capturing the proportion of the total new drugs that a firm introduced that the FDA classified as new molecular entities (the other ones being those classified by the FDA as incrementally modified drugs).
We also controlled for the potential influence stemming from a firm’s scientific acumen (or any other factors that affect its propensity to engage in the publication of scientific articles) by including a variable with yearly counts of scientific publications by researchers affiliated with that firm.
Further, there is the possibility that a firm with a greater number of commercially successful drugs is at a higher hazard of experiencing a serious safety-related event and that its commercial success somehow affects either its safety or its innovation focus.
The analysis accounted for this likely influence by adding a variable with the number of
<!-- para 42 -->
a firm’s drugs that the _Pharmacy Times_ ranked among the 200 best-selling drugs in the United States.
Firms that undergo M&A activities may readjust their safety focus and innovation focus as they promote the integration of the acquired companies and may also be subject to a higher incidence of serious drug errors.
To control for this possibility, we added a variable with the number of a firm’s mergers and acquisitions in a given year.
A final possibility is that, as a result of vicarious learning (Haunschild and Sullivan 2002, Baum and Dahlin 2007, Madsen 2009), a firm might react to drug errors that other firms experience and that such events in the industry might also affect the incidence of an error in that firm.
To account for this potential influence, we included a variable with the number of sample firms that experienced drug errors in a given year.
<!-- para 43 -->
Our theory also proposes that the influence of a serious error on a firm’s safety/innovation focus diminishes over time.
Note, however, that our arguments pertain to the moderating role of time elapsed since an error and not to its main effect.
As we discuss later, we test these propositions through graphical analysis and formal econometric tests of differences in marginal effects across time.
To ensure that the results we report in the paper are the net of the influence of the main effect of time elapsed, we entered this variable as a control.
Moreover, as we report below, we adopted a model specification that accounted for any additional time-invariant heterogeneity across sample firms that the aforementioned controls might not capture.
By accounting for such firm heterogeneity, the analysis examines influences of within-firm variation over time in terms of drug errors on a firm’s safety focus and innovation focus.
All independent and control variables were lagged by one year to ensure temporal precedence.
<!-- para 45 -->
Both dependent variables, safety focus and innovation focus, are count variables, which violate the assumption of homoskedastic, normally distributed error terms.
Although Poisson and negative binomial models can be used to estimate influences on count variables (Hausman et al. 1984), Poisson models can produce underestimated standard errors and spuriously high significance levels when the assumption of equality between the mean and the variance is violated.
As a result, our analysis adopts a negative binomial specification (Wooldridge 2002).
Models account for random effects across firms.

Further, the analysis shows that several controls have a significant impact on safety focus and on innovation focus, a number of which are particularly interesting.

Firms experiencing M&A seem to move away from safety focus.
Results in Models 1 and 2 show that the main effect of time elapsed since a drug error on the number of clinical trials is significantly positive.
This result is arguably driven by observations of firms that did not experience a serious drug error during the period of analysis, in which case this result captures an overall tendency over time toward more clinical trials.
As we discussed above, however, such time lapse attenuates the influence of drug errors on safety focus.
<!-- para 14 -->
In addition to the models reported above, we conducted a variety of supplemental analyses to address potential concerns that might interfere with the interpretation of our findings.5 A potential concern is that, although all models included a variety of control variables to capture sources of heterogeneity across observations and accounted for firm random effects, there might remain some unobserved time-varying firm attributes that correlate with both the incidence of serious drug errors and the levels of safety or innovation focus that firms maintain.
Further, the controls do not fully rule out the possibility of reverse causality.
These possibilities imply that observations might be nonrandomly assigned to levels of the independent variable of interest, thereby resulting in biased estimates (Holland 1986).
To address these issues, we ran a supplemental analysis adopting the instrumental variable approach in a two-stage estimation (Wooldridge 2002).
That is, we predicted the likelihood of a drug error with the potentially endogenous variables and an exogenous instrument in the first stage, and then we examined the effect of the lag of the predicted value on a firm’s safety focus and innovation focus.
The results of the two-stage estimation of influences on safety and innovation focus remained robust, suggesting that our findings were not driven either by the possibility of residual omitted-variable bias or by potential reverse causality.
<!-- para 15 -->
In addition, the models predicting safety focus include information about innovation focus (and vice versa) because our theory suggests that firms face a trade-off between the two.
Moreover, we also propose that both are related to errors.
Hence, it is important that models predicting one focus account for the potential influence of the alternative focus to correct for the potential omitted-variable bias.
To ensure that findings were not driven by collinearity involving the respective variables, we also ran models dropping the alternative focus from the equation.
Both approaches showed fully robust results, thus increasing confidence that the findings are not merely driven by collinearity issues.
<!-- para 16 -->
Finally, we probed further into an implication of our oscillation model—because a safety focus reduces the incidence of errors, as it gradually phases out the firm becomes more vulnerable to a subsequent error.
Similarly, as a firm’s innovation focus progressively recovers, that firm likely becomes more susceptible to a serious error.
Although formally testing such extensions of our theory is inordinately challenging given the implicit endogeneity issues, as we detail in the online appendix, our study reveals both qualitative and quantitative evidence suggestive of these effects.
As such, in additional analyses using an accelerated-event time Weibull specification (which accounts for the monotonic effect of time as well as left-censoring), we examined influences on the time elapsed before the occurrence of an error.
Results offered evidence that the greater the number of a firm’s clinical trials, the longer the time elapsed before a drug error; conversely, the greater the firm’s innovative output, the shorter the period elapsed before a drug error.
In other words, by gradually moving away from a safety focus toward an innovation focus, a firm seeds the roots of a subsequent serious error.
We emphasize that this evidence is not conclusive, but it does suggest that a full oscillation cycle occurs—errors lead to a safety focus, which declines over time to be replaced by an innovation focus, which increases the likelihood of the next error, and the cycle repeats.

**Discussion**
<!-- para 18 -->
The results of this study provide strong support for our theory that organizations vary systematically over time in their focus on safety-related behaviors and on non-safety-related behaviors (such as efficiency or innovation)—variance that is predicted by serious errors and by how much time has passed since the error occurred.
Our empirics demonstrate it is both difficult and rare for an organization to be at the high end of both safety and innovation simultaneously.
Our data, across different contexts, provide compelling evidence that firms’ emphasis on safety ebbs and flows, and that periods when safety is prominent are distinct (and consequential) from other periods.
Relating back to the analogy of Type I and Type II errors, our evidence suggests that organizations cannot minimize both error types simultaneously.
<!-- para 19 -->
Specifically, our results demonstrate that serious organizational errors increase safety focus and decrease innovation focus.
Thus, there is a trade-off between periods of learning (a focus on safety) and periods of forgetting (a focus on efficiency/innovation) that causes organizations to oscillate between the two states.
Serious errors increase safety focus, but this focus declines over time.
Pharmaceutical firms experiencing serious errors increase the testing of their drugs and publish more scientific articles about clinical trials; however, over time, this caution and investigation into potential problems declines.
Serious errors also cause firms to decrease their innovation activities, but this effect also declines with time since the error occurred as the focus gradually shifts back to innovating so that new drugs can be released to the market.
In identifying this central triggering role of serious errors, our work responds directly to the call for greater research on both the symptomatic antecedents and the firm-level consequences of organizational errors, including a specific focus on “assessing the role of context” (Goodman et al. 2011, p. 173).
<!-- para 20 -->
**Limitations and Areas for Future Research**
<!-- para 21 -->
Several caveats must be taken into account in interpreting our findings.
First, our model in Figure 1 is obviously stylized.
The regularity of the cycling through
<!-- para 22 -->
periods of safety focus and nonsafety focus will likely vary, both within and across firms.
Although we know that safety focus declines over time, we do not know exactly how much time will elapse.
Further, both the extent of change and the starting location of any given firm in the cycle are also likely to vary.
These issues present interesting areas for future research, however, as there may be systematic factors that affect the timing, location, shape, and periodicity of the oscillation cycle.
Firms whose serious errors are more hidden from the public and regulators, for example, may have shallower oscillation cycles than the highly visible crises that we studied.
<!-- para 23 -->
Similarly, although we demonstrate that firms cannot be at the high end of both safety focus and efficiency/innovation focus at the same time, there may well be significant differences across organizations in how much of each outcome they can simultaneously obtain and for how long they can sustain that mix.
A fruitful extension of our model would be to examine variation _across_ organizations in their ability to combine safety and innovation (as opposed to examining _within_organization variation) or to investigate variation across organizations in terms of how long any particular focus can be sustained.
<!-- para 24 -->
It is also true that what we are showing is a relative, rather than absolute, increase in safety and nonsafety.
Although there is an empirically identifiable shift from one behavior to the other, we do not mean to imply that firms focused on efficiency have no interest in safety.
What we show is that wherever a firm is in its safety/nonsafety mix, errors alter this state (increasing safety and decreasing nonsafety), and that this impact will decline over time.
Since we control for firm-level effects, any factor that affects where a firm is in its safety/nonsafety mix is accounted for in our results.
And it is unlikely that safety focus declines because firms become more efficient at being safe.
Drug trials and drug research leading to scientific articles are expensive, with few or no cumulative efficiency benefits.
In addition, as time passes, drug problems are more likely to occur.
The fact that firms shift their focus away from safety over time buttresses the idea that there are internal and external pressures that drive the oscillation pattern, and the effect we identify is not due simply to the passage of time or because firms get more efficient in their safety procedures.
<!-- para 25 -->
It is also worth noting that we find this oscillation effect in two highly regulated industries with strong safety norms.
Yet even organizations focused on safety experience crises that can cause a shift from forgetting to learning (and back again).
In essence, therefore, NASA and the pharmaceutical industry represent conservative settings from which to derive and test our model.
We would therefore expect the effects we found to be even more likely in organizations that do not have a strong safety focus embedded in their procedures.
As such, the opportunity remains for future research to examine oscillation between behavioral foci in settings where organizations manifest different types of efficiency or innovation focus.
<!-- para 26 -->
**Theoretical Contributions and Potential for Future Research**
<!-- para 27 -->
Notwithstanding these caveats, our study has important implications for theories of learning and work on organizational errors, as well as for understanding the factors that facilitate and inhibit innovation.
<!-- para 28 -->
_Learning Theory and Research on Errors._ This study both violates and bridges traditional accounts of organizational learning and responses to errors on two counts.
First, most scholars have examined variance in behavior _across_ firms and focused on explaining either how firms learn or fail to learn from their experience (see Lapré and Nembhard 2011 for a review).
In contrast to the notion that some firms are highly reliable and resilient whereas others are not (Weick et al. 1999), our study examines variation _within_ firms over time and shows that they cycle through periods more conducive to high reliability and resilience and others less conducive to these processes.
Competing tensions between Type I (safety focus) and Type II (nonsafety focus) error avoidance, which are triggered by serious errors, appear to underwrite these cycles.
A promising path for future research, therefore, is to examine how oscillation is affected either by the amount of prior experience or by a comparison between experiential and vicarious learning.
We found, for example, that external errors (a control variable) increase the safety focus of pharmaceutical firms.
It also appears that this effect, although significant, is weaker than the effect of the firm’s own experience—a result that is consistent with recent work on vicarious learning from major errors (Madsen 2009).
<!-- para 29 -->
Clearly, in our model, both internal and external pressures play a role in a firm’s oscillation between learning and forgetting.
Although we account for the relative degree of scrutiny exerted by the FDA in our analyses (in that greater scrutiny should result in the detection of more drug errors in the industry overall), a fascinating avenue for future research is to examine how coevolution in a given field affects organizational learning (and forgetting).
For instance, outside entities that typically impose external pressures on firms experiencing errors (e.g., regulators and the media) may also shift their focus from some types of serious errors to other types.
We observe, for example, how the media maintains a focus on a tragic occurrence, such as a major oil spill or a major car recall associated with casualties, only gradually to weaken that focus as other serious issues emerge (e.g., Chandler 2014).
Understanding the antecedents of these shifts in external pressures on organizations that
<!-- para 30 -->
have committed errors can lead to a more comprehensive picture of how organizations shift between foci (i.e., how they learn and forget) in succession.
<!-- para 31 -->
Second, another contribution of our study is to existing work on organizational memory (Walsh and Ungson 1991, Martin de Holan and Nelson 2004).
By identifying specific processes by which firms forget, which then leads to subsequent errors, we contribute to knowledge of how and why organizational memories fail.
We also contribute to work on organizational errors by showing that these failures occur as a result of forgetting processes that shift behavior away from safety, and that these shifts generate serial serious errors.
<!-- para 32 -->
_Organizational Innovation._ This study also furthers our understanding of the challenges firms face in innovating.
Extant literature emphasizes a variety of factors, such as the path-dependent nature of knowledge accumulation, the embedding of innovations in routines, and the attendant cognitive traps that influence organizations’ abilities to innovate, especially when drawing on novel knowledge to create new products (e.g., Tushman and Anderson 1986, Levinthal and March 1993).
Our analysis points to a conceptually distinct influence on the innovation outcomes a firm achieves, one that does not stem from the locus of learning (e.g., existing or novel, internal or external) but rather from shifts between safety and nonsafety foci.
Because the types of responses to errors that this study underlines are organizational, rather than product specific, a firm’s location on the safety– innovation continuum affects the extent to which it successfully exploits a particular technology.
That is, sometimes what may hold a firm back from pursuing important discoveries is not its inability to absorb the corresponding knowledge but rather a prevailing concern with getting new products out in an error-free manner.
<!-- para 33 -->
A model of organizational oscillation provides additional insights for future innovation research.
Because our model differs distinctly from prior work on both punctuated equilibrium (which captures a drastic shift, such as a fundamental change in technology) and exploration–exploitation (which is more of a linear, rather than cyclical, conceptualization), it raises important questions about how these different models link together.
Shifts toward safety, for example, may affect the relative weights an organization places on the potential upside and likely downside of different technologies such that, following a serious error, it may forgo exploration and continue to exploit a proven technology.
Future studies can advance our understanding in this respect by investigating further how firms balance these tensions by oscillating between safety and nonsafety foci.
In this vein, the results we present should be of interest to scholars who study organizational ambidexterity (for reviews, see Birkinshaw and Gupta 2013 and O’Reilly and Tushman 2013).
Because we investigate the tension between innovation and safety (as opposed to the tensions within innovation itself), an opportunity for future research would be to study whether the associated trade-offs change as firms shift their innovation approach from exploitation to exploration.
<!-- para 34 -->
**Conclusion**
<!-- para 35 -->
Overall, in addition to these contributions, we hope that our oscillation model provides a foundation for more generalizable theory about how organizations shift back and forth between different foci.
That is, while we are focused on one type of oscillation (toward safety and away from nonsafety, and the role of learning from serious errors and forgetting that drive these shifts), we believe this same model can apply to oscillations between other organizational foci, such as internal and external, long term and short term, and so on.
We hope that future research takes up the challenge of investigating how it is that organizations experience and shift between such extremes, and that our model can help contribute to that work in what is an important area of organizational learning.
Although it is unrealistic to expect to eliminate serious errors and the oscillation cycles they generate, understanding the nature of such phenomena can only help organizations to mitigate the consequences of these complex processes of learning (and forgetting).
