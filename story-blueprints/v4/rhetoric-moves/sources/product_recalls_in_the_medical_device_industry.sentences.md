# product_recalls_in_the_medical_device_industry 句子库存

## introduction
<!-- para 2 -->
The ever-growing role of medical devices in the delivery of health care is evident from the wide array of medical equipment, gadgets, and complex machinery that are used across clinical settings ranging from small community care clinics to large super-specialty hospitals.
A good example of this growth is the use of robotics in high-risk, high-precision surgeries today.
A medical device is “an instrument, apparatus, implement, machine, contrivance, implant, in vitro reagent, or other similar or related article, including a component part, or accessory, which is intended for use in the diagnosis of disease or other conditions, or in the cure, mitigation, treatment, or prevention of disease” (Federal Food, Drug and Cosmetic Act, Section 201, http://www.fda.gov).
Medical devices, from thermometers to artificial hearts, have become a necessary and critical component in health-care delivery.
The medical device industry is one of the fastest growing industries in the economy today.
The overall revenue for the medical device industry in the year 2006 was estimated at about US$90 billion, with an annual growth rate of about 6% (U.S. Department of Commerce 2008).
<!-- para 3 -->
Although medical devices have become an indispensable component (often lifesaving) in the delivery of health care, sometimes they also become sources of significant risk to the consumers of medical devices.
Medical devices, like other products, are prone to quality failures such as manufacturing defects, functional defects, packaging errors, and software glitches, which present a potential health risk to the patients and personnel using these devices.
Trade journals and newspapers abound with articles about malfunctioning medical devices leading to severe losses.
Frequently, firms recall devices from the market.
Thus, on one hand, the medical device industry has pushed the frontier of superior health-care delivery, but on the other hand, the quality problems with medical devices have become the bane of this industry, resulting in ineffective health-care delivery.
<!-- para 4 -->
Medical device failures and subsequent recalls impact almost all the key participants of the medical device supply chain.
Understandably, patients—the end customers—tend to be the worst affected from medical device malfunctions.
The severity of the impact of device malfunction ranges from minor inconveniences (as in the case of a biased thermometer) to severe losses, sometimes fatal (as in the case of a defective implant).
Similarly, while healthcare providers (hospital/physicians—also consumers of medical devices such as a magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) scanner) constantly attempt to adopt innovative devices in the best interests of the patients, they are often stymied in their attempts by the high incidence of defects in medical devices (Abelson 2004, Hauser and Maron 2005, Kennedy 2006, Wallack 2008).
In the event of a device quality failure and a subsequent recall, patients and physicians are forced to go through the painful steps of adjusting to and/or replacing the defective devices, the brunt of which is also faced by the insurance providers.
Thus, customers of medical devices (direct or indirect) downstream in the health-care supply chain are harshly affected by device malfunctions and subsequent recalls.
Yet, this industry is plagued by frequent product quality failures and associated recalls. [^1]
<!-- para 5 -->
Although it is conceivable that the manufacturers and suppliers of these devices upstream in the supply chain do allocate resources to ensure the quality of such devices, the persistent issue of quality failures suggests otherwise.
It leads us to question if the costs of quality failures are high enough for the manufacturers of these devices.
Furthermore, it casts doubts on the ability of the market to regulate their behavior—for public firms in efficient markets, one would expect the fear of shareholder backlash to drive firms' commitment to providing reliable products to the market.
Bromiley and Marcus (1989) suggest that the market serves as a deterrent to dubious firm behavior (as in production of defective devices) only if the firm perceives significant associated market losses.
The prevalence of quality failures in the medical device industry, despite the health risks to consumers, raises many questions:
<!-- para 6 -->
• What are the financial consequences of recalls in the medical device industry?
<!-- para 7 -->
—Are the performance implications severe enough to deter firms from introducing potentially hazardous products into the market?
<!-- para 8 -->
—How do the financial consequences vary across firms in the medical device industry?
<!-- para 9 -->
While the questions above are focused on the performance implications of quality failures and its subsequent impact on firm behavior (ex post), the prevalence of quality failures also points to the inability of firms to overcome defects in the medical device industry.
For firms to overcome such failures, it is important to identify factors that systematically increase or decrease the likelihood of having device failures (ex ante).
In other words,
<!-- para 10 -->
• What are the sources of product recalls in medical device firms?
<!-- para 11 -->
—Are some firms more likely than others to have device failures?
<!-- para 12 -->
This study is an attempt to address these questions.
Understanding medical device recalls, their sources, and financial consequences, is important for a number of other reasons.
First, it fills a gap in the operations management literature regarding the financial consequences of product quality failures.
Whereas extant literature has addressed financial impact of supply chain glitches and the resulting inability to match supply and demand (e.g., Hendricks and Singhal 2005), empirical studies focused on the performance implications of product quality failures and subsequent recalls are lacking.
As mentioned earlier, an understanding of the severity of the financial implications of product recalls also indicates the ability of the market to deter dubious firm behavior (as in the production of defective devices or the failure to take actions to prevent the same).
Second, from a product development perspective, product recalls—which represent the failure of a firm in delivering reliable high-quality products—may be viewed as an undesirable outcome of being early to the market with new products.
Extant research in the new product development literature (e.g., Cohen et al. 1996, Bhaskaran and Ramachandran 2006) identifies the trade-off between development time and product quality/performance in the presence of competition.
Although medical device launches are regulated by the U.S. Food and Drug Administration (FDA), understanding the economic costs of failure (in addition to the social costs) could nevertheless provide firms a systematic way of balancing the trade-offs between speed to market and product quality.
In other words, it could enable firms to assess the economic benefits/viability of going beyond conformance quality.
Third, because medical devices form a critical component of health-care delivery, unanticipated device malfunctions, and failures present significant health risks to consumers.
A study of product recalls and their implications would be valuable in drawing out risk specification, risk assessment, and risk mitigation strategies (Kliendorfer and Saad 2005), which are central to managing the health risks posed by device failures.
<!-- para 13 -->
The econometric analyses in the study are based on data from manufacturers in the medical device industry over a four-year period from 2002 to 2005.
Contrary to our expectations, the findings of the study indicate that at an aggregate level, the market penalties for medical device recalls are not significant, i.e., at the aggregate level, the costs of poor quality are not severe.
However, we find systematic differences in the magnitude of financial consequences across firms.
We find that the product scope, sales, growth prospects, and the capital structure of a firm affect the magnitude of the financial consequences of recalls.
In our analyses exploring the sources of device recalls, we find that firms with a research and development (R&D) focus, developing broader product portfolios, have a higher likelihood of device recalls.
Also, we find that firms with prior recall experience have a lower likelihood of future recalls, indicating the presence of learning.
<!-- para 14 -->
The remainder of this paper is organized as follows.
Section 2 provides a brief overview of the medical device recall process and describes the development of the testable hypotheses in the study.
The hypotheses link device recalls and firm performance, identify the effect of firm characteristics on the financial consequences of recalls, and examine the impact of operational focus and learning on the likelihood of recalls.
Section 3 describes the data collection, the empirical analyses to test the study hypotheses, and the results.
Section 4 presents a discussion of the key study findings.
Section 5 highlights the conclusions and directions for future research.

## theory
<!-- para 3 -->
The medical device supply chain, as illustrated in Figure 1, is a highly regulated supply chain with multiple stakeholders serving multiple customer groups.
Given the significant role of medical devices in the delivery of health care, this industry is regulated by the FDA (http://www.fda.gov).
The FDA is responsible for ensuring that medical devices are safe, effective, and accurately represented to the public.
It accomplishes this by approving new devices, ensuring manufacturing and performance standards, tracking device malfunctions and adverse reactions, and monitoring the medical device recall process.
<!-- para 4 -->
The medical device supply chain serves a two-tier customer structure.
The first group of customers includes the retail customers.
For devices such as thermometers, pregnancy test kits, and blood pressure monitors, patients buying these devices from retail outlets make the purchase decisions independently.
The second group of customers includes the providers (hospital/physicians), who are consumers of medical devices, such as MRI scanners and robotic surgical tools used in the delivery of health care.
The providers are often in direct consultation with the manufacturers in making the purchase decisions.
Furthermore, for some categories of devices such as a pacemaker, the providers and patients come together in making purchase decisions.
Thus, the purchase decision making downstream in the supply chain is distributed across customer groups.
The decision making across these groups is also strongly influenced by insurance providers and the Medicare/Medicaid policies.
<!-- para 5 -->
A recall in the medical device supply chain is a method of removing or correcting products in use that are in violation of laws administered by the FDA.
It is triggered by quality failures such as manufacturing defects, functional defects, packaging errors, and software glitches, which present a potential health risk to the users of the medical device.
Generally, a recall is a voluntary action by the manufacturer.
However, sometimes the FDA may order the manufacturer to recall a product, if it deems that the product poses a significant health risk.
When a manufacturer recalls a device, the FDA evaluates the degree of health risk presented by the recalled product and classifies the recall as a class I, class II, or class III recall.
A class I recall involves a situation in which there is a reasonable probability of serious adverse health consequences.
Class II recalls involve situations where the adverse health consequences are temporary or medically reversible, and class III recalls characterize situations with minimal adverse health consequences (FDA, http://www.fda.gov).
Central to all recalls is a recall strategy developed by the manufacturer, which involves multiple aspects (such as depth of recall, consumer and retail/wholesale level determination, public warning methods, and effectiveness checks) suited to the individual circumstances of the particular recall.
The FDA reviews the adequacy of this plan and recommends changes as appropriate.
Recalling firms are also responsible for notifying customers about the recall and keeping the FDA informed of the recall status.
A recall is terminated when the FDA determines that all reasonable efforts have been made in accordance with the recall plan.
The FDA publishes a weekly Enforcement Report that contains all enforcement actions including recalls, field corrections, seizures, and injunctions.
<!-- para 8 -->
The recall of a defective product from the market represents the failure of a manufacturer in delivering the promised level of quality over the product life cycle.
It is reflective of a device supply chain that has encountered a serious disruption.
Product defects, as mentioned earlier, occur for a variety of reasons, such as design flaws, production errors, material defects, functional defects, packaging errors, and software glitches.
In this section, we identify the financial consequences of such quality failures based on market perceptions of the impact of the quality failures on the firm's profitability.
<!-- para 9 -->
Product recalls may affect firm profitability in three key ways (Hendricks and Singhal 2003).
First, it increases the costs to the firm.
The additional costs can be from multiple sources—these include the costs of correcting/replacing the defective product, the transaction costs of the recall process, and the costs of unsold inventory.
Second, it lowers the firm's revenues resulting from loss of sales.
This could happen owing to factors such as the loss of sales of the product being recalled; reduction in sales of related products, owing to spillover effects; and, loss of market share, owing to the recall.
Third, it affects the overall credibility and reputation of the firm—consumer confidence in the firm's ability to manufacture reliable and high-quality products is lowered, which, in turn, has negative consequences for the firm's ability to sustain its position in the market.
Consequently, firms announcing the recall of medical devices are likely to face significant disadvantages in the market.
While a direct assessment of all the costs and lost revenues from a recall is difficult, owing to a myriad of measurement issues (Thomsen and Mckenzie 2001), in this study we focus on the impact of the recall on the shareholder wealth as reflected in the stock market, which often is far more than the direct costs of recalls (Jarrell and Peltzman 1985).
<!-- para 10 -->
Investors in efficient capital markets are assumed to build on all available information about a firm's activities in evaluating the current and future performance of that firm.
When new information about the firm's actions (such as a recall announcement) becomes available in the market, it is incorporated into the investor's evaluation of the expected net cash flows in the future, which, in turn, is reflected in the stock prices.
In other words, the market assesses the likely impact of an announcement on the costs to the firm, future revenues, and the firm's reputation, which, in turn, is reflected in a positive/negative adjustment of the stock price.
Hence, monitoring changes in stock prices over a relatively short period around an event would effectively capture the impact of the event on firm performance (McWilliams and Siegel 1997, Mackinlay 1997).
It obviates the need for the accounting-based measures of firm performance, which are often ineffective in capturing the strategic nature of events (McWilliams and Siegel 1997, Subramani and Walden 2001).
A device failure and a subsequent recall would be perceived as a negative announcement by the market because it would decrease the expected net cash flows (costs increase, revenues decrease).
Consequently, the market reaction to a recall announcement would be reflected in a downward adjustment of the stock price.
Hence, we argue that:
<!-- para 11 -->
HYPOTHESIS 1.
The abnormal returns attributable to medical device recall announcements are negative.
<!-- para 13 -->
The abnormal returns attributable to device recall announcements reflect market perceptions of the impact of the recall on firm profitability.
However, given heterogeneous firms with varied resource strengths, the impact of a recall on firms' profitability and the ability of firms to recover from such quality failures would be varied.
Hence, it is conceivable that the severity of market reactions to recall announcements would be varied across firms.
Below, we identify factors that affect the severity of market reactions to recalls.
Specifically, we explore the influence of firms' product scope, growth prospects, sales, and capital structure on the magnitude of abnormal returns attributable to recall announcements.
<!-- para 14 -->
**2.3.1.
Product Scope.**
<!-- para 15 -->
There are 19 categories of medical devices per FDA classification rules. [^2] Medical device firms could offer products across more than one category and within each category they could offer multiple products.
Product scope captures this breadth and depth of the product portfolio of a firm.
A broader and deeper product portfolio increases the ability of a firm to not only cover a wider range of customer needs but also provide more options to choose from for its customers, which, in turn, enables firms to be more successful in meeting customer needs (Kekre and Srinivasan 1990).
Firms that have a product presence across multiple categories would also be able to cope with the recall of one of its products better than firms with a more focused product line.
Given that firms with higher product scope could fall back on their other products, when one of their products is recalled, ceteris paribus, the negative impact of recall on profitability and the market reactions would be less severe for these firms compared to that of the firms with focused product lines.
Similarly, in the context of pharmaceutical drug development, Girotra et al. (2007) find that firms with fewer product alternatives for a drug under development face a more negative reaction in the market to late-stage failure than firms with many product substitutes.
This suggests that not only is the impact of a recall relatively smaller, but also the market confidence in the firm's ability to recover from a failure is stronger when the firm has a broader and deeper product portfolio.
Hence, we argue that:
<!-- para 16 -->
HYPOTHESIS 2.
The abnormal returns attributable to medical device recalls are less negative for firms with greater product scope.
<!-- para 17 -->
**2.3.2.
Growth Prospects**.
<!-- para 18 -->
Another factor that might influence the ability of a firm to recover from a recall is the nature of the product market and the firm's growth prospects in that market.
Although the medical device supply chain is broadly innovation driven, it is conceivable that the product market for certain categories of devices is more mature than others.
For example, whereas the product market for gauzes and wound dressing is relatively steady and mature, cardiovascular devices (includes devices such as drug-eluting stents and implantable defibrillators) form one of the fastest growing category of devices that drives revenue growth in this industry (Centers for Medicare and Medicaid Services (CMS) 2003).
Firms that compete in such categories face high growth prospects and need to design and deliver reliable products in a consistent manner.
However, when firms in such markets encounter quality failures and subsequent recalls, the lost sales from existing and potential customers in current and future periods results in a severe negative impact on performance (Hendricks and Singhal 2003).
Consequently, when firms with high growth prospects announce a quality failure, the investor's confidence in the ability of the firm to continue in the high growth trajectory is lowered, which, in turn, results in a more negative market reaction than in a low growth scenario.
Hence, we argue that:
<!-- para 19 -->
HYPOTHESIS 3.
The abnormal returns attributable to medical device recalls are more negative for firms with high growth prospects.
<!-- para 20 -->
**2.3.3.
Sales.** Because the sales of a firm is one of its main sources of revenue, it plays a central role in the ability of a firm to absorb and overcome the negative impact of a recall.
Firms with lower sales are generally ill-suited to handle and recover from a disruption such as the recall of a defective product.
This is because the revenue stream for the firm is strongly dependent on the success of all its products.
When one of its products is recalled, the firm's profitability is significantly diminished.
Further, the costs of recalls and the associated loss of revenue are worsened by the considerably lower ability of these firms to reinvest scarce resources in fixing the products and/or developing alternative products.
This makes it difficult to make a quick turn around and recapture the lost market share.
Hence, it is conceivable that firms with lower sales will have a more severe market reaction to recall announcements.
Consequently, we argue that:
<!-- para 21 -->
HYPOTHESIS 4.
The abnormal returns attributable to medical device recalls are more negative for firms with lower sales.
<!-- para 22 -->
**2.3.4.
Capital Structure.**
<!-- para 23 -->
The capital structure of a firm is another factor that influences shareholder reaction to device recalls.
The capital structure is assessed using the debt-to-equity ratio, which simply describes the proportion of a firm's asset burden borne by the shareholders.
We discussed earlier that the market value of firms is lowered because of the recalls.
However, shareholder's burden of the decrease in the market value is relatively low for firms with high debt-to-equity ratio.
Also, lowered revenue from loss of current and future sales and increased costs of handling the recall lead to an increase in the risk of the firm.
However, ceteris paribus, an increase in risk is associated with a decrease in the debt value and increase in the market value, more so for firms with high debt-to-equity ratio (Hendricks and Singhal 2003).
Thus, the extent to which shareholders are affected by a decrease in the market value or an increase in the risk is inversely dependent on the debt-to-equity ratio.
Consequently, the severity of the market reactions to recall announcements is inversely related to the debt-to-equity ratio.
Hence, we argue that:
<!-- para 24 -->
HYPOTHESIS 5.
The abnormal returns attributable to medical device recalls are less negative for firms with high debt-to-equity ratio.
<!-- para 26 -->
Next, we explore factors that systematically increase or decrease the likelihood of having device failures and subsequent recalls in medical device firms.
Specifically, we explore the effect of learning (from prior recalls) and operational focus (assessed by the product scope of a firm) on the likelihood of future device recalls.
<!-- para 27 -->
2.4.1.
Learning.
Quality failures and subsequent recalls, as mentioned earlier, arise because of factors such as unanticipated production defects, material faults, and packaging errors.
Such failures are surprise events for firms and are situations where the existing procedures and routines failed to meet their designed requirements and are rendered ineffective in ensuring quality.
However, these events trigger renewed attention to the weak links in the process and foster research toward improving the existing operations by bringing new information and resources (Haunschild and Rhee 2004, Sitkin 1992, Haunschild and Sullivan 2002).
They provide an opportunity for firms to learn and improve their operations, and, consequently, can prevent such errors from recurring.
Learning from a recall can be along multiple dimensions such as better product design, software design, and material selection; improved manufacturing processes; and tighter quality control.
Learning across these aspects increases with cumulative recall experience, and, in turn, could lead to improved product quality and a reduction in the likelihood of quality failures over time.
Hence, we argue that:
<!-- para 28 -->
HYPOTHESIS 6.
The greater the prior recall experience of firms, the lower the likelihood of future recalls.
<!-- para 29 -->
2.4.2.
Product Scope and Operational Focus.
Product scope, as discussed earlier, is a measure of breadth and depth of products offered by a firm across the 19 categories of medical devices.
Although having a greater product scope may increase the firm's ability to cover a wider range of customer needs, and, hence, capture a bigger share of the market, it nevertheless lowers the firm's focus on each product line.
Given that firms have resource constraints, firms with broader and deeper portfolios will be drawn thin in their ability to dedicate resources to design, develop, produce, and monitor individual product lines, ceteris paribus.
The key role of operational focus in firm competitiveness has been discussed fairly extensively in the literature spanning multiple industries across the manufacturing and service sectors (e.g., Skinner 1974, Heskett et al. 1997, Ingram and Baum 1997, Lapre and Tsikritsis 2006).
This literature argues that focused firms with fewer product lines are able to achieve better operational performance, better competitive position, and higher customer satisfaction, owing to their simplicity, task homogeneity, and faster learning (Lapre and Tsikritsis 2006).
We draw from this literature to suggest that medical device firms that are focused on a few product lines will be able to achieve better performance with fewer design flaws, production defects, and packaging errors than their less focused counterparts.
In other words, when firms try to design and deliver multiple products, the likelihood of committing errors is greater, ceteris paribus.
Hence, we argue that:
<!-- para 30 -->
HYPOTHESIS 7.
The broader the product scope of a medical device firm, the greater the likelihood of future recalls.

## methods
<!-- para 3 -->
Testing the hypotheses developed above requires information on medical device recalls, market performance of firms recalling devices, and firm characteristics.
Collecting this data involved drawing from three key sources: the FDA; the Center for Research on Security Prices (CRSP) database; and the Compustat database, accessed through the Wharton Research Data Services.
<!-- para 4 -->
Information on medical device recalls was obtained from the weekly Enforcement Reports on the FDA website.
The Enforcement Reports contain announcements on medical device recalls across the three types of recalls (i.e., class I, class II, and class III).
Each recall announcement contains the name of the product being recalled, the recalling firm, the first recall date (characterized by a press release or letter from the manufacturer), the number of units being recalled, whether the recall is ongoing or complete, the class of the recall, the distribution of the product being recalled, and the reasons for the recall.
This information was collected for all device recalls made over a four-year period from the second quarter of 2002 to the fourth quarter of 2005 (i.e., all public information that was downloadable).
Over this period, there were 4,476 recalls of medical devices by 537 firms, which also included multiple product recalls by the same firm on the same day (products from the same product line), and both public and private firms.
Accounting for this multiple counting resulted in 1,885 recall events.
<!-- para 5 -->
The estimation and analyses testing the financial consequences of recall announcements in Hypothesis 1 requires a clean estimation window, i.e., there are no other recall events during the window.
Consequently, we identified a subset of recalls for each firm in the sample that were at least five months apart.
This resulted in a data set with 767 recall events.
The next step involved obtaining information on the market performance of firms around the recall events identified in the sample.
We identified firms that were publicly traded for which the accounting and stock information was available on the CRSP database.
On the CRSP database, information on stock returns (daily/monthly) and market indices that are needed for the market model for returns to be used in the event study methodology can be obtained for publicly traded firms.
This step involved checking if each firm in the initial sample was listed on the CRSP database and collecting the identifiers (e.g., PERMNO—permanent number on CRSP) for each listed firm.
We also cross-checked the list with the Hoover's company database to obtain information on firms in our set.
At the end of this step, we arrived at a final sample of 223 recall events.
This formed our study sample for the analyses testing Hypothesis 1.
<!-- para 6 -->
The analysis examining Hypotheses 2–5 discussed earlier also requires information on firm characteristics.
So, in the next step, we collected information on the characteristics of firms in our study sample.
Information on firm characteristics, such as sales, R&D expenditure, employees, growth prospects, and capital structure was obtained from the Compustat database.
We also obtained information on the breadth and depth of products offered by firms.
This information was collected from the FDA database on the medical device listing.
Whereas we had 223 recall events in our sample for the earlier analysis, complete information on the recall characteristics and firm characteristics was only available for 154 recall events.
This formed our sample for the analyses assessing the effect of the recall and firm characteristics on the financial consequences of recall announcements.
For the study examining the sources of recalls (Hypotheses 6 and 7), the product recall information for publicly traded firms over the period 2002–2005 formed our dependent variable.
Information on firm characteristics was obtained from Compustat, as described above.
Overall, a usable sample of 276 recall events was collected for this analysis.
Next, we present the empirical analyses used to test the study hypotheses.
<!-- para 8 -->
3.2.1.
Event Study Methodology and Model Specification.
In examining the performance implications of product quality failures, we adopt an event study methodology.
As discussed earlier, rational and efficient markets incorporate all available information in arriving at the evaluation of the expected net cash flows in the future, and the impact of a significant event is reflected immediately in the stock price of a firm (Mackinlay 1997).
Consequently, monitoring changes in stock prices over a relatively short period around an event would effectively capture the financial impact of the event on firm performance (McWilliams and Siegel 1997, Mackinlay 1997).
The event study methodology is a powerful tool in assessing the financial impact of specific unanticipated events (Brown and Warner 1980, 1985; McWilliams and Siegel 1997; MacKinlay 1997) and has been useful in understanding the economic effects of unanticipated events across a variety of contexts (Hendricks and Singhal 2003) such as effective total quality management (TQM) implementation (Hendricks and Singhal 1996), new product introduction (Chaney et al. 1991), e-commerce announcements (Subramani and Walden 2001), and seasoned equity offerings (Speiss and Affleck-Grave 1995).
Below, we provide a brief description of the key steps in conducting an event study.
<!-- para 9 -->
The first step in conducting an event study involves identifying the event of interest.
In our study, the event of interest is the recall of a medical device, specifically the announcement of a recall by the firm or the FDA (see the appendix for a sample recall announcement).
Although the FDA forms the key source of recall information in our study, we realize that sometimes the firm independently announces the recall earlier than the FDA.
So, the market might be able to sense the recall before the FDA announcement.
Additionally, for the more severe recalls (class I recalls), it is conceivable that the market may be aware of an impending recall based on input from public information sources.
To assess systematic prior information in the market about the recalls, we combed public information sources (major U.S. and world publications, news wire services, company, SEC filings, and legal news) via Lexis-Nexis and Google News archives.
In our study, we consider the earliest signal to the market as the date of the announcement.
The date of the recall could be the announcement by the FDA, the firm's announcement on its website, or a press release, whichever is the earliest.
The next step involves determining the time period over which the stock returns of the firm will be observed.
In studies using daily stock returns, the day on which the event occurs is referred to as the event day (or day "0") and assessing market reactions to events involves examining abnormal returns on that day.
However, typically the abnormal returns are examined over a short period surrounding the event of interest, referred to as the event window.
This is to account for the possibility that the effect of the event may be spread out over a small period, owing to multiple reasons such as end-of-day announcements or markets sensing the impending recall a few days before the announcement, based on insider information.
Event windows that have been used in the extant studies include 2-day windows (0, +1) (-1, 0), a 3-day window (-1, +1), 11-day windows (-5, +5) (-10, +1), and a 21-day window (-10, +10). [^3] In this study, we present results from analyses using multiple event windows to assess the sensitivity of the results to the choice of the event window.
<!-- para 10 -->
The next step involves the identification of a measure of abnormal returns to be used in assessing the impact of the event over the event window.
Abnormal return $AR_{it}$ represents the difference between actual returns $R_{it}$ and expected return $E_{it}$ for firm $i$ at time $t$.
Whereas $R_{it}$ is observed, $E_{it}$ is estimated based on a prespecified model for expected returns.
There are many options in the choice of a model for expected returns, such as the constant return model, the market model, and the comparison period adjusted model.
Among these multiple options, simple market models have been shown to perform as well as or better than other sophisticated economic/statistical models in the extant literature.
Thus, in this research we use the market model in estimating the expected returns.
However, to assess the sensitivity of the results to the choice of the model for expected returns, we also conducted additional analyses using the comparison period adjusted model.
<!-- para 11 -->
A market model is specified as a linear function of market returns at time $t$, i.e., $E_{it} = \alpha_i + \beta_i * R_{mt}$, where $R_{mt}$ is the average market returns (based on a market index such as the S&P 500) at time $t$, $\alpha_i$ is the risk-free return of firm $i$, and $\beta_i$ is the risk factor.
The terms $\alpha_i$ and $\beta_i$ are firm specific factors that need to be estimated.
This is done using data on daily returns from an estimation period, the period before the event in which there are no unanticipated events affecting normal stock returns.
The estimation of $\alpha_i$ and $\beta_i$ is done using ordinary least squares regression of the normal returns over the estimation period on the market returns $R_{mt}$ in the same period.
In our study, we used data over a 120-day period ending 21 days before the event for our estimation.
For the market returns, we used the CRSP value weighted index.
After the $\alpha_i$ and $\beta_i$ are estimated, the abnormal returns for firm $i$ on day $t$ in the event period and its variance can then be estimated as
<!-- para 12 -->
$$
AR_{it} = R_{it} - \hat{\alpha}_i - \hat{\beta}_i * R_{mt},
$$
<!-- para 13 -->
$$
\mathrm{Var}(AR_{it}) = S_i^2 \bigg[ 1 + \frac{1}{T} + \frac{(R_{mt} - \bar{R}_m)}{\sum_{t=1}^T (R_{mt} - \bar{R}_m)^2} \bigg].
$$
<!-- para 14 -->
The variance of the abnormal return for firm $i$ on day $t$ in the event period is estimated as a function of the variance ($S_i^2$) of the estimation model and the spread of the market return on day $t$ about the mean market return ($\bar{R}_m$) over the estimation period of length $T$.
Next, the mean abnormal return on day $t$ for the entire sample ($\overline{AR}_t$) and its variance are estimated as
<!-- para 15 -->
$$
\overline{AR}_t = \frac{\sum_{i=1}^N AR_{it}}{N},\qquad \mathrm{Var}(\overline{AR}_t) = \frac{\sum_{i=1}^N \mathrm{Var} AR_{it}}{N^2}.
$$
<!-- para 16 -->
As mentioned earlier, assessing the market reactions to events involves examining abnormal returns over

Variables and Model Specification.
Firm characteristics specified in the model (corresponding to Hypotheses 2–5) include product scope, sales, growth prospects, and the capital structure of the firm.
Product scope, as defined earlier, measures the breadth and depth of the product portfolio of a firm.
Specifically, medical device firms could offer products across 19 categories.
The product scope (Sorescu et al. 2003) of a firm is calculated as
<!-- para 36 -->
$$ S_{i}=\left[\sum_{j=1}^{19}\frac{P_{j}}{P}\ln\left(\frac{P}{P_{j}}\right)\right]P, $$
<!-- para 37 -->
where $P_{j}$ refers to the number of products within category $j$, and $P$ represents the total number of products across all categories.
Sales of the firm are measured as the natural logarithm of the total sales.
The growth prospects of the firm are assessed using the market-to-book ratio (Hendricks and Singhal 2003) and the capital structure of the firm is assessed using the debt-to-equity ratio.
<!-- para 38 -->
Recall characteristics specified in the model include the recall class, recall volume, recall distribution, and the number of products being recalled.
Recall class refers to the severity of the recall.
We included the recall class to account for any differences in the market reactions to these three types of recalls.
Because of the severity of a class I recall, the costs of correction, the loss of market reputation, and the erosion in the investor's confidence in the firm's ability to make safe devices may be higher for class I recalls.
Consequently, it is conceivable that the market reaction to class I recalls will be more negative than that for class II or class III recalls.
<!-- para 39 -->
Recall volume refers to the number of units in distribution when the recall is made.
Recall distribution refers to the extent of geographic distribution of the product in the market—local, regional, national or international.
Thus, recall volume and recall distribution capture the extent of product adoption in the market.
We expect market reactions to be more severe for recalls of products that have been more widely adopted in the market.
Number of products refers to the number of products in the product family affected by a quality failure.
It is conceivable that the recall process may be more intensive when quality failure affects many products in a product family.
The more the number of products affected, the greater the impact on the investor's confidence in the firm's ability to overcome the setback.
<!-- para 40 -->
Additionally, we include controls for the R&D intensity (R&D expenditure/sales) of a firm and the extent of labor use (employees/sales) at the firm.
We control for the firm's R&D efforts because market perceptions of the ability of a firm to bounce back from a product failure may be affected by its innovation orientation.
Although the R&D efforts of a firm may mitigate the chances of a quality failure, higher R&D intensity may also indicate the firm's focus on developing new products.
Also, there is considerable heterogeneity in the extent of labor use in medical device firms.
Higher labor per unit of output (Norton 1988) could mean higher retraining/monitoring costs.
Also, it is conceivable that the chances of error (due to human causes) would vary with increased use of labor.
These factors may in turn affect the likelihood of recalls and market perceptions of the ability to bounce back from the recall.
Hence, we control for this variation in our analysis.
We conducted checks to ensure the lack of multicollinearity in the variables included in the analyses.
In our analyses, the estimated variance inflation factor (VIF) scores, as shown in Table 4, were all significantly less than 10, indicating no serious multicollinearity (Neter et al. 1996).

Also, our observations of the CARs are heteroskedastic.
Hence, we adopt a weighted least squares regression with correction for clustered errors in our analysis.

3.4.1.
Variables and Model Specification.
The dependent variable in this analysis is the number of recalls made by firms during the years 2003–2005.
The independent variables in the model specification (corresponding to Hypotheses 6 and 7) include the prior recall experience, proportions of class I and class II recalls, and product scope of a firm.
The prior recall experience of a firm each year is the cumulative number of recalls by the firm up until the end of previous year.
We included the proportions of class I and class II recalls to account for any differences in the learning from the three types of recalls.
It is conceivable that the more frequent class II and class III recalls would foster greater learning.
However, it could also be argued that the more severe class I recalls provide a greater opportunity for learning.
The product scope of a firm, as discussed earlier (§3.3.1), assesses the breadth and depth of the products offered across 19 medical device categories.
<!-- para 50 -->
The control variables in our model specification include year-specific effects (y2004, y2005), the yearly sales (logarithm of total sales), R&D intensity (R&D expenditure/sales), and the extent of labor use (employees/sales).
We account for the yearly sales to account for the scale of the firm's operations across years.
We control for the R&D intensity of a firm, because, as mentioned earlier, the R&D efforts of a firm may mitigate the chances of a quality failure, and, hence, affect the likelihood of recalls.
On the contrary, higher R&D intensity may also indicate the firm's innovation orientation with the focus on new product development, which may, in turn, increase the likelihood of errors.
We also controlled for the labor per unit of output as the chances of error (due to human causes) may vary with increased use of labor.
We conducted checks to ensure the lack of multicollinearity in the variables included in the analyses.
In our analyses the estimated VIFs were all less than 10, indicating no serious multicollinearity.
<!-- para 51 -->
Given that recalls are relatively rare events for individual firms and the number of recalls (dependent variable) is count data, it is more appropriately characterized by a Poisson/negative binomial distribution.
We use a negative binomial distribution, owing to the high variance relative to the mean, a characteristic inconsistent with Poisson distribution.
And, because we observe the occurrence of recalls for firms over multiple time periods, we use a panel specification.
Firms vary in their ability to design and deliver defect-free products, because of a variety of factors, such as unique design and production capabilities, managerial abilities, and legacy effects.
Thus, the likelihood of recalls could be lower for firms that tend to have lower recalls in the first place.
To control for the unobserved heterogeneity arising out of firm specific operations and learning ability, we use a conditional fixed effects specification.
This enables us to test whether firms learn from their recall experience, ceteris paribus.
In this approach, "the fixed effects are conditioned on the sum of the event counts within the panel and then concentrated out of the maximum likelihood," accounting for any firm specific heterogeneity (Haunschild and Rhee 2004, pp. 1552–1553; Cameron and Trivedi 1998).
Thus, the model is specified as a conditional fixed effects negative binomial panel regression.

## discussion
<!-- para 2 -->
The empirical analyses above ( §§3.2, 3.3, and 3.4) assess the impact of product recalls on the shareholder wealth in the medical device industry, identify the effect of firm characteristics on the financial consequences of recalls, and examine the impact of operational focus and learning on likelihood of recalls.
The findings from the analyses suggest that capital market penalties for medical device recalls are not severe and that market reaction to recall announcements is varied across heterogeneous firms.
The results also indicate that as firms broaden their product scope, the likelihood of product failures and recalls increases, owing to a decreased operational focus.
Lastly, the results suggest that firms do learn from recalls.
<!-- para 3 -->
That the capital market costs of poor quality are not a significant deterrent to introducing potentially hazardous products in the medical device industry is troublesome from a consumer's perspective.
A possible explanation for the lack of strong market reaction to product recalls is that markets learn to expect recalls.
In other words, the recalls of medical devices by firms in this study were consistent with market expectations.
Bromiley and Marcus (1989) suggest that at a fundamental level, the market expects a normal number of recalls per firm per period.
Markets learn to expect medical device firms to make mistakes, some severe and others minor.
The learning is reflected in the expected number of recalls in each time period, where such expectations are constantly updated, based on observations of firm recall behavior.
The market's valuation of the stock prices varies with the expected number of recalls in each time period (Bromiley and Marcus 1989).
Whereas each recall event can be treated as a random occurrence at an aggregate level, the recall rate can be assumed to follow a Poisson/negative binomial distribution.
The moments of this distribution (specific to the firm in the industry) are estimated by the market, based on the observations of the firm's recall events over fixed periods.
The market's expectations of firm's recalls can be fashioned as a Bayesian model where the estimates are updated each time new information about the firm (e.g., recall) becomes available (Bromiley and Marcus 1989).
The market's valuation of the stock prices (expected net cash flows) varies with the variation in the expected number of recalls in each time period and the size and impact of the expected recalls.
The consistently high recurrence of recalls in the medical device industry suggests that the recalls are common in this industry.
This, in turn, could lead to a higher expected number of recalls and, consequently, the lack of severe market reaction to recalls.
<!-- para 4 -->
A related explanation for the lack of strong market reaction to device recalls is that firms learn from recalls.
Recalls, as mentioned before, are surprise events that represent the failure of a firm to meet specifications.
Because failures suggest that the current firm practices are ineffective in ensuring quality, these events may act as a strong catalyst of search and learning in bringing new information and practices (Haunschild and Rhee 2004, Sitkin 1992, Haunschild and Sullivan 2002).
Learning from recalls could be related to two aspects of a firm's future operations—(preventive) operations before a recall, and (control) operations in the event of a recall.
Learning opportunities in a firm's operations before a recall involve preventive aspects such as better product design, software design, material selection, and improved manufacturing processes in the supply chain.
Learning across these aspects could lead to improved product quality and a reduction in the probability of future recalls.
Learning opportunities in a firm's operations in the event of a recall involve control aspects such as effective management of withdrawal, replacement or correction of failed products, and better preparedness in handling recall events.
Learning across these aspects could improve the effectiveness and efficiency of a firm's actions, given a recall.
Overall, the improved operations of a firm (both preventive and control) could lower recalls and their disruptive effects on the firm's cash flows in the future.
This may in turn serve to bolster market confidence in the firm's recovery and performance, resulting in less severe reactions to device recalls.
Although the limitations of data availability make it difficult for us to individually examine the learning mechanisms described above, our analyses (in §3.4) do reveal the presence of learning from recalls.
We find that the likelihood of recalls decreases with prior recall experience.
Firms seem to improve their operations as they learn from their mistakes, fixing exposed weak links in the process, thus reducing the likelihood of having future failures.
<!-- para 5 -->
The lack of significant capital market penalties in the medical device industry is also interesting, when compared to market reaction to recall announcements in other industries, e.g., food (Thomsen and McKenzie 2001) and automotive (Jarrell and Peltzman 1985, Bromiley and Marcus 1989).
Differences in market reactions to recall announcements across industries may be attributed to factors such as the frequency and volume of recalls in the industry, regulation, and purchase relationships.
Specifically, the medical device industry is among the fastest growing industry with a very high level of R&D investments (Office of Health and Consumer Goods 2005).
It is fueled by the need for superior and innovative devices in meeting the increasing demand for health care (unlike mature markets for commodity items).
An inevitable reality accompanying this rapid innovation is product failure.
The average number of device failures and subsequent recalls per year in this industry is many times that in other industries. [^9] Thus, whereas recalls may be common to all industries, firms in this nascent and dynamic industry have a higher frequency and volume of recalls than firms in other industries.
Consequently, market perception of recalls is likely to be varied across industries.
<!-- para 6 -->
Furthermore, whereas regulation and oversight is common to other industries, the medical device industry (like pharmaceutical drugs) is closely regulated by the FDA.
Regulation plays two key roles that serve to bolster market confidence in the medical device industry.
First, the FDA plays a proactive role in ensuring that medical devices are safe, effective, and accurately represented to the public.
Starting from clinical trials, product approvals and licenses, ensuring manufacturing and performance standards, to tracking device malfunctions and adverse reactions post-launch, the FDA monitors the development and functioning of medical devices.
Approval and sale of a device positively signals the ability of a firm to develop and launch devices that are reliable and perform at the standards of the regulatory requirements.
This bolsters market confidence in the firm's ability to successfully compete in the market in contrast to firms in an unregulated industry where the product performance and reliability are not realized until market.
Second, devices that are subject to FDA's stringent premarket approval are often not subject to products liability or other personal injury claim owing to device failures under state law, as evidenced in the Supreme Court's decision in the Reigel v.
Medtronic Inc. case [^10] and other related cases.
Although this does not eliminate liability costs all together, it does considerably reduce the liability concerns.
Reduction of liability concerns has been observed both by the courts and the industry as central to encouraging innovation and development in the medical device industry.
Thus, regulation in turn reinforces the market confidence in the ability of firms in this industry to innovate and bounce back from a setback.
<!-- para 7 -->
The nature of interactions between the customers, the device manufacturers, insurance providers, and Medicare/Medicaid, is another differentiating characteristic in this industry.
The medical device supply chain, as depicted earlier in Figure 1, serves a two-tier customer structure.
The first set of customers includes the retail customers purchasing devices such as a blood pressure monitor from retail outlets independently.
The second group of customers includes the providers (hospital/physicians), who are often in direct consultation with the device manufacturers in making the purchase decisions, typically involving long-term contracts.
The decision making across these groups, particularly the latter, is strongly influenced by private insurance providers and the Medicare/Medicaid policies.
An approval by Medicare/Medicaid for use by health-care providers not only affects the provider's ability to recoup the investment in devices but also significantly affects the profitability of a device.
Post-FDA clearance of a device, device firms typically seek Medicare coverage and payment from the CMS (CMS 2003).
The rate of device adoption "often depends on a combination of clinical benefit data, regulatory decisions (including approval, coverage, and payment), and distribution" (CMS 2003, p. 8).
In these settings, once a device clears the regulatory hurdles, the stakeholders (hospitals and manufacturers) get into device contracts with payments supported directly or indirectly by the insurance providers (Medicare, private insurance).
A recall of an approved device may result in the subsequent replacement of the device by the manufacturer after corrections, per the contractual terms.
Also, the strong links between the provider (physician) and the manufacturer (sales representative) often create barriers for new competition (CMS 2003).
Such relationships may serve to bolster market confidence in the ability of device firms to sustain or recover from a recall event.
Overall, an assessment of the implications of these differences (in purchase relationships, recall frequency and volume, and the role of regulation) across industries is central to understanding the varied market reaction to recall announcements across industries and forms a key direction for future research.
<!-- para 8 -->
Although the capital market costs of recalls are not significant at the aggregate level, we do find that the market reaction to product recall announcements varies with firm heterogeneity (growth prospects, product scope, sales, and leverage).
Specifically, the results suggest that the market reaction is more severe, when the product being recalled is one of the few products generating the high sales of a firm with high growth prospects.
In other words, market confidence in the ability to recover from a setback is lower for firms that are highly focused with promising growth prospects.
On the contrary, consistent with Skinner's (1974) argument that having a focused factory is key to manufacturing success, we also find that focused medical device firms with a narrower product portfolio are less likely to have product failures and subsequent recalls.
This indicates the presence of a trade-off as the product scope increases—between the increased market coverage (Kekre and Srinivasan 1990) with less severe market reactions to recalls on one hand, and the decrease in focus and an associated increase in the likelihood of recalls on the other hand.
In other words, the advantage of lower likelihood of recalls from a focused product line comes at the cost of a decreased ability of a firm to cater to a broad set of customer needs and the disadvantage of a severe market reaction in recall situations.
Thus, firms need to find an appropriate level of product diversification in order to minimize the trade-off.
<!-- para 10 -->
Despite the growing importance of medical devices, the prevalence of device malfunctions causes significant concern in the health-care delivery.
Little systematic attention has been paid to understanding the financial consequences of quality failures in the highly regulated device supply chain.
This study is a step toward addressing the issue.
Based on a sample of product recall events between 2002 and 2005, it provides an empirical assessment of the impact of the product recalls on the shareholder wealth in the medical device industry, identifies the effect of firm characteristics on the financial consequences of recalls, examines the influence of operational focus on the likelihood of recalls, and, lastly, examines if firms learn from recalls.
Overall, the findings from the study suggest the following:
<!-- para 11 -->
• Although medical device recalls represent a firm’s failure to deliver quality over the product life cycle causing significant discomfort to consumers, firms face little by way of capital market penalties.
<!-- para 12 -->
There are systematic differences in the market reaction to recalls across firms.
The product scope, sales, growth prospects, and the capital structure of a firm affect the magnitude of the financial consequences of recalls.
<!-- para 13 -->
As firms broaden their product scope, the likelihood of product failures and subsequent recalls increases.
<!-- para 14 -->
Likelihood of recalls decreases with the cumulative recall experience of firms, suggesting a learning effect from the renewed attention and improvements to firm operations triggered by recalls.
<!-- para 15 -->
There are a number of possible extensions to this study, some that could address the limitations of this study.
First, whereas this study focuses on the market reactions to recall announcements in the short-term, future research could focus on the long-term impact of recalls on shareholder wealth.
Because the recall process can be significantly drawn out and the outcomes may be uncertain over the long period, an analysis of the abnormal returns over the long term would be able to capture the continuing effects of the recall effectively.
Second, although we observe that firms learn from their recall experience, owing to data limitations, this study did not explore the mechanisms by which firms learn.
Given the high frequency of recalls across firms in the medical device industry, future research could explore the role and significance of vicarious learning.
Furthermore, understanding the role of learning spillover from recall experience across product categories and exploring the preventive and controls aspects of learning (discussed earlier) could serve as excellent directions for further research in this area.
Third, future research could examine the causes of a lack of strong negative to device recalls.
Specifically, given that markets are known to react strongly (negatively) to supply chain glitches (Hendricks and Singhal 2003), automotive recalls (Jarrell and Peltzman 1985), food recalls (Thomsen and McKenzie 2001), the finding—that when it comes to device recalls, market reactions are not strong—is surprising.
This suggests the need for further research into this behavior.
