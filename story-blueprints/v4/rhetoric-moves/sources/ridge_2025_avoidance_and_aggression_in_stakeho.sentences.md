# ridge_2025_avoidance_and_aggression_in_stakeho 句子库存

## introduction
<!-- para 2 -->
> We absolutely see the competition.
It's continuing ...
I think it will always get better and more intense.
We don't take that competition without our typical paranoia and focus to make sure we continue to drive our performance.
>
> — Brian Krzanich, CEO of Intel
<!-- para 3 -->
As the epigraphs imply, chief executive officers (CEOs) may view stakeholders such as the government and competitors through a prism of paranoia, defined as “relatively stable tendencies toward suspiciousness, feelings of ill will or resentment, mistrust, and belief in external control or influence” (Combs & Penn, 2004: 94). [^1] Indeed, Microsoft’s Bill Gates has often been cited for his “paranoia principle” that “no threat is too improbable to ignore,” which is said to have shaped his choice to approach the government with skepticism (Romano, 2008; Surowiecki, 2001).
Similarly, Intel's Andy Grove, whose guiding motto was "only the paranoid survive," was quoted as saying that he "wanted everyone at Intel running scared" about the "imminent, existential threat" of competitors (Ibarra, 2016).
<!-- para 4 -->
While paranoia has been invoked and discussed by CEOs for decades, theoretical explanations for how it may impact organizational actions is practically nonexistent.
Moreover, little research has focused on how any CEO characteristic, much less paranoia, impacts stakeholder engagement actions.
Indeed, as Gamache, Neville, Bundy, and Short (2020: 1306) noted, theory has “yet to seriously consider how and why CEOs might pursue more specific stakeholder [engagement] strategies.” Gamache et al. (2020) argued that to truly understand the role and influence of CEOs in engagements with stakeholders, we need to develop theory to address both why CEOs take certain approaches as well as how and why those approaches shift over time (for similar calls, see Harrison, Phillips, & Freeman, 2020; Kujala, Sachs, Leinonen, Heikkinen, & Laude, 2022; Pedrini & Ferri, 2019).
<!-- para 5 -->
Further, although upper echelons theory (Hambrick & Mason, 1984) offers logic for how CEOs' traits affect organizational actions such as stakeholder engagement (e.g., Agle, Mitchell, & Sonnenfeld, 1999; Chin, Hambrick, & Treviño, 2013; Gamache et al., 2020; Petrenko, Aime, Ridge, & Hill, 2016), extant upper echelons studies have primarily considered CEOs to have high levels of self-belief, confidence, and attention-seeking tendencies (Finkelstein, Hambrick, & Cannella, 2009; Hiller & Hambrick, 2005), while less attention has been given to more self-deflecting traits such as paranoia (Neely, Lovelace, Cowen, & Hiller, 2020).
Thus, understanding how CEO paranoia impacts stakeholder engagement is particularly important for at least two reasons.
First, paranoia affects both views and actions toward others.
Second, paranoia is subject to activation, a process in which cognitions and ensuing choice tendencies stemming from the trait change after prompting by trait-relevant cues (i.e., actions by external entities that reinforce paranoia-related feelings; Chan & McAllister, 2014; Freeman, Garety, Kuipers, Fowler, & Bebbington, 2002).
<!-- para 6 -->
We address the foregoing issues by developing a more nuanced theory of (a) how CEO paranoia affects specific approaches to stakeholder engagement and (b) what might prompt a change in approach by CEOs.
To do so, like notable studies linking CEOs to stakeholder strategy (e.g., Agle et al., 1999; Chin et al., 2013; Gamache et al., 2020; Petrenko et al., 2016), we build from upper echelons theory to derive a foundational logic of why a focal CEO characteristic such as paranoia affects how that CEO engages stakeholders.
We then integrate two salient aspects of paranoia to theorize about the link between CEO paranoia and stakeholder engagement.
<!-- para 7 -->
First, the underlying tendencies stemming from paranoia manifest in seeking protection from external entities via safety behaviors, defined as actions that aim to reduce the risk and magnitude of threat from external entities (Chan & McAllister, 2014).
While there are numerous safety behaviors along a continuum marked by the degree to which an individual interacts with external entities, those high in paranoia often employ avoidance, defined as the reduction of engagement with external entities with potential to cause negative outcomes.
Avoidance not only marks one end of the safety behavior continuum but is also by far the most frequently employed behavior of those higher in paranoia, and thus is typically strongly associated with the trait (e.g., Freeman & Garety, 2004; Freeman, Garety, & Kuipers, 2001; Freeman et al., 2005).
Integrating this logic about paranoia into upper echelons theory, we argue that CEOs higher in paranoia will tend to avoid engaging powerful stakeholders, manifesting in fewer strategic actions.
<!-- para 8 -->
Second, drawing on theorizing about activation of paranoia (Chan & McAllister, 2014; Freeman et al., 2002), which builds on trait activation (Tett & Burnett, 2003; Tett & Guterman, 2000) and related personality theories (e.g., Mischel & Shoda, 1995), we argue that paranoia-relevant cues affect how those with elevated levels of paranoia engage stakeholders.
Specifically, when cues indicate that avoidance has not offered protection from external entities, individuals higher in the trait tend to shift away from avoidance to directly engage the external entities.
This direct engagement, referred to as aggression, in effect sits on the opposite end of the safety behavior continuum from avoidance (e.g., Freeman et al., 2001) and intends to provide protection from external entities by showing others that the individual is “not to be messed with” (Chan & McAllister, 2014: 54).
Thus, aggression is an avenue through which to try to restore desired safety (Salkovskis, 1991).
Following paranoia-relevant cues from a stakeholder, then, we expect those higher in paranoia to display more aggressive behavior by increasing engagement with stakeholders, rather than displaying the initial avoidance strategy (Freeman et al., 2001).
<!-- para 9 -->
While we expect a variety of stakeholders to be affected by CEO paranoia, as an initial exposition of our logic we focus on two external stakeholders: government and competitors.
Viewing an organization's engagement with government and competitors through CEO paranoia is particularly germane given perceptions of "malevolent intent" that tend to arise when an individual higher in paranoia (here, a CEO) believes that the actions of an external entity could "pose an economic, social, or existential threat" (Kramer & Schaffer, 2014: 199)—which government and competitors have the power to affect (Porter, 1985).
To test our theorizing, we develop and validate a content-analytic measure of CEO paranoia following both theory and evidence in psychology (Oxman, Rosenberg, Schnurr, Tucker, & Gala, 1988; Oxman, Rosenberg, & Tucker, 1982), as well as methodological best practices and recent exemplars (Gamache et al., 2020; McKenny, Short, & Payne, 2013; Short, Broberg, Cogliser, & Brigham, 2010).
<!-- para 10 -->
Using our validated measure, we capture CEO paranoia from earnings call transcripts.
In a longitudinal sample from S&P 1500 firms, we find support for our theoretical logic that CEO paranoia relates to avoidance of government (but do not find a systematic relationship with avoidance of competitors).
As further tests of our premise, we likewise find evidence that CEOs higher in paranoia lead top management teams (TMTs) that perceive greater political and nonpolitical risk.
We also find broad support for our trait-activation logic that when cues indicate that avoidance is not offering safety, individuals higher in paranoia shift away from avoidance to directly engage the external entities.
As we detail in the Discussion section, our work advances upper echelons theory, particularly by integrating the idea of trait activation, as well as our knowledge of paranoia and stakeholder engagement.
The content-analytic measure of paranoia we develop and validate also contributes to the literature.

## theory
<!-- para 3 -->
Early studies in psychology focused on rare and clinical manifestations of paranoia (Cameron, 1943; Colby, 1981; Kramer, 2004).
However, scholars have increasingly shifted focus to the subclinical variant that is pervasive in society (Combs & Penn, 2004; Freeman, 2007; Freeman & Freeman, 2008; Freeman et al., 2005).
Indeed, paranoia is a dispositional trait (Combs & Penn, 2004: 94; Edens, Marcus, & Morey, 2009) appearing “on a spectrum from normality to pathology” (Oxman et al., 1982: 275) that engenders “feelings of mistrust and suspicion, whether of a particular person, a group of people, or an entire organization” (Savulich, Freeman, Shergill, & Yiend, 2015: 111).
Higher levels of paranoia are similar to an information-processing bias that focuses cognitive resources on threatening information, leading to heightened suspicion and perceptions of being threatened, persecuted, or harmed by others (Kramer, 2001).
<!-- para 4 -->
Some scholars have suggested that paranoia is an evolutionary response developed to assist people in detecting, anticipating, and addressing threats (Raihani & Bell, 2019).
That is, paranoia has been argued to represent “the inevitable cost of efficient threat perception—or ‘justified’ suspicion—that is necessary for survival of the human species” and thus, inevitably, manifests differently at times to facilitate survival when triggered by appropriate cues (Green & Phillips, 2004: 333).
Further, paranoid cognitions can be perceived akin to other types of traits recognized in social cognition research (i.e., the Big Five and Machiavellianism; Greenbaum, Hill, Mawritz, & Quade, 2017, Tett & Burnett, 2003) that exhibit general stability but can manifest in altered activations (e.g., Bentall, Corcoran, Howard, Blackwood, & Kinderman, 2001; Kramer, 2001; Mischel & Shoda, 1995; Nittel et al., 2018; Tett & Burnett, 2003).
<!-- para 5 -->
The information-processing biases from paranoia manifest in hyper-vigilance, with individuals higher in paranoia tending to incessantly scan and monitor their environments for perceived threats from, or vulnerabilities to, external entities (Fan, Klein, Bass, Springfield, & Pinkham, 2021; Moritz & Laudan, 2007).
In turn, this hyper-vigilance often leads individuals higher in paranoia to ruminate—or engage in “repetitive, recursive thinking” (Thoroughgood, Sawyer, & Webster, 2017: 102)—which further focuses attention on identified threats or vulnerabilities.
While hyper-vigilance and rumination shift cognitive resources toward identifying and monitoring threats, those higher in paranoia also tend to disproportionately believe that external entities both target them (known as self-as-target bias) and have malintent (known as sinister attribution bias) (Chan & McAllister, 2014; Combs & Penn, 2004; Freeman et al., 2005).
As Kramer and Gavrieli (2004: 255) noted, unlike “the sort of vigilant and careful consideration of the motives and causes influencing other people,” self-as-target and sinister attribution biases reflect “a tendency to go beyond what the data warrant.” As such, those higher in paranoia increasingly see negative events as driven by external entities’ intent to harm them (Kaney & Bentall, 1989; Kramer, 1998; Thoroughgood et al., 2017).
<!-- para 6 -->
Further, individuals higher in paranoia tend to form associations based on less information (Bentall et al., 2001; Combs, Penn, & Mathews, 2003; Garety, Hemsley, & Wessely, 1991), allowing them to jump to conclusions that support their suspicions (Dudley, John, Young, & Over, 1997; Freeman, Pugh, & Garety, 2008).
The rumination about threat-related information increases negative thinking and leads to pessimistic interpretations of information (Kramer & Gavrieli, 2004)—or, simply, suspicion confirmation.
As such, these characteristics (i.e., hyper-vigilance, rumination, self-as-target and sinister attribution biases, and jumping to conclusions) culminate with individuals higher in paranoia tending to search for perceived threats, ruminate on identified threats, selectively perceive new information in such a way as to support their prior suspicions, and jump to conclusions that they are being targeted for harm.
<!-- para 7 -->
Given that these cognitive processing tendencies affect how paranoia manifests in one's perceptions and attentional focus, it is perhaps logical that they also affect actions taken in response.
Specifically, paranoia tends to be associated strongly with safety behaviors, or behaviors intended to reduce the risk and magnitude of an external threat (Chan & McAllister, 2014).
To reduce such threats, individuals higher in paranoia primarily tend toward the safety behavior of avoidance, which aims "to reduce the strength or intensity of contact with a core threat" (Goetz, Davine, Siwiec, & Lee, 2016: 114) through less engagement with external entities that can cause negative outcomes (Freeman & Garety, 2004; Freeman et al., 2005).
Common examples of avoidance include exiting a room if people seen as threatening enter, and reducing dependence on external entities (e.g., for resources or information) (Chan & McAllister, 2014).
Studies have found that people higher in paranoia tend to avoid external entities to minimize the attention of suspicious and threatening others, as well as to reduce their own visibility to diminish the threat.
Avoidance also manifests in a reluctance to share information for fear that the information could be used against them (Robins & Post, 1997).
<!-- para 8 -->
While individuals higher in paranoia overwhelmingly tend toward avoidance to reduce perceived threats, in instances in which avoidance has been unsuccessful, these individuals tend to become aggressive to restore desired protection (Freeman et al., 2001).
Such aggressive actions mark a distinct shift from avoidance to directly engaging external entities to remedy the threats (e.g., by employing physical aggression to attack the source of the threat; Chan & McAllister, 2014; Freeman et al., 2001; Robins & Post, 1997).
<!-- para 9 -->
While paranoia is theoretically a distinct construct, with evidence of discriminant validity, from related traits (e.g., Fenigstein & Vanable, 1992), some of the information-processing tendencies noted above may overlap with other personality and motivational constructs.
In Table 1, we provide theoretical information about the discriminant validity of paranoia from other potentially related constructs (e.g., trust, locus of control, and prevention focus, among others).

<!-- para 15 -->
Upper echelons theory posits that a CEO's cognitive base (e.g., personality traits, values, experiences, etc.) serves "to filter and distort the decision maker's perception of what is going on and what should be done about it" (Hambrick & Mason, 1984: 195).
For instance, from a values perspective, CEO political ideology has been shown to impact corporate social responsibility (Chin et al., 2013), tax avoidance (Christensen, Dhaliwal, Boivie, & Graffin, 2015), and government interactions (Nalick, Kuban, Ridge, Zardkoohi, Bierman, & Schijven, 2023).
Similarly, regulatory focus has been shown to influence acquisition decisions (Gamache et al., 2015) and stakeholder strategies (Gamache, et al., 2020).
These two aspects of a CEO's cognitive base—political ideology and regulatory focus—are but a small sample of the immense literature linking a CEO's cognitive base to key strategic decisions such as stakeholder engagement.
<!-- para 16 -->
In this vein, and drawing on upper echelons theory, we suggest that paranoia is a key aspect of a CEO's cognitive base that affects their stakeholder engagement actions.
Specifically, given that paranoia is a relatively stable tendency that primarily manifests in the avoidance of external entities, we expect that organizations with CEOs higher in paranoia will tend to avoid actions that engage external stakeholders (i.e., actively avoiding actions that may inflame or create tensions with stakeholders) to prevent antagonizing them and potentially eliciting negative repercussions.
As we have chosen to consider two of the primary external and powerful stakeholders with which a firm must engage—government and competitors—we next will review how CEO paranoia may affect engagement with these two stakeholders.
<!-- para 17 -->
The primary way in which firms engage the government is through lobbying, which is conducted with the express intent of altering public policy to benefit the firm (Hillman, Keim, & Schuler, 2004).
<!-- para 18 -->
Lobbying activities are also a way for firms to signal their willingness to fight the decisions of a specific governmental agency in the political arena (Gordon & Hafer, 2005).
At its most fundamental level, firm lobbying provides information to government officials about the expected impact of proposed policies on a firm (Austen-Smith & Wright, 1996; Nownes, 2006), thus offering insight about the firm to powerful political leaders to alter the proposed policy or implementation of the policy.
Further, research has suggested that outcomes of lobbying may be influenced by lobbying breadth, which is defined as the number of government agencies and pieces of legislation targeted for information exchange (Ridge, Ingram, & Hill, 2017).
<!-- para 19 -->
Thus, a key component of lobbying is deciding how many agencies or pieces of legislation are targeted for information exchange.
As the number of governmental lobbying targets increase, so too does the number of government officials charged with both writing legislation and enforcing the associated laws and regulations with which information about the firm is distributed.
However, lobbying may also pose substantial risk to the firm because disclosing information to governmental officials requires firms to divulge their stances on public policy issues (and thus information about their interests, current and future expectations, etc.) while also trusting that the information will be used to benefit, rather than hurt, the company (Dahm & Porteiro, 2008).
<!-- para 20 -->
As previously argued, individuals higher in paranoia are suspicious of powerful external entities and expect those entities to target them with malevolent intent, making them reluctant to engage in actions that could antagonize them, or reluctant to share information with them (Kramer, 2001; Robins & Post, 1997).
Considering this with the fact that (a) lobbying is a signal of willingness to fight legislation and regulation (Gordon & Hafer, 2005), (b) lobbying entails information exchange with a powerful entity, and (c) lobbying breadth indicates that a greater amount of powerful individuals in the government have access to the provided information, we expect that higher-paranoia CEOs will avoid lobbying activity and thus engage in reduced levels of lobbying breadth.
Thus, we hypothesize:
<!-- para 21 -->
**Hypothesis 1.
CEO paranoia is negatively related to government lobbying breadth.**
<!-- para 22 -->
Competitors are also highly relevant to CEOs (Porter, 1985) because their actions (e.g., product launches, pricing battles) have the potential to harm the CEOs' firms (for a review, see Chen & Miller, 2012).
For example, Walmart's CEO, Doug McMillon, told one interviewer he has kept a photo on his phone of the top 10 retailers in the United States over the past few decades to remind him to maintain a healthy paranoia about competitors, because "you can see the rise and fall of Sears and others—it's just a reminder that this can happen to us, too" (Thomas, 2018).
As with lobbying, then, we expect firms with CEOs higher in paranoia to engage in less competitive actions to avoid the potential for retribution from those competitors.
That is, because a firm's competitive actions are likely to both make competitors aware of possible harm and increase the likelihood of those firms responding with attacks of their own, one way to avoid such concerns is by reducing antagonistic actions oneself (Chen, 1996).
<!-- para 23 -->
Based on upper echelons theory, we expect CEOs higher in paranoia to increasingly presume that competitors are keenly aware of their competitive actions because they see themselves as targets of external attention and expect malevolent responses to actions in which they engage.
Indeed, engaging in a competitive action, “even an indirect and weak one…can provoke fierce and forceful retaliation” (Chen & Miller, 2012: 154).
As such, we expect organizations helmed by CEOs higher in paranoia to engage in fewer competitive actions to reduce their competitive visibility and the likelihood of retaliatory attacks from competitors.
Thus, we hypothesize:
<!-- para 24 -->
**Hypothesis 2.
CEO paranoia is negatively related to firm competitive actions.**
<!-- para 26 -->
Beyond our expectation that CEOs higher in paranoia tend to avoid engaging powerful entities such as the government and competitors, we also expect that when prompted by trait-relevant cues the behavioral response of CEOs higher in paranoia may change (Chan & McAllister, 2014; Tett & Burnett, 2003).
In the context of stakeholder engagement, actions by stakeholders that target a CEO or the CEO's firm are paranoia-relevant cues that provide evidence that avoidance has not provided protection.
Therefore, instead of avoidance, aggressive actions that directly engage stakeholders are needed to indicate to the stakeholders that the CEO should not be targeted (Chan & McAllister, 2014: 54).
Given that CEOs have limited options in how they may engage stakeholders, we expect that aggression will manifest in the same actions discussed above (i.e., lobbying breadth and competitive actions); however, the relationship will switch to less avoidance and more aggression as negative cues from those stakeholders increase.
<!-- para 27 -->
From a governmental perspective, we expect that being targeted for more severe regulatory rulings is a cue that avoidance through reduced lobbying breadth has not offered protection.
As such, we expect firms with CEOs higher in paranoia to engage the government more aggressively by increasing lobbying breadth following more monetarily severe regulatory rulings.
Indeed, lobbying can “serve as a visible means by which corporations flex their muscles to potential adversaries, in particular, the bureaucracies charged with regulating them,” and one way firms do so is by “enhancing their political ‘footprint’ with more lobbying” (Gordon & Hafer, 2005: 245, 300).
We expect this for two related reasons.
<!-- para 28 -->
First, from the stance of a CEO higher in paranoia, since the firm avoided the government through reduced engagement in the form of lower lobbying breadth, the firm should have received little attention from this powerful entity.
However, attacks by this external entity (i.e., more severe rulings against the firm) cue that avoidance has failed to provide safety.
As such, more aggressive actions in the form of increased lobbying are likely seen as needed.
<!-- para 29 -->
Second, a firm receiving a more severe regulatory ruling requires government officials to take several steps.
The officials must identify possible regulatory issues, decide to investigate, issue a formal charge, ascertain guilt in formal proceedings, and determine an appropriate penalty.
Each of these steps is subjective to some degree, as officials assess and interpret many factors before issuing regulatory violations to a firm.
As such, the process may be perceived as targeted and malicious in nature by a CEO higher in paranoia.
More severe regulatory rulings may stem from problematic actions by the firm.
However, a CEO higher in paranoia is likely to view the rulings as unwarranted.
Indeed, the CEO is likely to view the firm as being singled out where the governmental agency actively focuses on the firm (self-as-target) with malevolent intent (sinister attribution).
The severity of regulatory rulings assessed by the government thus confirms suspicions of being targeted maliciously, acting as a paranoia-relevant cue.
Given that avoidance can be increasingly seen as not providing safety as regulatory rulings severity increases, CEOs higher in paranoia will respond with more lobbying to “flex their muscles” and restore a prior desired state of reduced government targeting.
<!-- para 30 -->
In sum, we posit that the negative relationship between CEO paranoia and lobbying breadth argued in Hypothesis 1 will be mitigated as regulatory ruling severity increases and prompts more aggressive lobbying action.
<!-- para 31 -->
**Hypothesis 3.
There will be a positive interaction effect of paranoia and regulatory ruling severity on lobbying breadth, such that the negative relationship between CEO paranoia and lobbying breadth is mitigated as regulatory ruling severity increases.**
<!-- para 32 -->
From a competitor perspective, we expect that CEOs higher in paranoia will perceive any actions directed at their firms as attacks by competitors.
Such actions are thus a paranoia-relevant cue that avoidance has been ineffective and that more aggressive actions are needed to reduce attacks.
(Chan & McAllister, 2014; Freeman et al., 2001).
We expect such a response because CEOs higher in paranoia will view an action directed at their firm through the self-as-target and sinister attribution biases.
In this way, a competitor's attack indicates that avoidant behavior through reduced competitive actions has not provided safety, and thus more aggressive actions that directly engage stakeholders will be taken to show the CEO's strength and discourage additional attacks (Chan & McAllister, 2014).
That is, because the primary manner in which CEOs are able to engage competitors and provide a signal of strength to discourage additional attacks is through competitive actions, we expect that the negative relationship between CEO paranoia and competitive actions will be mitigated as the firm increasingly uses aggression after being targeted by rivals.
Thus, we posit:
<!-- para 33 -->
**Hypothesis 4.
There will be a positive interaction effect of paranoia and rival attacks on competitive actions, such that the negative relationship between CEO paranoia and competitive actions is mitigated as rival attacks increase.**

## methods
<!-- para 3 -->
Our sample consists of all S&P 1500 firms listed in Execucomp from 2010–2017.
To measure CEO paranoia, we collected earnings call transcripts from CapitalIQ and then linked them to data collected from the Center for Responsive Politics, Thomson-Reuters, Compustat, USAspending.gov, goodjobsfirst.org, and RavenPack for control and dependent variable construction.
To appropriately focus only on statements made by a firm's CEO in the earnings calls, we first eliminated all speech by operators or analysts and retained only executive statements.
As there was no consistent indicator of executive title in the transcripts, we next fuzzy-matched the CEO name provided in Execucomp to the executive names in CapitalIQ, and then verified matches by hand to ensure that we were tracking only speech by the CEO in each earnings call.
Finally, we collapsed these earnings calls by firm-year (details on this process are provided in the Measurement section).
After data collection, merging, and reduction due to absent data (i.e., not all CEOs attend earnings calls, etc.), our final sample size was 925 CEOs across 774 firms for a total of 3,823 firm-year observations.
All the dependent variables are measured in year  $t + 1$, while all independent and control variables are measured in year t to establish temporal spacing.
<!-- para 4 -->
Dependent variables.
To assess our government engagement variable, lobbying breadth, we follow prior research and standardize and sum both the number of agencies and pieces of legislation a firm targeted with lobbying expenditures (Ridge et al., 2017).
This measurement provides an indication of the amount of unique governmental entities targeted for lobbying influence and thus information provision.
Competitive actions is measured using Raven-Pack; we use eight major types of competitive actions in which a focal firm engaged each year, identified by Connelly, Tihanyi, Ketchen, Carnes, and Ferrier (2017), that align with prior research: new product actions, capacity-related actions, pricing actions, marketing actions, acquisitions, strategic alliances, market expansion, and legal actions (see also Connelly, Lee, Tihanyi, Certo, & Johnson, 2019; Guo, Sengul, & Yu, 2020; Shi & DesJardine, 2022).
Thus, our primary measure is a count of the number of competitive actions taken by a firm in a year, with fewer indicating more avoidance and more actions indicating engaging with external entities with aggressive actions (e.g., Hughes-Morgan, Kolev, & McNamara, 2018).
<!-- para 5 -->
Independent variables.
We measure CEO paranoia by content-analyzing earnings calls.
The approach aids in capturing CEO traits given the technique provides both a nonintrusive and consistent avenue of assessment that can be directly compared across years.
This represents an advantage over surveys or interviews, which may lack the ability to track informants over long periods of time and have inherent risks of retrospective bias (Gamache et al., 2015).
Consistent with this notion, paranoia measurement has received significant attention in the psychology literature and has been shown to be effectively assessed via language use (Oxman et al., 1982).
Indeed, the usage of words to determine individual differences in psychology has a long history (Junghaenel, Smyth, & Santner, 2008; Pennebaker, Mehl, & Niederhoffer, 2003) and has been studied considerably for the measurement of paranoia (Oxman et al., 1982; Oxman et al., 1988).
For instance, Oxman et al. (1982: 275) used computerized content analysis of speech to discriminate between differing pathologies, finding that paranoia is “more identifiable than the effects of any other patient characteristic.” In a different study, Oxman et al. (1988: 467) found that “lexical content analysis may be superior to unstructured reading of transcripts by clinicians in cases where common diagnostic clues are not clearly conveyed." Thus, given the difficulties in measuring CEO traits through direct reports (Hambrick & Mason, 1984) and the accuracy with which content analysis can measure paranoia, we follow studies in the psychology literature using content analysis to measure CEO paranoia.
Unfortunately, there is no widely validated tool available to follow as a guide to capture paranoia via content analysis.
We thus developed a measure of paranoia through a rigorous process following prior work and best practices in content-analytic measurement (Gamache, et al., 2020; McKenny et al., 2013; Short et al., 2010).
<!-- para 6 -->
Specifically, our first step was to build on our extensive theoretical review to develop a clear description of potential underlying components of paranoia that can be provided to experts, along with the definition to begin developing a content-analytic dictionary.
As Kramer and Gavrieli (2004: 244) noted, the relatively stable tendencies toward suspiciousness, feelings of ill will or resentment, mistrust, and belief in external control or influence that define paranoia imply “a manifestation of underlying and pre-existing personality traits and dispositional tendencies.” Accordingly, we followed a process that views paranoia to be a construct consisting of multiple components as articulated in the definition and, building on our review, developed a list of theoretically derived components.
<!-- para 7 -->
This process of theoretical review led to the six underlying components that align with the definition of paranoia: anxiety, distrust, fear, obsession, suspicion, and threat.
Anxiety is essential to paranoia and plays a key role in the expectation of persecutory threats underlying the manifestation of the trait, an argument that has been provided with broad support (Freeman et al., 2001; Bird, Evans, Waite, Loe, & Freeman, 2019; McReynolds, 1960; Michail & Birchwood, 2009); distrust is a synonym of suspicion; fear is defined as an unpleasant emotion caused by the belief that someone or something is dangerous, likely to cause pain, or a threat, and thus aligns with the focus on external entities with potential to cause harm, explicitly; obsession, defined by ideas or thoughts that continually preoccupy or intrude a person's mind, is indicative of associated feelings; suspicion is defined by feelings or thoughts that something is possible and aligns with the feelings and thoughts of paranoia that external threats are indeed possible; and threat, specifically, is defined by the possibility that something (e.g., a natural disaster) or somebody (threat actor) will likely take advantage of a vulnerability.
Beyond the alignment with definitions of the components and the definition of paranoia, four components—distrust, fear, suspicion, and threat—are suggestive of hypervigilance, and two components—anxiety and obsession—are reminiscent of rumination, and thus capture key mechanisms of how paranoia manifests.
<!-- para 8 -->
After identifying components that align with paranoia, we then sought to validate our list.
To do so, we provided an alphabetized list of the components and others that are distinct from paranoia (and thus serve both as an attention check that raters do not simply rate “yes” or “no” on all components randomly and as a way to help establish convergent and discriminant validity; e.g., Short et al., 2010), along with a clear description of paranoia, to 20 management faculty members for validation that the components indicate our construct of paranoia. [^2] Using the 20 responses, we retained only those components that had more than 75% agreement (e.g., Gamache et al., 2015). [^3] Here, each of our six identified components were seen as indicative of paranoia, but no other components were, suggesting that our theoretical derivation is sound.
<!-- para 9 -->
Next, following exemplars (Gamache et al., 2015, 2020; McKenny et al., 2013; Short et al., 2010) we developed a deductive word list for each of the underlying components.
To do so, as recommended, we created an initial list of both derivations of the root word and synonyms for each component word, and, in turn, synonyms related to those words returned from the derivations of the root words and synonym words.
Specifically, we used a thesaurus and WordNet (a lexical database) to find words that are derivations of the root word and synonymous or closely related in meaning.
For instance, for anxiety, we included “anxious,” “anxiousness,” and “unease,” among others.
For suspicion, we used “suspicious,” “suspiciousness,” and the synonym “misgiving,” and so on.
This resulted in a deductively generated word list of 94 words for anxiety, 76 words for distrust, 59 words for fear, 93 words for obsession, 73 words for suspicion, and 110 words for threat.
After creating the deductive word list for each of the six components, we used classes of undergraduate students to help validate each component; specifically, each class rated one word list with the same sort task used above; we provided each student with the definition of one component and the related word list while asking them to indicate whether the words appropriately indicated the underlying component. [^4] In total, an average of 26 students rated each component.
After this rating, we retained words with 75% or higher agreement that the word appropriately indicated the underlying component (e.g., Gamache et al., 2015).
This resulted in a final word list with 46 words for anxiety, 26 words for distrust, 21 words for fear, 18 words for obsession, 19 words for suspicion, and 48 for threat. [^5] An unrotated, iterated principal factor analysis of these subcomponents in our sample indicates a single factor with an eigenvalue of 1.16 (i.e., above 1) explaining 67% of the variance.
<!-- para 10 -->
After the development of the word lists, we calculated the percentage of words utilized by each CEO in each earnings call representative of each underlying component (e.g., Gamache et al., 2015, 2020). [^6] We then averaged the percentage by call year and summed subcomponent yearly averages to obtain a yearly measure of CEO paranoia.
After calculating the measure, we sought to validate it against other characteristics that would indicate appropriate fit in the nomological network (Gamache et al., 2015; McKenny et al., 2013).
We thus considered multiple previously validated content-analytic measures that would theoretically be predicted by CEO paranoia.
Specifically, we collected validated measures of desperation, fixation, hardship, sadness, and negativity from LIWC and Diction 5.0, which contain common tools for content-analytic measures.
We first considered the correlation of our paranoia construct with each of these alternative measures, and then conducted CEO-level fixed-effect analysis to predict these measures.
Across each measure, the results are in the theoretically expected direction, with desperation, fixation, hardship, and sadness below the p < .05 threshold and negativity below the p < .10 threshold. [^7] We next assessed, consistent with the view of paranoia as a relatively stable tendency (Combs & Penn, 2004), whether the measure shows stability within CEO observations.
Specifically, we assessed the reliability of the measure within a CEO over time, finding sound consistency as indicated by a Cronbach's  $\alpha$ of 0.83.
This finding suggests a general consistency of paranoia over time within CEOs as captured by our measure.
We also assessed whether the measure exhibits adequate variability between CEOs in two ways.
First, we assessed the means and standard deviations of our measure as a single average measure for each CEO across the sample, [^8] finding an average mean value 0.0008 and standard deviation of 0.0005, which reflects variation between CEOs.
Second, as indicated by the mean and standard deviation across the entirety of the sample being equal (0.0008 and 0.0008, respectively; see footnote of Table 2, as values in Table 2 are reported at 0.00 and 0.00 to conform with journal formatting that uses two decimal places), there appears to be meaningful variability between CEOs over time as well.

Moderating variables.
The paranoia-relevant cue of regulatory rulings captures the result and severity of a regulatory process exacted on a firm using the penalty amount, in millions of U.S. dollars, for assessed rulings (values are from www.goodjobsfirst.org [^9]; see also Abdurakhmonov, Ridge, & Hill, 2021).
We capture the paranoia-relevant cue of rival attacks by counting the number of competitive actions in RavenPack (Connelly et al., 2017; Connelly et al., 2019; Guo et al., 2020; Shi & Desjardine, 2022) with the caveat that the focal CEO or firm is the target of the action rather than the initiator of the action (Hill, Recendes, & Ridge, 2019).
<!-- para 17 -->
Control variables. [^10] We include several control variables that may covary with the focal outcomes and independent variable.
We control for firm size (logarithm of total assets), market performance (measured as Tobin's Q; Kim & Bettis, 2014), and tax aggressiveness (measured using the logarithm of unrecognized tax benefits; Hasan, Hoi, Wu, & Zhang, 2014) because lobbying and competitive actions may be affected by all three (Ridge et al., 2017).
Likewise, there is reason to expect that each may covary with paranoia, given tendencies of those higher in the trait to avoid attention that comes with larger firms and to reduce risk associated with aggressive taxation (e.g., Freeman et al., 2005), along with the fact that higher-performing firms may be unlikely to select such individuals (i.e., Hambrick, 2007).
From a governmental perspective, we control for governmental subsidies that a firm receives (logarithm) and the amount of government contracting in which the firm engages (logarithm) given that these variables are potential outcomes of lobbying activity (Goldman, Rocholl, & So, 2013; Ridge et al., 2017).
Consistent with our theorizing of avoidance toward the government, we expect more paranoid CEOs to avoid seeking out both government subsidies and contracts.
Data on firm subsidies were collected from www.goodjobsfirst.com and government contract data were collected from usaspending.gov (Abdurakhmonov et al., 2021).
We also control for the logarithm of lobbying expenditures because lobbying breadth is limited as lobbying expenditures decreases and additionally will likely covary with the avoidance behavior of CEOs higher in paranoia (e.g., Freeman et al., 2001).
<!-- para 18 -->
To account for governance factors, we control for blockholder ownership as the logarithm of the equity value of blockholders, because, from an agency perspective, as blockholders are more invested they will provide a greater monitoring role over CEO actions, which could affect both the actions taken and CEO paranoia levels.
Similarly, to account for potential pushback by executives desiring greater strategic actions, we include TMT power, measured as CEO pay divided by the average pay of the next four highest-paid TMT members.
Both blockholders and TMT power additionally may influence the level of avoidance a CEO higher in paranoia exhibits, and potentially covary with paranoia to the degree to which the trait affects the desire of CEOs to select into conditions with more rigorous oversight (Hambrick, 2007).
Finally, at the CEO level, we include aspects of the CEO's pay that may incentivize strategic actions in the form of CEO compensation (measured as the logarithm of the CEO's total pay) and pay sensitivity (calculated as the proportion of total CEO pay, comprised of salary and bonus).
Since compensation is often used to elicit greater risk-taking from executives, there is theoretical reason to suggest that these variables likely covary with CEO paranoia (e.g., Freeman et al., 2005).
<!-- para 19 -->
We control for perceptions of political risk and nonpolitical risk using content analysis of quarterly earnings calls to identify two-word combinations that appear frequently in political science textbooks and newspapers (Hassan, Hollander, Van Lent, & Tahoun, 2019).
These variables indicate the level of risk that the firm's executives perceive in the market and non-market arena, and thus may impact the level of avoidance a firm exhibits.
We include these variables as the level of risk perceived by the TMT will likely covary with the level of CEO paranoia, given that the CEO provides the direction and instills the culture of the team as well.
Since our data are yearly and these variables are quarterly, we derive annual measures as the average quarterly values over each focal year.
<!-- para 20 -->
Selection correction.
We sample CEOs of S&P 1500 firms that are listed in Execucomp and Capita-LIQ, as well as being present on earnings calls.
The fact that some CEOs may be missing from the data may give rise to sample-induced endogeneity, or selection bias.
To address this possibility, we derive a selection correction (Certo, Busenbark, Woo, & Semadeni, 2016) by conducting a first-stage probit model predicting sample inclusion from the population.
The variables in the first-stage model include return on sales, return on equity, number of employees, logarithm of total assets, capital intensity, debt-to-equity ratio, as well as year and two-digit SIC dummies. [^11] Following this analysis, we calculate the inverse Mills ratio and include it in the second stage. [^12]

Lobbying breadth is left-censored since not all firms engage in lobbying activity, and thus we use a Tobit analysis for this dependent variable.
Competitive actions are measured as a count variable, and thus our analysis of this dependent variable uses a negative binomial model.
Two-digit SIC and year dummies are included to account for industry and temporal effects.
<!-- para 3 -->
A concern in testing how CEOs' characteristics like paranoia affect firm-level outcomes is endogeneity, especially from nonrandom hiring, which may mean CEO characteristics are not randomly distributed across firms (Hambrick, 2007).
Addressing endogeneity "involves a balancing act" (Semadeni et al., 2014: 1070) for two related reasons.
First, the effect of endogeneity can be inflationary or suppressive, meaning the coefficients are just as likely to be biased upward as downward, but there is no method to predict the direction (Hill et al., 2021).
Second, tools to "cure" any bias can introduce inflationary or suppressive biases themselves, and thus may be ill-advised if the concern is not a strong threat.
We therefore follow best practices to diagnose potential endogeneity and assess robustness across analyses (Hill et al., 2021; Semadeni et al., 2014).
<!-- para 4 -->
Specifically, we first use the robustness of inference to replacement (RIR) (Frank et al., 2021).
RIR results suggest that to alter our inferences for lobbying breadth, 675 observations would have to be replaced with observations for which there is an effect of zero, and for competitive actions, 1,793 observations would have to be replaced by observations for which the effect is zero.
Collectively, the large number of observations that must be replaced by zero to alter inferences suggests that our results are likely broadly robust to concerns of endogeneity.
Next, though RIR suggests endogeneity is unlikely to bias our estimates, we follow Chin et al. (2013) and suggestions by Semadeni et al. (2014) and Hill et al. (2021) to conduct robustness tests by pairing so-called “naïve” analyses that do not use tools to “cure” endogeneity with analyses that do attempt such a cure.
In addition to the main naïve analyses, we use two-stage residual inclusion (2SRI) as a cure, since the approach is amenable with censored and count dependent variables that we use to capture lobbying breadth and competitive attacks.
For the 2SRI, we use the measures of sadness and negativity discussed earlier in our validation of the measure of paranoia as instruments in the first-stage models predicting CEO paranoia.
We then derive the residual inclusion variable that we added in second-stage models testing the effect of CEO paranoia on our outcomes (Terza, Basu, & Rathouz, 2008).
We find that estimates are consistent across approaches (see Table A7 in the Appendix, in the Additional Materials section online), offering evidence that our results are robust to potential endogeneity (Hill et al., 2021; Semadeni et al., 2014). [^13]

Hypotheses 3 and 4 focus on moderating effects of paranoia-relevant cues for stakeholder engagement.
Specifically, Hypothesis 3 posits that the negative CEO paranoia–lobbying breadth relationship will be mitigated as the severity of regulatory rulings increases.
The negative direct effects of both CEO paranoia and regulatory rulings, coupled with the positive and significant interaction term between the two, in Model 3 of Table 3 (0.13; p < .01) offers support for Hypothesis 3.
Figure 1 shows that for CEOs who are high in paranoia (i.e., one standard deviation above the mean, or +1 SD), as regulatory rulings increase from the mean to one standard deviation above the mean, lobbying breadth more than doubles, increasing from about 0.06 to approximately 0.13.
<!-- para 22 -->
When considering evidence noted above that even small changes in lobbying can have significant impacts on firms (e.g., Richter et al., 2009; Ridge et al., 2017): this is a meaningful practical effect.
In particular, for firms helmed by CEOs high in paranoia, Figure 1 shows that CEO paranoia has a negative relationship with lobbying breadth at mean values of regulatory rulings indicative of avoidance.
However, consistent with Hypothesis 3, with higher values of regulatory rulings (here, +1 SD), the negative effect of CEO paranoia on lobbying breadth indicative of avoidance is not just weaker (i.e., less negative), but flips to positive to indicate more direct engagement (i.e., aggression).
The depiction in Figure 1 is thus consistent with our logic that being targeted by regulatory rulings activates different response tendencies, in effect cueing those higher in paranoia to switch from avoidance (which results in lower values of lobbying breadth) to aggression (which results in higher values of lobbying breadth).
For a direct interpretation of value, our findings constitute a change of 1% in lobbying breadth per $82 million increase in regulatory rulings against the firm.
<!-- para 23 -->
Hypothesis 4 posits that as rival attacks directed at CEOs higher in paranoia or their firms increase, the negative relationship between CEO paranoia and competitive attacks will weaken.
This hypothesis receives some support.
Specifically, in Model 3 of Table 4, there is a positive interaction coefficient (2.25; p = .01); however, the main effect of CEO paranoia is also positive (though not statistically significant), as noted in our prior discussion of Hypothesis 2.
These results suggest that competitive attacks that target CEOs and their firms do affect the manifestation of CEO paranoia in the level of competitive actions in which firms engage.
However, the attacks strengthen an already positive (albeit statistically insignificant) coefficient of CEO paranoia (9.55; p = .67 in Model 3 of Table 4).
Thus, there is some evidence of a positive interactive effect (again, 2.25; p = .01 in Model 3 of Table 4), which supports the idea of Hypothesis 4 that trait-relevant cues alter how CEO paranoia manifests in competitive actions.
To be clear, the effect of the interaction of rival attacks and CEO paranoia is not directly in line with our theorizing of weaker a negative effect on such attacks.
Rather, we find evidence that such cues strengthen a somewhat positive effect of the trait on competitive actions such that the CEOs take a more aggressive posture toward competitors, at least in terms of statistical significance.
Figure 2 illustrates this finding graphically, showing that for high paranoia CEOs (+1 SD), as rival competitive actions increase from the mean by one standard deviation, the firm engages in a little over 0.02 more competitive actions.
<!-- para 26 -->
This practical result is quite small, however, and thus should be considered with caution.
That is, to again try to be clear, the statistically significant effect we find for Hypothesis 4 does not appear to be particularly meaningful in practice.
<!-- para 27 -->
Broadly, the results for our theorizing relating CEO paranoia to competitive actions for both Hypotheses 2 and 4 are either not supportive or exhibit a pattern of results that is not particularly meaningful in practice.
When considered in concert with the results supporting Hypotheses 1 and 3, the findings suggest that the effects of CEO paranoia differ for different stakeholders, in this case the government and competitors.
For instance, it may be that government is viewed with more skepticism by CEOs with higher levels of paranoia because it has regulatory oversight that is exercised with political power in political markets, while competitors may receive less skepticism as competitive attacks may be viewed as an economic activity that is expected in the normal operation of competitive markets.
Or, more simply, different stakeholders may elicit a greater level of sinister attribution and malevolent intent depending on the relationship with the firm.
We revisit these possibilities in greater detail in the Discussion section.
<!-- para 29 -->
We conducted analyses of the underlying assumption that CEOs higher in paranoia will focus on threat- or risk-related information or focus their attention away from cooperative types of strategic alternatives.
To test risk perceptions, we utilized our perception of political and nonpolitical risk-control measures as dependent variables, and to test attentional focus we utilized recently developed content-analytic measures focusing on strategic attention, specifically on alliances and planning (Eklund & Mannor, 2021).
Results support our underlying assumptions, suggesting that CEOs higher in paranoia lead TMTs with a greater attention toward political and nonpolitical risk perceptions, decreased attention toward more vulnerable strategic relationships such as alliances, and increased attention toward strategic planning overall, each of which is consistent with expectations (see Table A8 in the Appendix).
<!-- para 30 -->
We also tested the sensitivity of our CEO paranoia measure to other potential constructs that may confound our results.
Specifically, we added CEO prevention and promotion focus, blame, jealousy, and loneliness to our models and then added the 13 unique strategic attention variables to our models; even after including these variables, the pattern of results remains similar in terms of statistical significance and support for respective hypotheses to those described above (see Tables A9 and A10 in the Appendix).
However, the coefficient for CEO paranoia is somewhat weaker in Table A9, with all the additional control variables in Model 3 (-83.57; p = .06).
This finding is likely expected, as added covariates that may predict the dependent variable and covary with the independent variable may suppress the effect somewhat.
At the same time, the interaction in Model 4 of Table A9 remains statistically significant (0.13; p = .01), but the practical effect decreases by about half with the added control variables.
Nonetheless, the interaction in Table A9 remains meaningful, as lobbying breadth is approximately 3% higher at higher regulatory ruling values, suggesting that the tendency to avoid stakeholders weakens as paranoia-relevant cues in the form of regulatory rulings prompt the incorporation of direct engagements consistent with more aggressive safety behaviors.
The pattern of results in Table A10 exhibits a somewhat stronger positive effect of CEO paranoia on competitive actions (ranging from 28.78 with p = .15 to 40.85 with p = .05 depending on which control variables are included; each of these is stronger than those in Table 4, with 21.90 and p = .30).
These findings may also be expected since adding covariates that predict the dependent variable and covary with the independent variable may inflate some estimates (Kalnins, 2018); nonetheless, the findings remain at odds with Hypothesis 2.
Results in Model 4 of Table A10 support the logic of Hypothesis 4 that being attacked by rivals may prompt CEOs higher in paranoia to seek safety by more directly engaging competitors, as is indicative of aggression (2.29; p = .00).
As with our main findings, the practical effect of the statistically significant interaction in A10 is quite small, suggesting that while being attacked seems to activate alternative mechanisms of CEO paranoia consistent with our logic, the degree to which it does so is not particularly strong.

## discussion
<!-- para 2 -->
Many studies have noted the importance of CEO characteristics in stakeholder engagement (e.g., Agile et al., 1999; Chin et al., 2013; Petrenko et al., 2016).
Despite this growing literature, to our knowledge no studies have addressed the role of CEO paranoia in how and why CEOs change the way they engage stakeholders.
Both omissions are notable given a host of executives publicly stating that paranoia is at play in such decisions, and the number of scholarly calls to better understand what may trigger a change stakeholder strategy (Gamache et al., 2020; Harrison et al., 2020; Kujala et al., 2022; Pedrini & Ferri, 2019).
In addition, CEO paranoia seems particularly salient to understanding stakeholder engagement and changes because the trait relates to individuals' views of, and actions toward, others (like stakeholders), and is subject to activation (e.g., Greenbaum et al., 2017; Tett & Burnett, 2003; Tett & Guterman, 2000), which may alter the way the trait relates to paranoia-driven views and actions (e.g., engaging stakeholders) (Chan & McAllister, 2014; Freeman et al., 2001).
<!-- para 3 -->
This study advances and tests theory linking CEO paranoia to stakeholder engagement and offers an initial foray into understanding how and why stakeholder engagement may change over time.
Using a content-analytic measure of paranoia developed and validated for this study, we find some support for our theorizing about the activation of CEO paranoia changing the way the trait manifests in stakeholder engagement.
Specifically, we provide evidence that CEO paranoia relates to stakeholder engagement with government in a manner consistent with the safety-seeking actions of avoidance (e.g., Freeman & Garety, 2004; Freeman et al., 2005).
We also find that perceived attacks trigger activation such that the typical relationship between paranoia and avoidance alters as CEOs shift toward more aggressively engaging a stakeholder (Chan & McAllister, 2014; Freeman et al., 2001).
However, we do not find support for a direct effect of CEO paranoia on avoiding engagement with competitors.
Rather than a negative direct effect as posited, we find that CEO paranoia positively (though not in a statistically significant manner) relates to engaging competitors.
We do, however, find evidence consistent with our logic that paranoia-relevant cues alter how the trait manifests in safety behaviors that are more aggressive rather than avoidant.
<!-- para 4 -->
The findings of our study collectively advance knowledge in several ways.
First, our study advances upper echelons theory and knowledge of the effects of CEO characteristics on firm-level outcomes by introducing CEO paranoia and highlighting how the mechanisms that link CEO traits to firm-level outcomes may change when triggered by trait-relevant cues (Freeman et al., 2002; Tett & Burnett, 2003; Tett & Guterman, 2000).
Specifically, we integrate into upper echelons theory the logic of trait activation and related theories to suggest that the manifestation of individuals' traits—though relatively stable—may be altered when triggered by trait-relevant cues (here, paranoia-relevant cues) (Freeman et al., 2001).
The evidence we find supporting our trait-activation theorizing extends upper echelons theory and calls for considering how other construct-relevant cues may alter how other CEO constructs manifest.
In particular, we find that with respect to engagements with government, a statistically significant relationship between paranoia and avoidance is weaker as paranoia-relevant cues indicating avoidance has not worked increase.
We also find that a statistically nonsignificant relationship between paranoia and avoidance of competitors alters in a significant fashion when paranoia-relevant cues indicate that aggression may be needed to provide safety.
These findings collectively offer insights into upper echelons theory by indicating how trait-relevant cues can alter the manifestation of traits on organizational outcomes.
<!-- para 5 -->
Our study also helps provide a more holistic view of executives.
Here, the literature has largely portrayed CEOs as self-assured attention seekers—views that are at odds with tendencies associated with paranoia—so much so that the most thorough reviews of executive research have devoted attention to such characteristics with nary a mention of paranoia or similar (Finkelstein et al., 2009; Neely et al., 2020; Smith, Hill, Wallace, Recendes, & Judge, 2018).
Yet, our findings provide evidence that not only is paranoia an important attribute to consider at the CEO level but that it impacts organizations in important ways.
<!-- para 6 -->
Second, this study advances our theoretical understanding of stakeholder engagement and changes to such actions over time.
Specifically, we develop and test theory that answers the call by Gamache et al. (2020) for a better understanding of why CEOs engage stakeholders as they do, while also advancing insights about why such engagement changes over time—a notion that extant theory has largely overlooked (Harrison et al., 2020; Kujala et al., 2022; Pedrini & Ferri, 2019).
More specifically, we follow others in using upper echelons theory to explain how CEO-level variables affect an organization's stakeholder engagement.
We advance knowledge of stakeholder engagement by moving beyond the largely used corporate social responsibility as an outcome that represents a broad focus on stakeholders (e.g., Agle et al., 1999; Chin et al., 2013; Petrenko et al., 2016) to focus on specific engagements with government and competitors.
We also advance this line of inquiry by introducing a new CEO trait and associated theory about how the manifestation of CEO traits broadly (Mischel & Shoda, 1995; Tett & Burnett, 2003; Tett & Guterman, 2000), and paranoia specifically (Chan & McAllister, 2014; Freeman et al., 2002), may change when paranoia-relevant cues activate different response tendencies.
Our study thus advances the theoretical understanding of stakeholder engagement in the form of avoidance toward certain stakeholders, broadly.
Our study also identifies how shifts in stakeholder engagement may occur; specifically, when trait-relevant cues indicate that avoidance has not rendered safety, CEOs increasingly shift toward non-avoidant actions that directly engage external entities with aggression to restore desired states of safety.
Thus, our findings imply that CEO paranoia manifests in paradoxical ways, where CEOs initially engage in avoidance yet, when avoidance does not work, more directly engage with stakeholders, as is consistent with aggression (e.g., Freeman et al., 2001).
<!-- para 7 -->
Important to our findings, we also find complexity with respect to the way the trait manifests for engagement with different stakeholders.
Consistent with our theorizing, we find that CEO paranoia exhibits a statistically significant association with avoidance of governmental stakeholders.
At odds with our theorizing, however, we find that CEO paranoia is not systematically related to engagement with competitors, and even leans slightly toward aggression rather than avoidance (the other end of the dynamic continuum).
This finding not only points to interesting differences between stakeholders that pose questions for future research.
Here, it may be that competitors or government are unique from other stakeholders in ways that affect engagements with such stakeholders, and thus that the unique aspects should be investigated.
Beyond the direct relationships with engagement, we also find evidence that paranoia-relevant cues—whether from government or from competitors—seem to alter the manifestation of CEO paranoia in stakeholder engagement.
This finding also presents avenues for future work into why direct effects differ across stakeholders but yet the cues seem to trigger more aggression in both cases.
<!-- para 8 -->
Finally, this paper also contributes in terms of methods.
To our knowledge, this is the first linguistic measure of paranoia created and levered in management research.
This measure is particularly useful when surveys of CEOs are not possible; moreover, our development and validation process follows research in psychology suggesting that measuring paranoia via content analysis is not only possible but highly valid (Oxman et al., 1982).
In turn, our measure can help facilitate inquiry into paranoia in CEOs but also enable scholars to capture paranoia in other populations using linguistic methods.
<!-- para 10 -->
While this paper focuses on particularly powerful external entities to establish the importance of CEO paranoia, it also has some limitations, as paranoia may manifest differently when considering different stakeholders.
This opens the door to future research.
For example, given the hyper-vigilance and sinister attribution tendencies of CEOs higher in paranoia, paranoia could provide explanatory power for both the selection of partner firms for cooperative strategy or for acquisitions, or, potentially, the way they engage with entities with which they have relationships, such as investors, boards of directors, suppliers, and buyers, among others.
Moreover, given that paranoia tends to elicit the sinister attribution tendency and is developed through competition (Raihani & Bell, 2019), considering CEO paranoia through a tournament theory or structural interdependence (Hambrick, Humphrey, & Gupta, 2015) lens could provide further insight into how competition and paranoia impact organizations, conceivably via TMT functioning.
It may be that TMTs with executives higher in paranoia view each other with more distrust, thus minimizing cohesiveness and hurting their organizations.
Future work may also wish to use an agency theory perspective.
For instance, arguments in the legal field have suggested that certain actions are agency costs reflecting CEOs' interests rather than those of shareholders (Bebchuk & Jackson, 2010).
Our findings that CEOs higher in paranoia lobby less may reveal that they are not subject to lobbying-based agency costs but may have paranoia-related drivers that determine their approach to stakeholder engagement.
Future research could also explore how CEO paranoia is likely to influence a variety of critical strategic management behaviors, beyond lobbying and competitive actions.
Here, measuring strategies toward stakeholders has long proven a challenge (Gamache et al., 2020), but it is our hope that our findings also provide a basis for delving more deeply into stakeholder differences.
<!-- para 11 -->
Beyond laying the groundwork for future research on other types of stakeholders, our work opens the door to a more balanced view of paranoia (Smith et al., 2018) and CEO traits.
That is, paranoia has been traditionally seen as a largely “dark” trait—for example, associated with full-fledged delusions—and, for this reason, may be seen as unlikely in executives (e.g., Hiller & Hambrick, 2005).
We, however, highlight that subclinical paranoia is pervasive and present in executive settings, suggesting that not all CEOs are the confident, attention-seeking types and that such considerations merit additional attention.
Further, we suggest that there are likely some “bright” sides of the trait insofar as the associated tendency toward safety may manifest in a wide variety of positive outcomes.
For example, CEOs with elevated levels of paranoia may avoid unnecessary information sharing or attacks with little upside to their firms.
Future research may thus wish to delve more deeply into CEO paranoia to identify both bright and dark aspects, as well as the balance of the trait in total.
To this end, CEOs with elevated levels of paranoia might push their firms toward better risk-management practices, such as more prudent approaches to supply chain partners with a robust system of diversification and verification, instead of reliance on verbal trust and promises.
On the other hand, reduced trust by a CEO might result in missed partnerships due to a CEO’s personal mistrust of newcomers and outsiders.
Moreover, elevated CEO suspicion levels might result in strategic decision-making that relies either too little on certain individuals (e.g., less well-known but potentially more competent outsiders) or too much on others (e.g., trusted confidants).
These questions pose ample opportunities for future research to shed light on paranoia and firm-level outcomes, particularly regarding the totality of bright and dark aspects.
<!-- para 12 -->
Trait activation theory and related views also pose avenues for future inquiry into how relatively stable traits manifest differently in the wake of trait-relevant cues.
Prior work has largely treated CEO traits as relatively stable, implying that their effects manifest in broadly consistent ways over time.
Integrating trait activation and related theories may offer a means to more fully understand how CEO traits manifest.
Though such logic may not apply to all CEO traits, it stands to reason that as with theorizing at the individual level, certain traits may manifest differently when triggered to do so.
To this end, our study may help researchers advance a similar logic to explain inconsistent findings in prior work by highlighting why a trait relates a certain way generally but can be altered when activated by trait-relevant cues.
Such logic seemingly can apply directly to future inquiries seeking to explain why firms change stakeholder engagement approaches over time, as well as to investigations of other strategic adjustments.
For example, various CEO traits have been linked to acquisition behaviors, but our theorizing about trait activation suggests the possibility that such relationships are triggered by trait-relevant cues.
Examining extant relationships considering such cues, then, may render more nuanced insights than viewing traits as consistently manifesting in outcomes.
<!-- para 13 -->
Relatedly, we follow best practices in developing and validating a content-analytic measure of CEO paranoia, but the context of earnings calls we use as a basis of testing our hypothesis merits attention.
For example, it may be that the relatively stable economic time period we sample has given way to meaningful earnings call changes in the post-pandemic world, or that specific firm-level contexts and associated queries or approaches to earnings calls can serve as important cues.
We encourage future research not only to further validate our measure in other settings but also to investigate the immediate and broader contexts of firms and earnings calls.
<!-- para 15 -->
At odds with the stereotypical expectation of executives being attention-seeking, we examine the effect of CEO paranoia on firms' stakeholder engagement.
Extending the logic of upper echelons theory that CEO traits affect firm-level actions such as stakeholder engagement, we also draw attention to how CEO paranoia may manifest differently when trait-relevant triggers cue the trait to activate in different ways.
Using a content-analytic measure of CEO paranoia developed and validated for our study, we find evidence consistent with our logic of trait activation that the manifestation of CEO traits on organizational outcomes may be altered by trait-relevant cues.
Specifically, we find that CEOs higher in paranoia tend to seek safety by avoiding government stakeholders, but such avoidance does not hold with competitors.
Indeed, CEOs higher in paranoia may even lean slightly toward engagement when it comes to competitors.
In both cases, however, the relationship is altered when triggered by stakeholder actions toward CEOs and their firms that prompts more aggressive actions.
We hope that this research serves as a catalyst for future studies on how CEO paranoia affects firms, and helps expand our view of upper echelons theory to identify how characteristic-relevant cues might affect how CEO variables such as paranoia manifest.
We also hope our study helps scholars in identifying pathways as to how various triggers may change CEOs' stances toward stakeholder engagement.
