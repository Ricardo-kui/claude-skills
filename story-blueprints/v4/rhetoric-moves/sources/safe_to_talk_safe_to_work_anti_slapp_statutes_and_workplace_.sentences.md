# safe_to_talk_safe_to_work_anti_slapp_statutes_and_workplace_ 句子库存

## introduction
<!-- para 2 -->
Corporate governance has traditionally focused on mechanisms operating within the boundaries of a firm's economic relationships—such as contracts, boards, managerial incentives, shareholder voting, and market discipline—or on external legal and regulatory mandates, including disclosure rules, fiduciary duties, and oversight of boards and accounting standards (Berle and Means, 1932; Jensen and Meckling, 1976; Jensen, 1986; Shleifer and Vishny, 1997; Ma and Shleifer, 2025).
Yet, as firms increasingly confront the externalities of their decisions and the social legitimacy of their practices, governance is no longer confined to investors, boards, market forces, and regulators; it now extends to a broader set of non-financial stakeholders whose ability to hold firms accountable depends critically on freedom of speech (Donaldson and Preston, 1995; Karpoff 2021; Starks, 2023; Chen et al., 2025c; Lee et al., 2025).
Unlike traditional financial stakeholders, these actors lack contractual or legal levers and instead depend on public discourse, whistleblowing, and media reporting to surface concealed risks (Core et al., 2008; Dyck et al., 2008; Heese and Pérez Cavazos, 2021; Heese and Pacelli, 2024).
This dependence makes the legal environment governing speech, particularly anti-SLAPP laws designed to prevent corporations from silencing critics, central to modern stakeholder governance.
Against this backdrop, we ask whether stronger speech protections can discipline firms in ways that enhance workplace safety, a domain of substantial public concern where information asymmetry is acute and the costs of silence are severe.
<!-- para 3 -->
We investigate this question in the context of anti-strategic lawsuit against public participation (anti-SLAPP) statutes, which protect individuals from meritless lawsuits intended to silence public criticism or disclosures of matters of public concern (Pring and Canan, 1996; Burke, 2020).
By enabling defendants to seek early dismissal of frivolous claims and, in many states, to recover attorneys' fees, anti-SLAPP statutes reduce the expected legal and financial costs of speaking out (Chen et al., 2025c; Lee et al., 2025).
We therefore hypothesize that the introduction of anti-SLAPP protections strengthens stakeholder voice in ways that influence firm safety practices.
When employees and external observers face lower risks of retaliatory litigation, they are more likely to raise concerns about unsafe practices and operational risks (Dyck et al., 2010; Berger and Lee, 2022; Call et al., 2022; Heese and Pacelli, 2024).
<!-- para 4 -->
Anticipating heightened internal and external scrutiny, firms have stronger incentives to mitigate safety hazards proactively (Wilde, 2017; Gao et al., 2020; Guernsey et al., 2025).
Accordingly, we expect the adoption of anti-SLAPP laws, and the resulting enhancement of speech protections, to be associated with fewer workplace injuries.
<!-- para 5 -->
To identify the effect of anti-SLAPP laws on workplace safety, we exploit their staggered adoption across U.S. states and estimate a stacked difference-in-differences (DiD) model using establishment-level injury data.
Our empirical strategy compares changes in injury rates and counts between establishments of the same firm located in states that adopt anti-SLAPP laws and those in states that have not yet done so.
This within-firm, cross-establishment design offers a distinct advantage: it isolates the impact of changes in the state legal environment on establishment-level safety outcomes while holding constant all firm-wide determinants of workplace safety, thereby providing a clean lens through which to observe how stronger speech protections translate into improved workplace outcomes.
<!-- para 6 -->
Our empirical specification further strengthens identification by incorporating cohort-interacted establishment and firm-year fixed effects.
The cohort-establishment fixed effects absorb time-invariant heterogeneity in safety risk and local working conditions within each adoption cohort and establishment, while the cohort-firm-year fixed effects capture contemporaneous firm-level changes, such as shifts in management, production intensity, or financial performance, that could affect all of a firm's establishments simultaneously.
Together, these fixed effects remove major sources of confounding factors and ensure that identification is driven solely by differences in legal environment across establishments of the same firm in the same year.
Moreover, the stacked DiD framework mitigates biases common in conventional staggered-adoption designs by comparing treated establishments only to not-yet-treated establishments within the same firm and cohort, rather than relying on comparisons across early and late adopters (Goodman-Bacon, 2021; Roth et al., 2023; Sun and Abraham, 2021).
<!-- para 7 -->
We use two measures to capture workplace injuries, namely injury rate, defined as the number of injuries divided by total hours worked, and injury count, the total number of injuries.
Our baseline estimates show that the enactment of state-level anti-SLAPP laws is associated with a significant reduction in workplace injuries.
Across multiple specifications, establishments in states that adopt anti-SLAPP statutes exhibit lower injury rates and fewer total injuries compared with establishments of the same firm located in states that have not yet adopted the statue.
These results are robust to alternative modeling approaches that account for the count nature of injury data, as well as to analyses focusing on severe injuries, industries with high tangible asset intensity, and establishment-year observations with positive injuries.
<!-- para 8 -->
Dynamic analyses indicate that pre-treatment trends in treated and control establishments are statistically indistinguishable, alleviating concerns about differential pre-trends or anticipatory behavior ahead of expected legislation (Roberts and Whited, 2013).
Following adoption, particularly in the first two years, workplace injuries decline noticeably, consistent with safety-enhancing adjustments implemented shortly after enactment.
Moreover, the effects are stronger in states with more robust anti-SLAPP statutes, suggesting that statutory strength amplifies the safety benefits.
Together, these findings provide consistent evidence that legal protections for public participation contribute to improved workplace safety outcomes.
<!-- para 9 -->
To better understand why anti-SLAPP statutes improve workplace safety, we examine cross-sectional heterogeneity in their effects.
If these statutes operate by increasing the likelihood that negative information about unsafe conditions reaches external audiences, their impact should be greater where such exposure carries more serious consequences.
We focus on two sources of heterogeneity that capture these differential consequences.
The first is workforce retention pressure.
When retaining employees is challenging, workplace injuries become more costly, as they can trigger dissatisfaction, turnover, or labor disputes, which in turn strengthens establishments' incentives to reduce safety risks (Dai et al., 2022).
Consistent with this prediction, we find significantly larger declines in workplace injuries in industries with higher labor mobility, where workers can more easily switch employers, and in markets with greater rival concentration, where competition for talent is more intense.
<!-- para 10 -->
The second source is local reputational pressure.
Unsafe conditions revealed through protected speech are likely to be more damaging in communities that impose stronger social or political sanctions on firms perceived as irresponsible (Hasan et al., 2017; Hoi et al., 2018; Hoi et al., 2019).
We capture this dimension using two measures: the county’s share of votes for the Democratic Party, as a proxy for stakeholder-oriented social preferences, and the county’s social capital index, which reflects the density of social networks and strength of local norms.
In both cases, safety improvements associated with anti-SLAPP laws are significantly larger in establishments located in communities with higher reputational pressure.
<!-- para 11 -->
These cross-sectional patterns suggest that anti-SLAPP statutes are more effective in contexts where establishments and firms face higher potential costs from the exposure of unsafe conditions, reinforcing the credibility of our results.
For an omitted factor to fully explain our findings, it would need to coincide with the staggered timing of anti-SLAPP adoptions, replicate the variation in statutory strength, and simultaneously generate the heterogeneous responses we observe across workforce retention and local community characteristics.
Such a factor is difficult to conceive.
In this sense, the cross-sectional evidence provides additional assurance that the observed reduction in workplace injuries is driven by the enactment of anti-SLAPP statutes rather than by unobserved confounding factors.
<!-- para 12 -->
In our final set of tests, we examine the mechanisms through which establishments affected by anti-SLAPP statutes improve workplace safety.
While these statutes may prompt firm-wide initiatives, such as broad safety campaigns or new compliance systems, our within-firm, cross-establishment design with firm-year fixed effects absorbs such centralized responses.
As a result, the safety improvements we observe are likely driven by establishment-level actions rather than uniform corporate changes.
<!-- para 13 -->
We identify two mechanisms that could underlie these improvements.
First, anti-SLAPP laws increase headquarters' incentives to monitor establishments more closely.
By strengthening the credibility of employee complaints and external scrutiny, these laws make unsafe practices more visible, motivating headquarters to detect and correct hazards proactively.
Consistent with this prediction, the injury-reducing effect is stronger for establishments located in the same state as, or closer to, headquarters, where monitoring is easier and less costly.
Second, anti-SLAPP statutes encourage firms to alleviate workload pressures that heighten injury risk.
Enhanced speech protections make employee concerns about unsustainable work demands more credible and consequential, prompting establishments to reduce workloads and, when feasible, increase staffing.
These adjustments lower operational pressures, reduce fatigue-related hazards, and improve compliance with safety protocols.
Supporting this mechanism, we find that establishments in adopting states exhibit fewer hours worked per employee and are more likely to expand staffing.
Overall, these findings illustrate how legal protections for free speech can translate into tangible improvements in workplace safety.
<!-- para 14 -->
Our study makes three key contributions to the literature.
First, we extend the emerging research on the firm-level consequences of anti-SLAPP laws.
Prior research shows that these statutes shape corporate information environments by increasing ESG disclosures (Griffin et al., 2024), reducing the concealment of adverse information (Chen et al., 2025c; Lee et al., 2025), and strengthening whistleblower activity (Böke et al., 2025).
Consistent with these effects, Guernsey et al. (2025) find that anti-SLAPP laws lower firms' cost of equity by enhancing transparency and reducing uncertainty in capital markets.
This literature has primarily examined changes in disclosure, information flows, and financing conditions.
We broaden this perspective by documenting that anti-SLAPP laws also have operational consequences that directly affect employees.
<!-- para 15 -->
Second, we contribute to a growing body of research on workplace safety (Cohn and Wardlaw, 2016; Caskey and Ozel, 2017; Guardado and Ziebarth, 2019; Cohn et al., 2021; Bradley et al., 2022; Charles et al., 2022; Hope et al., 2022; Qian et al., 2023; Chen et al., 2025b).
This literature spans finance, accounting, and management, reflecting widespread scholarly interest in the determinants of workplace injuries.
Within finance, prior studies show that financing constraints limit firms' safety investments (Cohn and Wardlaw, 2016), that workplace injuries decline following leveraged buyouts (Cohn et al., 2021), and that rank-and-file employee stock options reduce injury rates (Chen et al., 2025b).
In accounting, research examines how earnings pressure, information quality, and firms' reporting environments shape safety outcomes.
Caskey and Ozel (2017) find that pressure to meet earnings targets undermines safety; Hope et al. (2022) show that higher internal information quality is associated with fewer injuries; and Bradley et al. (2022) document a negative association between analyst coverage and injury rates.
Management research focuses more on managerial traits, with Qian et al. (2023) showing that firms led by loss-averse CEOs experience fewer injuries than those led by more gain-oriented CEOs.
<!-- para 16 -->
A central insight from this diverse literature is that firm characteristics, incentives, and information environments materially influence injury rates.
In contrast to this primarily firm-specific focus, we highlight the role of the broader legal environment—specifically, statutory protections for public participation and criticism—in shaping workplace conditions.
By showing that anti-SLAPP laws reduce workloads and strengthen headquarters monitoring, thereby lowering injury rates, we demonstrate that workplace safety responds not only to internal governance and incentives but also to the broader regulatory infrastructure governing speech and accountability.
Our findings thus encourage a more holistic view of the antecedents of workplace safety, incorporating legal and institutional features that affect how risks are surfaced and addressed within firms.
<!-- para 17 -->
Third, our paper contributes to research on stakeholder governance.
A growing literature documents how firms are held accountable through media coverage (Core et al., 2008; Dyck et al., 2008), public discourse (Chen et al., 2025c; Lee et al., 2025), local newspapers (Chen et al., 2025a; Heese et al., 2022), social media (Heese and Pacelli, 2024), and employee whistleblowing (Heese and Pérez Cavazos, 2021).
We show that legal protections for free speech strengthen public scrutiny by safeguarding the channels through which stakeholders can observe, report, and respond to unsafe practices.
This underscores an important institutional context: stakeholder scrutiny is more likely to be effective when the legal environment ensures that employees and the public can communicate concerns without fear of retaliation.
Our findings suggest that policymakers seeking to improve workplace safety should consider legal frameworks that protect reporting and public participation, as these measures can promote safer and more accountable firm behavior.
<!-- para 20 -->
SLAPPs refer to lawsuits, or threats of litigation, filed primarily to punish, intimidate, or silence individuals who engage in public discourse or disclose matters of public concern, rather than to resolve a legitimate legal claim (Pring and Canan, 1996).
Early literature documents how powerful actors, including corporations, have used SLAPPs to discourage public stakeholders (e.g., journalists, employees, consumers, and community groups) from speaking out about harmful practices (Tate, 2000; Hartzler, 2007; Lamont, 2012; Brendel and Ryans, 2021).
The primary harm of a SLAPP lies less in its legal merits and more in its chilling effect on subsequent speech, including that of third-party observers.
By intimidating or burdening the target, even a legally weak lawsuit can suppress speech through the financial costs, stress, and delays associated with litigation (Barker, 1993; Tate, 2000).
Consequently, standard legal protections, such as merits review in defamation or tort cases, may be insufficient to address the threat SLAPPs pose to freedom of expression (Barker, 1993; Borg-Barthet et al., 2021).
<!-- para 21 -->
In response to the growing problem of abusive lawsuits, many states began adopting anti-SLAPP statutes in the late 1980s.
By 2022, 32 states (including the District of Columbia) had enacted such laws, although there is still no federal anti-SLAPP statute (Chen et al., 2025c).
Anti-SLAPP laws are designed to protect individuals from strategic lawsuits filed to silence or intimidate them for speaking out on matters of public interest.
These statutes typically allow a defendant to request an early dismissal of the lawsuit, before the case becomes time-consuming and costly (Pring and Canan, 1996; Burke, 2020).
Once the defendant files this request, the plaintiff must demonstrate that their claim is likely to succeed; otherwise, the case can be dismissed.
This higher evidentiary standard makes it more difficult for plaintiffs to pursue weak or frivolous claims and makes it easier for defendants to defend themselves (Chen et al., 2025c).
As a result, uncertainty and litigation costs for defendants are reduced.
<!-- para 22 -->
In addition, many anti-SLAPP statutes allow prevailing defendants to recover attorneys' fees.
This further lowers the financial burden and improves access to legal representation, particularly for defendants with limited resources facing meritless suits (Chen et al., 2025c; Lee et al. 2025).
Consistent with the view that these statutes provide meaningful safeguards against frivolous SLAPPs, Lee et al. (2025) find that, after enactment, both the number and duration of such cases decrease significantly in treatment states relative to other states.
Overall, anti-SLAPP statutes reduce the expected costs of defending against meritless claims and strengthen legal protections for those who speak out on matters of public concern.
As a result, they help mitigate the chilling effects on both SLAPP targets and potential witnesses, who may otherwise remain silent out of fear of retaliation.
<!-- para 23 -->
The staggered adoption of anti-SLAPP statutes across U.S. states provides a powerful institutional setting in which certain states gain enhanced free-speech protections at a specific point in time, while other states without such laws do not (yet).
Because firms may maintain establishments in multiple states, establishments of the same firm can be compared in jurisdictions with versus without anti-SLAPP legislation, effectively controlling for firm-level unobservables such as managerial quality, governance structure, corporate culture, and internal reporting systems.
This setting creates plausibly exogenous variation in the legal environment surrounding speaking out on negative aspects of corporate matters (Chen et al., 2025c; Guernsey et al., 2025; Lee et al., 2025), enabling a credible empirical design to isolate the effect of stronger speech protections on workplace outcomes.
Moreover, because anti-SLAPP laws are motivated by the protection of public participation—not workplace regulation—their adoption is unlikely to be driven by contemporaneous changes in establishment-level safety policies, which helps mitigate endogeneity concerns.
<!-- para 25 -->
From a broader perspective, anti-SLAPP statutes not only reshape the legal landscape for public participation but also affect information flows, corporate accountability, and internal organizational practices (Lewis, 1980; Schauer, 2011; Ginsburg and Huq, 2018; Griffin et al., 2024; Wu et al., 2025).
At their core, these laws enhance the credibility of external scrutiny by lowering the expected costs of speaking out and expanding legal protections for individuals who disclose harmful or undesirable corporate practices (Böke et al., 2025; Guernsey et al., 2025; Lee et al., 2025).
Although anti-SLAPP statutes are not designed to regulate workplace behavior directly, they alter the strategic environment in which firms operate, effectively shifting the expected costs of neglecting safety hazards, operational risks, and other negative externalities (Murphy and Moerman, 2018; Freund et al., 2023; Wu et al., 2025).
Consequently, establishments located in states with stronger speech protections face substantially different incentives compared with those in jurisdictions lacking such legal safeguards.
<!-- para 26 -->
Workplace safety is fundamental to employee well-being, and failures in this domain can impose severe physical, psychological, and economic costs on workers (Cohn and Wardlaw,
<!-- para 27 -->
2016).
Yet many jobs remain inherently risky, and the potential for harm is non-trivial in everyday operations.
Figure 1 illustrates the distribution of workplace injuries in the U.S. in 2011 using data from the Bureau of Labor Statistics (BLS).
The leading causes of injuries include physical overexertion, contact with objects, and falls, all of which are hazards common across a wide range of industries.
The most prevalent injury types are sprains, strains or tears, soreness and pain, cuts and lacerations, bruises and contusions, and fractures.
These categories underscore that a large share of workplace injuries arises from routine operational pressures, task overload, and lapses in adherence to safety procedures.
As such, even seemingly minor failures in safety management can meaningfully erode workers' well-being, reduce productivity, and increase firms' exposure to medical, legal, and reputational costs.
<!-- para 28 -->
A longstanding literature in industrial relations and occupational safety emphasises that workers and other frontline stakeholders, such as maintenance technicians and line managers, are the primary “sensors” of hazardous conditions (Chan, 1979; Moore and Viscusi, 1990; Kniesner and Viscusi, 2005; Ruser and Butler, 2010; Cai et al., 2022).
They observe unsafe practices in real time, understand the operational pressures under which injuries occur, and possess tacit knowledge that formal oversight systems often fail to capture (Garicano, 2000; Edmondson, 2002; Stubben and Welch, 2020).
However, the effectiveness of this decentralised oversight depends critically on whether individuals feel able to raise concerns without fear of retaliation or costly legal conflict (Chen et al., 2025c; Lee et al., 2025).
When legal threats or strategic lawsuits can be used to silence or intimidate critics, a chilling effect arises (Barker, 1993; Tate, 2000): workers may remain silent about understaffing, heavy workloads, or malfunctioning equipment; third-party observers may avoid documenting unsafe operations; and local safety concerns may fail to escalate to senior management or reach outside audiences.
Such muted stakeholder voice weakens internal oversight and reduces the external visibility of safety-relevant issues (Hartzler, 2007).
<!-- para 29 -->
Anti-SLAPP statutes attenuate these chilling dynamics.
By enabling defendants to secure early dismissal of meritless claims and, in many states, to recover attorneys' fees, these statutes materially reduce the expected consequences of criticising corporate practices (Pring and Canan, 1996; Burke, 2020).
In doing so, they lessen the suppressive pressure that strategic litigation can impose on speech related to matters of public concern, including workplace safety.
Workers, community members, and other observers become more willing and able to voice concerns, both internally and externally, when statutory protections lower the risk of retaliatory lawsuits (Chen et al., 2025c; Lee et al., 2025).
Just as importantly, a strengthened legal environment raises firms' expectations that whistleblowing, third-party reports, social media activity, or media coverage of unsafe practices may become more frequent and more visible (Dyck et al., 2010; Berger and Lee, 2022; Call et al., 2022; Heese and Pacelli, 2024).
As the likelihood of external exposure increases, managers have stronger incentives to mitigate risks pre-emptively before they escalate into observable incidents (Wilde, 2017; Gao et al., 2020).
From the firm's perspective, anti-SLAPP laws heighten the expected costs of ignoring safety hazards—not only reputationally, but also in terms of potential legal and stakeholder backlash once information becomes more difficult to suppress (Guernsey et al., 2025).
<!-- para 30 -->
In this environment, managers may respond by strengthening internal safety measures, revising operational procedures, and reassessing how resources are allocated to risk mitigation (Cohn and Wardlaw, 2016; Heese and Pérez-Cavazos, 2020; Cohn et al., 2021; Hope et al., 2022).
Two responses at the establishment level are particularly noteworthy.
<!-- para 31 -->
The first is enhanced headquarters monitoring.
When speech is more strongly protected, the scope for concealing unsafe practices narrows (Chen et al., 2025c; Lee et al., 2025).
Headquarters recognize that safety issues, near misses, or operational shortcuts taken under local managerial discretion are now more likely to be exposed through external scrutiny or public discourse.
This shift increases the visibility of establishment-level risks within the firm's internal control system (Hope et al., 2022).
Consequently, firms intensify monitoring efforts for establishments located in states with anti-SLAPP protections (Johnson, 2020; Gong et al., 2023).
Such efforts may include more frequent safety audits, additional visits from headquarters, and closer oversight of managers whose decisions directly affect workers' exposure to hazards (Cohn and Wardlaw, 2016; Heese and Pérez-Cavazos, 2020; Ege et al., 2023).
Enhanced monitoring from headquarters not only facilitates the detection of unsafe practices but also shapes managerial behavior by raising the expected likelihood of internal detection and discipline (Heese and Pérez-Cavazos, 2020).
In response, establishment managers adhere more closely to safety protocols, enforce training requirements more rigorously, and address hazardous conditions more promptly (Cohn et al., 2021; Hope et al., 2022).
In this way, a legal environment that protects external voice translates into stronger internal governance and more effective enforcement of safety standards.
<!-- para 32 -->
The second response is to reduce workloads and increase staffing.
This approach reflects firms' incentives to mitigate upstream risk factors associated with workplace injuries.
Extensive evidence links heavy workloads, including long shifts, overtime, high production targets, and staffing shortages, to higher rates of accidents and injuries (Nahrgang et al., 2011; Lee and Lee, 2016; Qian et al., 2023; Qian et al., 2024).
When workers can voice concerns about unsustainable workloads more effectively, or when managers anticipate that such concerns could be raised credibly under stronger free-speech protections, firms adjust operational practices to reduce these risks (Qian et al., 2023; Qian et al., 2024).
Anti-SLAPP laws, therefore, increase the expected scrutiny of operational choices that elevate injury risk.
In response, establishments in adopting states may moderate workloads, shorten shifts, or allocate additional staff to meet demand more safely.
These adjustments reduce fatigue-related errors, improve task manageability, and provide workers with greater capacity to comply with safety procedures (Nahrgang et al., 2011; Qian et al., 2024; Liao et al., 2025).
By addressing workload-related risks at their source, firms can effectively lower injury rates at the establishment level.
<!-- para 33 -->
Taken together, anti-SLAPP laws mitigate the chilling effects of strategic lawsuits that suppress speech and public participation.
By lowering the legal and financial costs of speaking out, they strengthen stakeholder oversight and enhance the credibility of complaints, disclosures, and public criticism.
As a result, establishments in states with newly enacted anti-SLAPP laws operate in a more accountable environment, where employees, journalists, local communities, and other stakeholders can more freely expose unsafe or harmful practices.
Anticipating that internal and external scrutiny is now more credible and harder to suppress, managers respond by strengthening oversight and alleviating operational pressures that contribute to workplace hazards.
Through enhanced headquarters monitoring and moderated workloads, these establishments are better positioned to address hazards proactively and prevent injuries.
In this way, free speech protections generate meaningful spillovers into corporate behavior, improving working conditions and workplace safety.

## methods
<!-- para 2 -->
This paper examines how state-level anti-SLAPP laws affect workplace safety, measured by establishment-level injury rates and counts.
These laws protect individuals from retaliatory lawsuits that might otherwise deter reporting or criticism of corporate misconduct.
The staggered adoption of anti-SLAPP laws across U.S. states provides a natural setting to assess how stronger legal protection for stakeholder voice influences workplace outcomes.
<!-- para 3 -->
A key empirical challenge is that establishment-level injury rates may reflect not only legal environments but also firm-specific characteristics and internal policies, such as safety investments, training practices, or broader risk-management systems (Cohn et al., 2021).
Because many firms operate across multiple states, simple comparisons between treated and untreated states risk conflating the effects of anti-SLAPP laws with unobserved firm-level factors that systematically influence safety.
To address this, we use a stacked DiD design that leverages both the staggered timing of anti-SLAPP adoption and the cross-state distribution of a firm's establishments.
This approach compares changes in injury rates across a firm's establishments in adopting (treated) versus non-adopting (control) states around each adoption event, effectively accounting for firm-level shocks, contemporaneous policy shifts, and other time-varying confounders.
<!-- para 5 -->
Our workplace injury data come from the Occupational Safety and Health Administration (OSHA).
Between 1996 and 2011, OSHA’s Data Initiative (ODI) program collected establishment-level information on work-related injuries and illnesses from roughly 80,000 private-sector establishments each year. [^1] An establishment corresponds to a distinct physical location, such as a factory, warehouse, or store.
For each establishment, OSHA records injury and illness data along with the establishment name, geographic location, Standard Industrial Classification (SIC) code, number of employees, hours worked, and indicators for unusual events such as shutdowns, strikes, or natural disasters.
<!-- para 6 -->
Our sample comprises establishments from the ODI that can be linked to firms in the Compustat annual file over 2002–2011.
We begin the sample in 2002 because OSHA implemented major changes to its injury and illness recording criteria and adjusted industry coverage that year, rendering earlier data non-comparable.
The ODI program ended in 2011 due to funding cuts.
We link OSHA establishments to Compustat firms using a multi-step name-matching procedure.
First, we apply a fuzzy-matching algorithm to identify, for each establishment, the Compustat firm name with the highest similarity score.
Second, we manually verify each tentative match by cross-checking establishment and firm identities, using company and establishment websites and other online sources, to confirm that they operate in the same industry.
This step mitigates mismatches that may arise from highly similar firm names.
Following Cohn and Wardlaw (2016) and Caskey and Ozel (2017), we exclude financial (SIC 6000–6999) and regulated (SIC 4900–4999) firms and establishments.
<!-- para 7 -->
A key advantage of the establishment-level ODI data is that it allows us to include firm-year fixed effects, thereby absorbing all time-varying confounders at the firm level that might otherwise bias our estimates.
Nonetheless, because injury incidence can vary systematically across establishments within the same firm, we include several establishment-level controls.
These variables include the establishment's average number of employees during the year (EstablishmentSize) and an indicator for seasonal operations (Seasonal), the latter capturing the higher injury rates typically associated with temporary workers (Caskey and Ozel, 2017).
To account for unusual events, we also include indicators for strikes or lockouts (Strike), shutdowns or layoffs (Shutdown), and natural disasters or adverse weather conditions affecting the establishment (NaturalDisaster) records in case they are selected.
Compliance is enforced through audits and fines for record-keeping violations (Caskey and Ozel, 2017).
<!-- para 9 -->
Our identification strategy exploits within-firm, cross-establishment variation in exposure to anti-SLAPP laws.
Because the empirical specification includes cohort–firm–year fixed effects, identification comes from comparing establishments of the same firm operating under different state legal environments in the same year.
Variation in treatment status across a firm's establishments therefore provides a clean setting to examine how external legal protections for employee speech translate into internal workplace safety outcomes.
<!-- para 10 -->
To operationalize this design, we implement a stacked DiD framework following Cengiz et al. (2019).
We first define adoption cohorts based on the year in which each state s first enforces an anti-SLAPP law.
Each distinct adoption year forms a separate cohort.
For each cohort, we construct a cohort-specific dataset that includes both treated and control observations.
Treated establishments are those located in states that adopt the law in year t, and they are considered treated from that year onward.
Control establishments are drawn from the same firms as the treated establishments but are located in states that have not yet adopted the law by year t, that is, states with adoption year greater than t or that never adopt the law.
This sampling rule ensures that comparisons are made strictly within firms, between treated and not-yet-treated establishments, thereby eliminating potentially confounding differences across unrelated firms.
<!-- para 11 -->
To ensure valid within-firm contrasts, we exclude firms that do not operate in at least one treated and one untreated state within a cohort window, as such firms offer no identifying variation.
We also drop establishments in states that adopted the law before year t, since they are already treated and cannot serve as valid controls.
We restrict the event window to  $(t-3, t+3)$, keeping observations from three years before to three years after adoption.
This symmetric window allows us to capture the dynamics of treatment effects around the adoption year while maintaining comparability across cohorts.
<!-- para 12 -->
We then stack all cohort-specific datasets into a single estimation sample.
The regression includes cohort-establishment fixed effects and cohort-firm-year fixed effects.
The cohort-establishment fixed effects absorb all time-invariant characteristics within each establishment and cohort, such as baseline injury risk, industry composition, and local labor conditions.
The cohort-firm-year fixed effects capture contemporaneous firm-level shocks, such as managerial turnover, policy changes, shifts in production intensity, or financial shocks, that may influence safety outcomes across a firm's establishments in a given year.
<!-- para 13 -->
This sample construction and fixed-effects structure ensure that the identifying variation arises solely from differences in the timing of anti-SLAPP adoption across states within the same firm and cohort.
By comparing treated and untreated establishments of the same firm operating under different state legal environments, we isolate the effect of stronger speech protections on workplace safety while mitigating concerns about unobserved heterogeneity and firm-level confounders.
<!-- para 14 -->
This empirical design offers several advantages that make it particularly well suited for studying the impact of anti-SLAPP laws on workplace safety.
First, the design aligns the institutional and organizational layers of analysis.
Anti-SLAPP laws operate at the state level, shaping the external legal environment in which employees can speak out on unsafe or unethical practices.
Workplace safety outcomes, however, are determined at the establishment level but influenced by firm-wide managerial and operational policies.
The within-firm, cross-establishment identification therefore captures the effect of a change in state-level legal protection on establishments' safety outcomes, holding constant all firm-level determinants of workplace injuries.
By matching state-level legal variation to within-firm differences across establishments, the design provides a credible basis for identifying how external legal environments affect firms' internal safety practices.
<!-- para 15 -->
Second, the inclusion of cohort-firm-year fixed effects provides a powerful control for unobservable confounders.
These fixed effects absorb any contemporaneous firm-wide changes, such as safety investments, policy shifts, production adjustments, or financial shocks, that might simultaneously affect injury rates across establishments.
Identification therefore relies exclusively on within-firm, cross-establishment variation in exposure to anti-SLAPP laws.
By conditioning on firm-year effects, the specification ensures that only differences in state legal environments drive variation in injury rates, thereby mitigating concerns about omitted-variable bias.
<!-- para 16 -->
Third, the stacked DiD structure addresses well-known limitations of traditional staggered adoption designs.
In two-way fixed effects (TWFE) DiD models, heterogeneous treatment effects across cohorts or over time can generate biased estimates that mix comparisons between early and late adopters (Goodman-Bacon, 2021; Roth et al., 2023; Sun and Abraham, 2021).
The stacked DiD framework overcomes these issues by constructing cohort-specific samples and absorbing cohort-interacted establishment and firm-year fixed effects, ensuring that identification relies solely on within-cohort comparisons relative to not-yet-treated establishments of the same firm.
This approach produces clean estimates of treatment effects that are robust to variation in timing and dynamic responses (Roth et al., 2023).
<!-- para 17 -->
Collectively, these features ensure that the stacked DiD design with cohort-interacted fixed effects offers both conceptual precision and econometric rigor.
It isolates the impact of enhanced speech protection on workplace safety while remaining robust to confounding shocks, heterogeneous treatment timing, and unobserved firm-level dynamics.
<!-- para 19 -->
We estimate the following DiD regression using the stacked cohort sample described above to assess the effect of anti-SLAPP statutes on workplace injuries:
<!-- para 20 -->
$$
Injuries_{i,j,t}=\alpha_{c,i}+\alpha_{c,j,t}+\beta\times Anti\text{-}SLAPP_{i,j,t}+Controls+\varepsilon_{c,i,j,t}
$$
<!-- para 21 -->
(1) where i, j, c, and t index establishments, firms, cohorts, and years, respectively. $\alpha_{c,i}$, and $\alpha_{c,j,t}$ denote high-dimensional cohort-establishment and cohort-firm-year fixed effects.
Anti-SLAPP_{i,j,t} is an indicator equal to one if establishment i in firm j is located in a state that has enacted an anti-SLAPP statute by year t, and zero otherwise.
We use two main measures of workplace injuries: Injuries/Hour, defined as the number of injuries divided by total hours worked, and Injuries, the total number of injuries.
Our results are also robust to alternative measures based on injuries that result in days away from work.
Following Cohn and Wardlaw (2016), we multiply Injuries/Hour by 1,000 to facilitate interpretation.
The vector Controls captures establishment-level determinants of workplace injury rate.

## results
<!-- para 4 -->
Panel B reports modest correlations among the key variables, alleviating concerns about multicollinearity.
Injuries/Hour is strongly correlated with DAFW/Hour, consistent with both capturing related aspects of workplace incidents.
By contrast, correlations among the establishment characteristics are small in magnitude.
<!-- para 7 -->
Panel A of Table 3 reports the estimated effect of anti-SLAPP statutes on workplace injuries.
Column 1 presents the results from a specification that includes only cohort- establishment and cohort-firm-year fixed effects.
The coefficient on Anti-SLAPP is significantly negative.
Column 2 adds controls for time-varying establishment characteristics, and the results remain quantitatively similar.
The coefficient estimate indicates that the enactment of anti-SLAPP statutes is associated with a 0.002-unit reduction in Injuries/Hour, corresponding to a notable 4.4% decrease relative to the sample mean Injuries/Hour of 0.045.
For perspective, this effect is comparable in magnitude to previously documented impacts of firms' investment capacity, measured by cash flow, cash balances, and financial leverage, or of cash-flow shocks on injury rates (Cohn and Wardlaw, 2016).
Importantly, the stability of the coefficients on Anti-SLAPP across specifications with and without establishment-level controls suggests that omitted establishment characteristics are unlikely to introduce substantial bias (Oster, 2019).
This observation is reassuring, especially given that our high-dimensional fixed effects already absorb potential confounders at the firm level.
<!-- para 8 -->
In columns 3 and 4, we address concerns about the distribution of the injury data by estimating count models that explicitly account for the nonnegative, discrete nature of injuries.
These regressions use the number of injuries as the dependent variable and include total hours worked as an exposure variable to account for the scale of baseline exposure.
We report estimates from a fixed effects Poisson model with cohort-establishment and cohort-firm-year fixed effects.
With or without controls for establishment characteristics, the coefficient on Anti-SLAPP is negative and statistically significant at the 1% level, consistent with the results in columns 1 and 2.
<!-- para 9 -->
Panel B of Table 3 evaluates whether the effects of anti-SLAPP statutes extend to the more severe forms of workplace harm—injuries that result in days away from work.
The dependent variables are DAFW/Hour, defined as the number of injuries resulting in days away from work divided by total hours worked, and DAFW Injuries, the total count of such injuries.
The estimated coefficients on Anti-SLAPP are negative in both specifications, consistent with the view that stronger speech protections reduce the incidence of severe injuries.
However, statistical significance arises only when the dependent variable is the count of DAFW injuries in column 2, where the coefficient is also economically meaningful.
This pattern suggests that anti-SLAPP laws meaningfully reduce the number of serious injury events, but the proportional decline relative to hours worked is more modest and estimated with less precision.
One interpretation is that severe injuries are relatively infrequent, so raw counts may better capture discrete improvements in safety performance in deterring more serious injuries.
<!-- para 10 -->
Workplace safety is likely to be more relevant in firms whose production relies heavily on physical assets than in more service-oriented firms.
Consistent with this view, Panel C reports estimates from the main specification using a sample restricted to establishments of firms with an above-median Tangible asset ratio.
This ratio is defined as net property, plant, and equipment divided by total book assets, based on data from Compustat.
The coefficient on Anti-SLAPP continues to be negative and statistically significant.
<!-- para 11 -->
Panel D presents estimates from the main specification for establishment-years with a positive number of injuries.
As in the full sample (Panel A), Anti-SLAPP is negatively and significantly associated with both the injury rate and the total injury count, with coefficient magnitudes that are quantitatively similar.
These results alleviate concerns that the large number of zeros in the injury data might bias the regression results.
<!-- para 13 -->
Our stacked DiD framework relies on the key assumption that, absent state anti-SLAPP statutes, treatment and control establishments would have followed parallel trends in workplace safety outcomes.
This assumption is particularly credible in our context because we compare establishments within the same firm, which share identical corporate governance, leadership, policies, and organizational culture.
To formally assess the parallel-trends assumption and to examine the temporal pattern of the statute’s impact, we estimate dynamic treatment effects.
Specifically, we replace the Anti-SLAPP indicator in our main specification with a set of year-specific dummies spanning three years before and three years after enactment (t - 3 to t - 2 and t to t + 3), using the year immediately prior to enactment (t - 1) as the benchmark year.
This specification enables us to test for pre-existing differential trends and to evaluate the timing and evolution of the statute’s effect on workplace injuries.
<!-- para 15 -->
In contrast, the post-treatment coefficients reveal a clear and economically meaningful decline in workplace injuries following the enactment of anti-SLAPP statutes.
Beginning in the first year after adoption  $(t+1)$, we observe a statistically significant reduction in both the injury rate and the injury count, and this improvement persists through  $t+2$.
By  $t+3$, the coefficients remain negative but are no longer statistically significant, indicating that the strongest improvements occur in the early post-adoption period.
This pattern is consistent with establishments implementing safety-enhancing adjustments, such as reduced workloads and enhanced routine monitoring from headquarters, shortly after enactment.
Once these changes are in place, the resulting safety gains appear to stabilize, suggesting that these front-loaded responses account for the bulk of the observed improvements.
<!-- para 18 -->
To capture statutory strength, we construct an Anti-SLAPP Intensity variable based on the Institute for Free Speech (IFS) grading system. [^2] The IFS assigns each state’s anti-SLAPP statute a grade from A (strongest protection) to D (weakest protection), reflecting both the breadth of protected activities and the extent of procedural safeguards available to defendants in SLAPP cases.
We transform this ordinal scale into a continuous metric by taking the natural logarithm of one plus the numeric score associated with each grade, assigning states without an anti-SLAPP law a value of zero.
For example, states with an A grade receive a score of 4, those with a B receive 3, and so forth.
<!-- para 19 -->
Across both dependent variables, injury rate and injury count, the estimated coefficients on Anti-SLAPP Intensity are negative and statistically significant at the 5% level or better.
These findings indicate that workplace injuries decline more sharply in states with stronger anti-SLAPP provisions, consistent with the idea that robust speech protections enhance stakeholders' ability to report unsafe conditions, thereby prompting firms to adopt corrective measures and strengthen workplace safety practices.
<!-- para 21 -->
In this section, we extend our analysis by examining cross-sectional heterogeneity in the effects of anti-SLAPP statutes.
Exploring this heterogeneity clarifies the conditions under which speech-protection statutes are most effective in improving workplace safety and deepens our understanding of how these statutes affect firms' internal safety practices.
<!-- para 22 -->
The underlying premise is that anti-SLAPP statutes increase the likelihood that negative information about workplace conditions reaches external audiences by making it more difficult for firms to suppress or intimidate those who disclose such information.
When establishments anticipate more severe economic or reputational consequences from the exposure of unsafe conditions, the enactment of an anti-SLAPP statute should trigger a stronger safety response.
Guided by this reasoning, we focus on two sources of cross-sectional heterogeneity that reflect variation in the potential consequences of such exposure: workforce retention pressure and local reputational pressure.
<!-- para 24 -->
The first source of heterogeneity arises from differences in workforce retention pressures across establishments.
In environments where retaining employees is more challenging, workplace injuries can have particularly severe consequences (Dai et al., 2022).
Even a single safety incident may lead to employee dissatisfaction, higher turnover, or labor disputes, thereby increasing both operational and reputational costs.
As a result, establishments facing greater retention pressures have stronger incentives to enhance workplace safety.
We therefore predict that the reduction in workplace injuries following the adoption of anti-SLAPP laws is more pronounced in establishments with higher workforce retention pressures.
<!-- para 25 -->
We use two proxies to capture workforce retention pressure: labor mobility and rival concentration.
Higher labor mobility indicates that employees can more easily leave for alternative opportunities, increasing retention challenges.
Similarly, greater rival concentration reflects intensified competition for talent in the local labor market, which can also heighten workforce retention pressures.
<!-- para 26 -->
To measure labor mobility, we follow Donangelo (2014) and calculate the average occupation dispersion of employed workers within an industry. [^3] Workers in occupations concentrated in a few industries are associated with low labor mobility, while those in occupations dispersed across the economy are associated with high labor mobility.
We classify establishments into high- and low-mobility groups using a dummy variable, High labor mobility, which equals one if an establishment's industry-specific labor mobility is above the sample median and zero otherwise.
Our second measure, High rival concentration, is a dummy variable equal one if an establishment's Rival concentration is above the sample median and zero otherwise, where Rival concentration is the total number of establishments operating in the same state and belonging to the same three-digit SIC industry as the focal establishment in a given year.
<!-- para 27 -->
$$
CONC_{j,t}=\left(\frac{emp_{i,j,t}}{\sum_{i}emp_{i,j,t}}\right)^{2},
$$
<!-- para 28 -->
where $emp_{i,j,t}$ is the number of workers assigned to occupation j who are employed in industry i at time t.
In the second stage, the aggregate occupation-level concentration measure $CONC$ is computed by industry and weighted by the wage expense associated with each occupation:
<!-- para 29 -->
$$
LM_{i,t}=\left(\sum_{j}CONC_{j,t}\times\frac{emp_{i,j,t}\times wage_{i,j,t}}{\sum_{j}emp_{i,j,t}\,wage_{i,j,t}}\right)^{-1},
$$
<!-- para 30 -->
where $wage_{i,j,t}$ is a measure of the average annual wage paid to workers in industry i that are assigned to occupation j in year t.
Finally, the estimated LM in the above equation is standardized.
<!-- para 33 -->
The second source of heterogeneity concerns the potential reputational consequences establishments face within their local communities when unsafe conditions become public.
Even with identical corporate policies, establishments embedded in different communities may be subject to varying degrees of stakeholder scrutiny.
Communities characterised by strong social networks, active civic engagement, or progressive political preferences tend to be less tolerant of corporate behaviour perceived as irresponsible, including unsafe working environments (Hasan et al., 2017; Hoi et al., 2018; Hoi et al., 2019).
We therefore expect that establishments located in areas with stronger reputational pressure respond more strongly to anti-SLAPP statutes and have greater incentives to improve safety, ultimately resulting in larger reductions in workplace injuries.
<!-- para 34 -->
We test this conjecture in Table 7, using two measures to capture local reputational pressure.
Our first measure exploits the idea that political preferences reflect underlying attitudes toward stakeholder responsibility (Alesina and Giuliano, 2011; Luttmer and Singhal, 2011).
In the U.S., the Democratic Party aligns more closely than the Republican Party with stakeholder-oriented views, emphasizing corporate responsibilities beyond profit maximization, including responsibilities toward workers, communities, and the environment (Di Giuli and Kostovetsky, 2014).
Accordingly, we use the level of support for the Democratic Party in a county as a proxy for local stakeholder scrutiny.
High Democratic is a dummy variable that equals one if % Democratic is above the sample median and zero otherwise, where % Democratic is the percentage of votes the Democratic Party received in the most recent Presidential Election in the county where the establishment is located. [^4]
<!-- para 35 -->
Our second measure is based on local social capital.
Irresponsible corporate behavior and unsafe workplace practices are more likely to attract attention and provoke reputational costs in communities with high social capital, given their dense social networks, strong societal norms, and collective expectations of corporate responsibility (Hoi et al., 2019).
Following Hasan et al. (2017) and Hoi et al. (2019), we construct Social capital using the first principal component from a factor analysis based on four factors that capture societal norms and social networks in US counties: (i) Pvote, which is voter turnouts in presidential elections; (ii) Respn, response rates in US census surveys; (iii) Nccs, total numbers of nonprofit organizations; and (iv) Assn, total number of ten types of social organizations. [^5] We then define High social capital as a dummy variable that equals one if the county’s Social capital value is above the sample median, and zero otherwise.
<!-- para 37 -->
Overall, the cross-sectional evidence further reinforces the credibility of our within-firm, cross-establishment empirical strategy.
For an unobserved (non-firm-specific) factor to account for our main findings, it would need to satisfy an exceptionally high bar: it would have to vary precisely with the staggered timing of anti-SLAPP statute adoptions, align with the variation in statutory strength across states, and simultaneously produce the patterns of heterogeneity we document in workforce retention and local reputational pressures.
This combination of requirements substantially reduces the plausibility of omitted variable explanations.
In other words, the consistency of the cross-sectional heterogeneity results with our theoretical predictions provides additional reassurance that the observed reductions in workplace injuries are indeed driven by the enactment of anti-SLAPP statutes rather than by unobserved confounding factors.
<!-- para 39 -->
In this section, we examine the mechanisms through which establishments affected by anti-SLAPP statutes improve workplace safety.
Although the introduction of these statutes may trigger centralized, firm-wide responses, such as broad safety campaigns, the rollout of new compliance systems, or revisions to corporate safety initiatives, these adjustments are absorbed by the firm-year fixed effects in our within-firm, cross-establishment empirical design.
Because these fixed effects capture all common, firm-level changes, any uniformly implemented corporate initiatives are differenced out by construction.
Consequently, our estimates isolate variation in how individual establishments respond to changes in the external legal environment, independent of any firm-wide shifts.
This feature naturally directs our attention toward mechanisms that operate at the establishment level.
In particular, we highlight two such mechanisms that plausibly drive the observed improvements in workplace safety: strengthened monitoring from headquarters and reductions in workload pressure.
<!-- para 41 -->
The first mechanism concerns the idea that anti-SLAPP statutes strengthen the incentives for headquarters to monitor establishments located in adopting states.
By reducing the ability of local managers to conceal unsafe practices and increasing the credibility of both internal and external scrutiny, these laws heighten the likelihood that safety lapses, near misses, or hazardous managerial practices become public.
Anticipating this greater exposure risk, headquarters are more motivated to detect and correct unsafe practices before they attract external attention.
<!-- para 42 -->
Direct measures of monitoring intensity, such as audit frequency or supervisory visits, are not observable in our data.
We therefore use geographical proximity to headquarters as an indirect but conceptually grounded proxy, since monitoring is more feasible and less costly for establishments located nearby.
If anti-SLAPP laws prompt firms to strengthen oversight, this intensified monitoring should be more evident where such oversight is easier to implement.
<!-- para 43 -->
To capture the ease of monitoring, we construct two variables.
The first, Same state, is a dummy variable equal to one if the establishment and its headquarter are located in the same U.S. state.
The second, Ln(1+Distance) , is the natural logarithm of one plus the geographic distance between the establishment and its headquarters, where Distance is measured as the great-circle distance (in miles) calculated from latitude-longitude coordinates using the Vincenty (1975) formula.
Proximity to headquarters reduces the marginal cost of oversight and increases the likelihood that monitoring activities, whether formal audits or informal visits, occur more frequently.
<!-- para 44 -->
If anti-SLAPP statutes strengthen incentives to scrutinize establishment-level operations, their injury-reducing effect should be more pronounced where monitoring is relatively easy or inexpensive.
Consistent with this prediction, Table 8 shows that the decline in workplace injuries following anti-SLAPP adoption is significantly larger for establishments located in the same state as their headquarters and for those geographically closer to headquarters.
These patterns reinforce the premise that enhanced internal oversight is one mechanism through which anti-SLAPP laws translate external speech protections into safer workplace conditions.
<!-- para 46 -->
The second mechanism focuses on firms’ efforts to mitigate operational pressures that elevate injury risk.
Prior research links demanding work conditions, including long hours, overtime, aggressive production targets, and insufficient staffing, to higher accident and injury rates (Nahrgang et al., 2011; Lee and Lee, 2016; Qian et al., 2023; Qian et al., 2024).
Stronger speech protections heighten the credibility of employee complaints about unsustainable work demands and increase the likelihood that external stakeholders uncover harmful practices.
Anticipating these risks, establishments in adopting states have stronger incentives to reduce workload intensity or expand staffing to create safer operating conditions.
<!-- para 47 -->
To examine whether anti-SLAPP statutes alter workload dynamics, Table 9 analyzes two outcomes.
Ln(Hours/Employee) measures the average number of hours worked per employee at the establishment.
Employment increase is an indicator variable equal to one if the establishment expands its workforce relative to the prior year.
The results support the workload mechanism.
Establishments in states adopting anti-SLAPP laws exhibit a statistically significant decline in hours worked per employee and are more likely to increase staffing.
These findings indicate that firms operating under stronger speech protections respond by easing operational pressures, reducing fatigue-related risks, enhancing task manageability, and enabling more consistent adherence to safety protocols.
<!-- para 48 -->
Taken together, the analyses in this section provide evidence on how establishment-level responses translate the external consequences of anti-SLAPP laws into improved workplace safety.
Establishments closer to headquarters, where strengthening monitoring is more feasible and less costly, experience larger reductions in injuries, consistent with enhanced oversight.
At the same time, establishments in adopting states reduce working hours and expand staffing, indicating deliberate efforts to mitigate workload-related risks.
Because firm-year fixed effects absorb centralized changes at the corporate level, these results reflect heterogeneous adjustments across establishments within the same firm.
Overall, these additional analyses deepen our understanding of how free-speech protections shape corporate behavior and contribute to safer working environments.

## discussion
<!-- para 2 -->
This paper demonstrates that legal protections for public participation, specifically state-level anti-SLAPP statutes, have tangible operational consequences for firms by improving workplace safety.
Using a within-firm, cross-establishment research design that leverages the staggered adoption of anti-SLAPP laws across U.S. states, we show that establishments in adopting states experience significantly fewer workplace injuries relative to establishments of the same firm in non-adopting states.
The effects are concentrated in the years immediately following adoption, are stronger where statutes are more robust, and are amplified in contexts where unsafe conditions are more likely to be exposed, such as in industries with higher labor mobility or communities with strong reputational pressures.
<!-- para 3 -->
Our analyses suggest two mechanisms driving these safety improvements.
First, enhanced speech protections increase headquarters' incentives to monitor establishments closely, particularly when monitoring is less costly and more feasible.
Second, anti-SLAPP laws prompt establishments to alleviate heavy workloads, thereby reducing fatigue-related hazards and improving compliance with safety protocols.
Collectively, these mechanisms illustrate how a legal environment that safeguards stakeholder voice can shape managerial behavior, resource allocation, and operational practices in ways that directly benefit employees.
<!-- para 4 -->
Beyond documenting a novel determinant of workplace safety, our findings carry broader implications for both research and policy.
They highlight the importance of considering the legal and institutional context in which firms operate, showing that regulatory protections for stakeholder voice can complement internal governance mechanisms and market incentives in shaping firm outcomes.
For policymakers, the results suggest that enhancing protections for free speech and public participation is not only a matter of civil liberties but also a practical lever for promoting safer and more accountable organizational behavior.
For scholars, our study underscores the need to integrate external institutional and legal factors into analyses of corporate governance, operational risk, and stakeholder engagement.
<!-- para 5 -->
In sum, workplace safety reflects not only internal governance and managerial incentives but also the broader legal environment that enables stakeholders to observe, report, and influence firm practices.
By linking anti-SLAPP protections to reductions in workplace injuries, this paper demonstrates that safeguarding public participation strengthens accountability and operational outcomes, offering a new perspective on the intersection of law, governance, and stakeholder oversight in promoting safer workplaces.
