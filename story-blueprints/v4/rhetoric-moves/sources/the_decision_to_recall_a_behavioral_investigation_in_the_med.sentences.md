# the_decision_to_recall_a_behavioral_investigation_in_the_med 句子库存

## introduction
<!-- para 2 -->
The question of whether to recall a product is one of the most important, yet complex, decisions an operations manager may face in their career.
The criteria for recalling a product is highly contextual, multi-dimensional, and necessitates balancing the firm's profitability and reputation with customer satisfaction, customer safety, and regulatory oversight.
This is particularly salient in the medical device industry.
The Food and Drug Administration (FDA), the federal regulator responsible for medical device recalls in the U.S., recently reported that after controlling for market growth, the number of medical devices recalled between 2002 and 2012 increased nearly 50%, with the majority of these recalls having customer safety implications.[<sup>1</sup>](#fn1_1 "Link to note") Surprisingly, the FDA does not clearly specify how a manager should integrate the multiple, and potentially conflicting, criteria influencing whether or not to recall a product.
Consequently, managers frequently use individual judgment in arriving at recall decisions.
<!-- para 3 -->
Examples of recently publicized product recall incidents suggest that managerial judgment related to recalls may benefit from rigorous academic study.
On the one hand, some products are not recalled when perhaps they should be.
For instance, in 2010, Johnson & Johnson was sanctioned by the Food and Drug Administration (FDA) for neglecting to issue a product recall in a timely manner after receiving numerous customer complaints related to multiple faulty products.[<sup>2</sup>](#fn2_2 "Link to note") Conversely, there is also evidence of products being recalled perhaps unnecessarily.
For example, between 2008 and 2010, Toyota recalled almost ten million vehicles due to alleged unintended acceleration or “sticky gas pedals.” Initially, Toyota denied that their cars had a quality problem, but later succumbed to intense pressure from customers, regulators, and the media, and recalled all potentially faulty units.[<sup>3</sup>](#fn3_3 "Link to note") Eventually, after numerous investigations, including those completed by the National Aeronautics and Space Administration (NASA), it was determined that the vehicles were not defective and the recalls were likely unwarranted.[<sup>4</sup>](#fn4_4 "Link to note")
<!-- para 4 -->
These cases underscore the challenges managers face in assessing the need for a recall.
While a number of studies have examined the market and associated financial impacts of a recall ([Chen et al., 2009](#bib15); [Cheah et al., 2007](#bib13); [Jarrell and Peltzman, 1985](#bib29); [Bromiley and Marcus, 1989](#bib11)), less is known about how managers actually decide to recall a product.
In fact, there are no behavioral studies, to our knowledge, that have examined factors influencing judgment in the recall decision.
Our study takes a first step in addressing this gap by identifying a set of factors, informed by our industry and regulator interactions, and examining their influence on the decision of whether to recall a product.
The study also provides insight into how these factors influence the recall decision by testing possible mechanisms.
The results provide actionable guidance for regulators that oversee these decisions and for managers that make them in practice.
<!-- para 5 -->
We chose the medical device industry as the context for our study because the decision to recall a medical device is made voluntarily by firm managers, compared to both managers and regulators as in other recall intensive industries such as the automotive industry.[<sup>5</sup>](#fn5_9 "Link to note") Also, medical device recalls occur frequently and have serious implications for both the physicians who buy the products and the patients who use the products, two important yet distinct customers.
At the same time, the recall criteria outlined by the FDA are vague.
Specifically, FDA recall policy states that companies must recall products that “ *present a risk of injury or gross deception or are otherwise defective*.” [<sup>6</sup>](#fn6_10 "Link to note") How managers apply this guidance to a specific situation is open to interpretation.
The inherent subjectivity in this decision presents an ideal context to identify factors that influence the likelihood to recall and examine the nature of the influence.
<!-- para 6 -->
Given our intent to study unexplored relationships in an understudied industry context, we first developed an initial set of potential factors through interviews with FDA regulators and medical industry professionals most familiar with the recall decision, and then refined this list to arrive at three testable factors that form the focus on this study.
These factors are grounded in three aspects of the underlying product defect that may necessitate a recall, including physician customer opinion of the product defect, physician customer ability to detect the product defect, and managerial understanding of the root cause of the product defect.
More specifically, the three factors are: (a) *physician concern*: whether a product defect concern is raised by a single, influential, physician customer, b) *defect detectability*: whether a product defect, if it exists, is observable by physician customers before use, and c) *root cause understanding*: the manager's level of understanding of the root cause of the product defect.
<!-- para 7 -->
We conducted two main studies to examine whether physician concern, defect detectability, and root cause understanding influence the decision to recall, and to explore potential mechanisms that may explain the nature of the relationships we uncover.
Study 1 focuses on testing the factor effects.
Because the recall decision is highly contextual in nature, it is plausible that experienced professionals may exhibit different judgmental tendencies than the general population.
For this reason, we curated a unique subject pool of managers who have experience making actual product recall decisions at one of the world's largest medical device firms (167 manager subjects).
For comparison, we also draw from a general population of individuals using Amazon Mechanical Turk (614 additional subjects).
The experimental results are similar for both subject pools.
We find that physician concern does not influence the likelihood to recall, contrary to research related to salience bias ([Tiefenbeck et al., 2016](#bib57)).
However, both defect detectability and root cause understanding do influence the likelihood to recall.
More specifically, defect detectability decreases the likelihood to recall, while root cause understanding increases it.
<!-- para 8 -->
Study 2 examines potential mechanisms that may explain the influence of the defect detectability and root cause understanding factors.
In our theorizing, we posit that defect detectability may influence the recall decision through “perceived harm to the patient customer” or through “perceived recall cost to the firm”.
In terms of root cause understanding, we argued that it may influence recall likelihood through “perceived attribution of the defect” or through “perceived recall cost to the firm”.
We use Monte Carlo simulation to test these possible mediation effects, following [Rungtusanatham et al. (2014)](#bib51), [Preacher and Selig (2012)](#bib50), and [MacKinnon et al. (2004)](#bib36).
Our results demonstrate support for mediation.
A defect that is detectable to the physician customer leads to a perception of low recall costs and low patient harm.
Similarly, high root cause understanding leads to a perception of low recall costs.
Further, both perceived costs and perceived harm influence the recall likelihood.
High perceived costs lead to a low recall likelihood, while high patient harm leads to a high recall likelihood.
In the case of defect detectability, where both mechanisms are at play, the results of the mediation analysis suggests that while managers appear to hesitate to recall when perceived costs are high, the concern for the safety of the patient customer is more dominant than cost.
In the case of root cause, the analysis suggests that one reason why managers are more likely to recall when root cause is understood is due to low perceived costs to the firm.
<!-- para 9 -->
Finally, we present results of a post hoc analysis on the one subject-specific attribute that was consistently significant across both studies.
Specifically, results show that a subject's score on the cognitive reflection test (CRT) is a significant and negative predictor of the likelihood to recall.
Developed by [Frederick (2005)](#bib20), the CRT has become a well-used means by which behavioral researchers have examined an individual's propensity to make decisions via instinct or reflection.
We find that subjects who exhibit high levels of cognitive reflection are less likely to recall.
We also find that the relationship between our factors and likelihood to recall differ statistically between those with high versus low cognitive reflection.
More specifically, our results show that defect detectability and root cause understanding are significant predictors of a recall for subjects with high cognitive reflection, but are not significant for those with low cognitive reflection.
In aggregate, this research provides a first-look into what may influence managers' decision to recall, leading to insightful implications for medical device industry managers and FDA regulators.

## theory
<!-- para 3 -->
Because the decision to recall has not been studied previously from a behavioral perspective, there are numerous possible contributing factors to consider.
To ensure that we focus on factors that are important and relevant to industry stakeholders, we spoke with several senior FDA regulators and senior managers of two U.S. Fortune 500 medical device manufacturing firms.
We annotate the two firms as focal and secondary.
While both firms participated in these discussions, only the focal firm participated in the subsequent experiment.
The purpose of these unstructured discussions was to gather different perspectives on how the medical device product recall decision-making process is regulated by the FDA and how it plays out within firms.
These discussions provided an overview of the context in which the recall decision is made, the process used to make the decision, and the roles of the main participants.
Industry discussants held executive level positions within one of two medical device manufacturing firms and had significant experience in making recall decisions, including a global vice-president of quality reporting directly to the chief executive officer of the focal firm.
Our interviews at the FDA included the Director of the Center for Device and Radiological Health (CDRH), who leads the FDA division overseeing medical devices, and his senior staff.
This approach allowed us to juxtapose the intended recall policies from a regulatory perspective with how they are interpreted and implemented in the field from a managerial perspective.
<!-- para 4 -->
From these discussions, we learned that manufacturers design the device, gain FDA approval, manufacture the products, and sell to physicians who traditionally choose the products for patients.
Both the physician and the patient are important but distinct customers in this supply chain.
The end customer, who actually uses the product, is referred to internally in a medical device company as the “patient customer” to emphasize the importance of patients, who are the ultimate recipients of valuable life-saving or life-enhancing technology.
The “physician customer” plays a different, yet important, role in the supply chain.
The physician customer makes the purchasing decision, observes product performance, and provides frequent feedback to the company.
Once a device is placed in the field, quality signals are continually monitored and consolidated by quality assurance managers to provide a comprehensive view of product performance.
Complaints from a physician or patient customer are the most common sources of product quality feedback that may lead to a recall.
For example, physicians may report a complaint if they detect a defect either prior to or during use of the device, or when a patient suffers a negative health episode due to a possible device malfunction.
Physicians can be quite forceful in their complaints, and may sometimes include a recommendation that the firm recall the product.
When these signals indicate a potentially pervasive product quality problem, a quality assurance manager at the firm may initiate a product recall meeting.
<!-- para 5 -->
Product recall meetings bring together key functional area representatives (e.g., quality, manufacturing, clinical, medical) to review performance data, including the product's failure rate, the root cause of the failure if known, the length of time the product has been on the market, and the possible impact of product failures on the health and safety of the patient customer.
The outcome of a typical recall meeting is a list of recommendations by individuals from different functional areas.
Individuals can recommend no recall, which by default equates to continued monitoring, or recall.
These recommendations are then forwarded to the executive leader responsible for making recall decisions for the firm, and he or she uses these recommendations as inputs in making a final decision.
A decision to recall triggers an immediate cessation of product shipments, a notification to all customers, a formal communication to the FDA, and a detailed plan to retrieve or repair affected products.
Tables A1 and A2 in [Appendix A](#app1) provide further details on these discussions, the participants, and how this helped inform our factors of interest.
<!-- para 7 -->
We now describe in more detail the three experimental factors used in this research and the theory supporting our main hypotheses.
<!-- para 9 -->
*Physician Concern* reflects the scenario where an influential physician customer voices a quality concern about a defective product and inquires how the problem will be addressed.
During our discussions with firm managers, we learned that an influential physician, described to us as a physician who is an advocate for the firm and often serves on one of the firm's physician advisory boards,[<sup>7</sup>](#fn7_17 "Link to note") is important and someone to be kept satisfied.
Such physicians can have significant financial impact on the company's revenue through purchasing decisions made on behalf of their patients and they are often influencers who affect the purchase habits of other physician customers.
<!-- para 10 -->
The tendency of managers to respond to the most salient customer feedback has been studied by previous researchers, some of whom refer to this as a “squeaky wheel” bias ([Bendoly et al., 2010](#bib8); [Ma et al., 2015](#bib35); [Mitra and Golder, 2006](#bib38)).
For example, [Ma et al. (2015)](#bib35) examine the effect of customer compliments and complaints on firm decisions using a large panel dataset of Twitter comments.
They analyze thousands of individual customer compliments and complaints and find that firms take significant actions as a direct response to individual outspoken customers.
This tendency to respond to one vocal customer may also be attributable to salience bias, which occurs when decision-makers respond to the most noticeable feedback, instead of aggregating all relevant feedback and taking a holistic view of the data ([Tiefenbeck et al., 2016](#bib57)).
For example, one's perceived probability of a traffic accident is immediately raised after one observes a traffic accident ([Tversky and Kahneman, 1974](#bib61)).
Salience bias has also been observed in marketing studies that demonstrate that the prominence or salience of a brand in a consumer's memory is significantly related to a consumer's product choice ([Hutchinson, 1983](#bib27); [Alba and Chattopadhyay, 1986](#bib2); [Hauser and Wernerfelt, 1990](#bib25)).
In our context, it follows that a manager's perception of the need for a recall may increase after receiving a complaint from one outspoken and influential customer.
<!-- para 11 -->
One example from our interviews illustrates this relationship.
A physician demanded a conference call with the manufacturing manager to ask how a plastic coating defect on a catheter could have occurred.
In the course of the conference call, which included the manufacturing manager, manufacturing vice-president and the quality director, the upset physician demonstrated dissatisfaction with the defect and implied that if he observed further similar defects, he would elevate his concerns directly to the FDA.
In the case of this specific product, even though there had been no other reported complaints and the firm was unsure if the problem had occurred broadly or was isolated to this one catheter, the negative opinion of this important physician led to the decision to recall.
It is noteworthy that the FDA does not specify individual, or even aggregate, physician customer concern as recall criteria.
Nevertheless, anecdotal evidence and theory both suggest it may have a significant effect.
We therefore hypothesize:
<!-- para 12 -->
Hypothesis 1 1.Physician concern related to a product defect results in a higher likelihood to recall.
<!-- para 14 -->
Our second factor, *Defect Detectability*, refers to a physician customer's ability to detect a defect prior to using the product.
Unlike the *Physician Concern* factor, this factor does not examine how the physician responds to the firm after observing a defect, but instead is related to technical aspects of the defect itself.
Physician customers serve an important intermediary role between the manufacturer and patient customer in a medical device supply chain.
This intermediary role may include customizing the device to meet the patient's unique specifications, performing a procedure to place the device within the patient, and providing ongoing instruction and support to the patient once the device is activated.
Through this intermediate handling of the product, the physician customer also has an opportunity to detect a product defect prior to use and, by detecting the defect, potentially eliminate the risk of harm to the patient customer.
Certain defects, such as a cracked screen on an electronic medical device, would be highly detectable to a physician customer and, as a result, could be circumvented to avoid harm to the patient.
Other defects, such as a software coding error, would be difficult to detect and thus more likely to impact the patient.
<!-- para 15 -->
We contend that managers may be less likely to recall if a defect is more detectable to the physician customer pre-use.
In such a case, managers may view the physician as someone they trust to perform a final quality control inspection of the product, reducing the chance that a potential defect will go unnoticed and end up harming the patient.
This perspective is consistent with literature demonstrating that a final quality inspection step, historically viewed as non-value added in quality and operations management literature ([Yin et al., 2017](#bib68); [Schmenner and Swink, 1998](#bib53)), can result in lower levels of final product quality ([Deming, 2000](#bib17)) because individuals responsible for creating the defect may expect someone else to catch their mistakes ([Ebrahimpour, 1985](#bib18)).
Applying this logic to our context, managers may perceive a reduced level of harm to the patient when the defect is detectable, because the physician will catch their mistakes.
<!-- para 16 -->
While it may be surprising that firms could view physicians in this manner, and consequently allow defective products to be released to the market, such scenarios were revealed during our discussions with managers and FDA employees.
For example, in one recall scenario described to us, several batches of a product had not been sealed properly in the packaging process and were therefore not likely to be sterile or clean when shipped to the customer.
However, one of the first steps for a physician using the product was to personally tear open the sterile seal of the product.
While there was little doubt that a small number of devices built over a short period of time were shipped with a defective seal, the decision was made to not recall because managers expected that physician customers would observe the defect and not use the unsealed product.
<!-- para 17 -->
It is also possible that managers may view such a recall scenario in a purely cost/benefit perspective, rather than from the perspective of trusting the physician to catch the defect and lower patient customer harm.
The manager may simply perceive a reduced cost to the firm when the physician can be used to screen out defects.
Research supports this perspective by demonstrating that managers tend to prioritize cost over other less tangible measures of performance ([Nandakumar et al., 1993](#bib42)) and that, when possible, managers seek to find ways to push quality costs as far down the supply chain as possible ([Zhu et al., 2007](#bib69)).
Whether defect detectability influences the perceived harm that may come to the patient, or the perceived cost to the firm, it is possible that the more detectable the defect is to the physician customer, the less likely managers are to recall.
Accordingly, we hypothesize:
<!-- para 18 -->
Hypothesis 2 1.A detectable product defect results in a lower likelihood to recall.
<!-- para 20 -->
*Root Cause Understanding* is the third factor, and it reflects the level of understanding a manager has about the root cause of the potential defect.
Having a clear understanding of the cause of a defect can be difficult and time intensive, particularly for complex products such as medical devices, but is also quite relevant in the recall decision, as we learned in our discussions.
<!-- para 21 -->
Understanding why something occurs is the “basic spring of action” in human life ([Weiner et al., 1979](#bib65)).
Attribution theory ([Weiner et al., 1979](#bib65); [Weiner, 1985](#bib64)) informs us that the desire to understand the cause of an event is inherent among individuals.
The importance of attributing a cause to an event has been demonstrated in multiple settings including third-party logistics failures ([Oflac et al., 2012](#bib45)), customer satisfaction related to service failures ([Anderson et al., 2004](#bib5)), and new product designs ([Schreier, 2006](#bib54)).
Uncovering true root cause is a critical step in understanding and resolving a product defect.
In the absence of a definable cause, product recalls can be challenging, though still necessary.
<!-- para 22 -->
One applicable example from our discussions relates to an electronic cardiac device that was shorting out and experiencing premature battery depletion.
A certain number of devices were failing in the field, but the engineering team could not explain the cause of the failures.
Data analysis showed a sizable increase in field failures had occurred after a precise date, but a recall had not been initiated because some managers were hesitant to open a broad-based recall that affected so many units without a better understanding of the underlying root cause.
When engineering analysis pointed to a process change at an integrated circuit manufacturing step as the source of the failure, managers agreed to an immediate recall.
The decision to recall was influenced by the fact that now the recall was narrowed in scope, limited to high-risk units, and attributed to a specific process change at a particular piece of equipment.
However, the lag between “when failures first surfaced” and “when the root cause was identified” exposed additional patient customers to a higher risk and negatively affected some patient customers' health.
This example highlights the important, but controversial, role of root cause understanding in the recall decision.
<!-- para 23 -->
Once again, costs may be an alternative, underlying mechanism at work in this factor because a higher level of root cause understanding may create clarity regarding the cost of the potential recall.
This increased clarity may result from the ability to more precisely identify affected units in a recall, creating a tighter bound on the problem.
In other words, the absence of an assignable root cause to the defective units may cause the recall to be broader in scope and lead to more units requiring return, repair and replacement (hence, higher costs to the firm).
The presence of root cause understanding, we learned through our interviews, can lead to a clearer and generally more restricted set of units requiring disposition.
Relatedly, prior research has shown that individuals often exhibit ambiguity aversion, meaning they prefer known risks over unknown risks ([Fox and Tversky, 1995](#bib22)).
This aversion can cause individuals to perceive costs with an ambiguous distribution as higher than costs with a known distribution.
This higher evaluation is referred to as the ambiguity premium ([Trautmann et al., 2008](#bib59)).
In the context of a product recall decision, high root cause understanding limits the range that the cost of the recall will take.
This may lead to a positive relationship between root cause understanding and the likelihood to recall because of a reduced cost perception.
In sum, whether it is simply due to greater attribution or due to a reduced cost perception, we believe that the more information known about the root cause of the potential defect, the greater the likelihood of a recall decision.
We therefore hypothesize:
<!-- para 24 -->
Hypothesis 3 1.Root cause understanding of a product defect results in a higher likelihood to recall.
<!-- para 27 -->
To test our three hypotheses, we utilize a vignette-based experiment that uses situational descriptions to elicit individual recall decisions.
Vignettes in behavioral experiments of this sort provide at least three important benefits.
First, vignettes may reveal subconscious biases in decision-making of which the subject may not be directly aware.
Second, they allow for randomized variation of scenario characteristics, allowing the researcher to accurately test effects of changes in each variable independent of others ([Alexander and Becker, 1978](#bib3)).
Finally, vignettes allow researchers to study questions that have *serious consequences in safe settings*.
For example, a field experiment in which the factors surrounding real recall decisions were manipulated would likely be unethical, particularly if there was the potential for increased patient harm.
Recent vignette studies within behavioral operations include experiments exploring professional services ([Brandon-Jones et al., 2016](#bib10)) and the influence of power and expertise in supplier-buyer relationships ([Chen et al., 2016](#bib14)).
<!-- para 28 -->
The vignettes in our experiment included a product failure description for a cardiac medical device that was common to all treatments.
This description was followed by text representing one of eight treatment scenarios.
Each treatment scenario consisted of either high or low levels for each of the three situational factors (i.e., 2 <sup>3</sup>  = 8).
The intent of the high and low levels of the three factors was to provide an accurate representation of the concepts in the hypotheses (see [Appendix B](#app2) for the constant product failure scenario and all experimental text used).
A cardiac medical device was chosen because such devices are critical to patient health, technologically complex, and make up half of worldwide medical device sales.[<sup>8</sup>](#fn8_45 "Link to note") To construct the wording of the vignettes, we worked with leadership at a third medical device firm used exclusively to beta test the vignettes (see Table A.1 in [Appendix A](#app1) for details).
This allowed us to separate vignette development from the focal firm where the experiment was implemented.
Senior managers from this tertiary firm provided crucial input to help make the vignettes realistic, the recall decision *possible but not obvious*, and to verify that the high and low levels of each factor were representative of hypothesized concepts.
Involvement from this third firm also helped ensure that the factors and the wording of the vignettes were generalizable outside of the focal firm.
<!-- para 30 -->
We separate Study 1 into two parts, Study 1a and Study 1b, to delineate treatments conducted at the focal firm (Study 1a) and those using Amazon Mechanical Turk (MTurk) (Study 1b).
The subject pool for Study 1a consisted of managers within the focal firm that had experience making recall decisions.
We felt it was important to use managers initially given the highly contextualized and unique nature of a medical device recall decision and the lack of research exploring this decision.
As is typical for professional subjects, these participants received no monetary compensation.
The study pool was developed by a team headed by the firm's global vice-president of quality who compiled a list of all employees from each relevant functional area (quality, manufacturing, clinical, medical) involved in product recall decisions at the firm's headquarters.
The resulting list consisted of 287 individuals who were each sent an email requesting participation in the study ([Appendix B](#app2)).
This message explained the nature and objective of the study, included a link to the experiment, and clarified that all responses would be anonymous.
A follow-up reminder email was sent 11 days after the initial email ([Appendix B](#app2)).
At the end of the study duration, 167 subjects had participated in the experiment with complete responses.[<sup>9</sup>](#fn9_49 "Link to note")
<!-- para 31 -->
Subjects for Study 1b were drawn from a U.S. only subject pool on MTurk, which allowed us to test the robustness of our main results in a more general population.
These subjects were each compensated $1.00 for participation ([Agrawal et al., 2015](#bib1); [Berinsky et al., 2012](#bib9)).
MTurk has been used successfully in experimental research and has demonstrated generalizability outside of contextual settings ([Kaufmann et al., 2013](#bib32); [Agrawal et al., 2015](#bib1); [Paolacci et al., 2010](#bib46)).
MTurk has also shown close similarity to laboratory settings, and has a subject pool that matches the U.S. population more closely than student-only subject experiments ([Agrawal et al., 2015](#bib1); [Paolacci et al., 2010](#bib46)).
For both Study 1a and 1b, each subject saw only one treatment, making this a between subject design.
The treatments and associated number of subjects for Study 1a and 1b are summarized in [Table 1](#tbl1) [<sup>10</sup>](#fn10_58 "Link to note").

<!-- para 33 -->
Both Study 1a and 1b were conducted online using Qualtrics software.
To participate, a subject first navigated to the Qualtrics website using the link embedded in the email.
The first screen described the experiment and then asked for consent before proceeding to the second screen, where he/she was randomly assigned to one of the treatments and was presented with the associated recall decision scenario.
The second screen contained the constant product failure scenario, the text associated with the factors for a given treatment, and a prompt to choose whether they would recommend recalling or not based on the information provided.
After selecting an option, the subject was taken to the third screen which contained questions related to several control variables intended to capture how individual heterogeneity may influence the recall decision.
<!-- para 35 -->
The choice of control variables for Study 1a was informed by discussions with our industry and FDA partners on what individual level attributes might influence the likelihood to recall, as well as by theory on cognitive decision making.
First, we captured the gender of each respondent (*Male*) as an indicator variable with female as the reference category to control for possible gender differences.
We also measure a subject's functional area and categorized them into quality (*Quality)*, Clinical/Medical (*Clin/Med*) and operations (*Operations)* functions.
We used *Operations* as the reference category, as we learned through our interviews that operations personnel were suspected to be less likely to recall than other functions.
The perceived relationship that the company has with the FDA, a topic of interest specifically raised by FDA leadership, was also suspected to impact decision-makers in the recall decision.
We captured this relationship as whether the subject perceived their company's relationship with the FDA as collaborative, average or confrontational.
Because of the small percentage of responses in the confrontational category (5%), we grouped average and confrontational as the reference category and created an indicator variable for collaborative (*Collaborative FDA rel.*).
It is also possible that a subject's length of employment in the company may affect their recall decision.
To control for this, we measured the number of years of experience that the manager had at the company with four categories (*0–2 years at company, 3–5 years at company, 6–10 years at company, More than 10 years at company*) and created indicator variables treating the most experience (*More than 10 years at company*) as the reference category.
<!-- para 36 -->
Finally, the last means used to examine individual heterogeneity in this decision was a behavioral test examining a subject's cognitive reflection.
The Cognitive Reflection Test (CRT) proposed by [Frederick (2005)](#bib20) involves three questions that require reflection to be answered correctly, as they each have an immediately apparent and seemingly intuitive answer that is incorrect.
To arrive at the correct answers, subjects must override their initial instinctive response and reflect on what the correct answer should be.
The CRT has been used in previous experiments as a means to differentiate between instinctive versus reflective decision-making tendencies in subjects ([Oechssler et al., 2009](#bib44); [Moritz et al., 2014](#bib41)) in many diverse settings.
These include newsvendor ordering behavior ([Moritz et al., 2013](#bib40)), forecasting behavior ([Moritz et al., 2014](#bib41)), moral decision making ([Paxton et al., 2012](#bib47)), the tendency to fall victim to common heuristics and biases in decision making ([Toplak et al., 2011](#bib58)), and rulings by judges ([Guthrie et al., 2007](#bib24)).
<!-- para 37 -->
Our motivation for including cognitive reflection as a control variable came through our discussions with FDA regulators and industry managers who buttressed the importance of reflection.
Specifically, we learned that some recall decision-makers seem to be predisposed in their recall decision.
For example, certain managers hesitated to recall, regularly pushing for more data and analysis to be completed.
On the other hand, others managers seem to arrive at their recall decision more on instinct without the need for additional discussion or data.
We viewed CRT as a possible means to flush out the possible existence of this individual subject attribute in the recall decision.
To measure the influence of CRT, we follow the categorization approach used in prior studies ([Hoppe and Kusterer, 2011](#bib26); [Oechssler et al., 2009](#bib44); [Frederick, 2005](#bib20)) which codes subjects who answer zero or one of the CRT questions correctly as low cognitive reflection (*CR-Low*) and subjects who answer two or more of the CRT questions correctly as high cognitive reflection (*CR-High*).
<!-- para 38 -->
Controls for Study 1b were identical to those used in Study 1a, with the exception of measures that were not applicable to an MTurk subject pool.
Specifically, we made the following changes.
In two questions (functional area and years of experience), we provided an additional response category of “ *No business experience”*.
We also added one new question to determine if the subject worked in the healthcare industry.

In developing [Hypothesis 2](#enun_Hypothesis_2), we conjectured that high defect detectability may influence the likelihood to recall through the perceived harm that may come to the patient customer *or* through the perceived recall costs to the firm.
In developing [Hypothesis 3](#enun_Hypothesis_3), we theorized that a deeper root cause understanding may influence the decision to recall through greater attribution, as managers desire to understand the reason for something before supporting a decision, *or* through its impact on perceived recall costs.

To examine the influence of these potential mechanisms, we invoked the “fit as mediation” perspective ([Venkatraman, 1989](#bib63); [Bozarth and McDermott, 1998](#bib12)), and used Monte Carlo simulation following [Rungtusanatham et al. (2014)](#bib51), [Preacher and Selig (2012)](#bib50) and [MacKinnon et al. (2004)](#bib36).
This explicit mediation approach has been shown to be methodologically superior to more traditionally used implicit mediation methods (e.g., [Baron and Kenny, 1986](#bib7)) in its ability to incorporate multiple mediators simultaneously, and at the same time provide accurate estimates of mediation effects with adequate statistical power ([Rungtusanatham et al., 2014](#bib51)).
We provide a brief summary of the method here and refer the reader to Section 2.2.2.4 of [Rungtusanatham et al. (2014)](#bib51), as well as [Preacher and Selig (2012)](#bib50) and [MacKinnon et al. (2004)](#bib36) for further details.
<!-- para 18 -->
In summary, quantifiable evidence for mediation is found by simulating multiple draws of the *specific indirect effects* of interest and drawing inferences from the empirical sampling distribution of these specific indirect effects.
Each specific indirect effect is the multiplicative product of two β coefficients: the coefficient between the independent variable and each mediator, and the coefficient between each mediator and the dependent variable, which is obtained while controlling for the effects of all other mediators and independent variables.
After conducting *k* simulations of each specific indirect effect, statistical significance is evaluated by examining whether a 95% confidence interval of the specific indirect effect contains zero.
If the confidence interval does not include zero, mediation is statistically supported.
<!-- para 19 -->
Monte Carlo simulation requires that we obtain β coefficients of the experimental factors predicting the mediators, and the mediators predicting recall likelihood after accounting for the partial effects of all experimental factors and mediators.
Because we seek to analyze the relationship between the experimental factors and the three potential mediators simultaneously, we use a Seemingly Unrelated Regression (SUR) analysis with a normal distribution ([Table 8](#tbl8), columns 1–3).
Column 4 of [Table 8](#tbl8) then includes all mediators and experimental factors in a logistic regression model that assesses the partial effect of each of these measures concurrently on recall likelihood ([Rungtusanatham et al., 2014](#bib51)).
The β coefficients from [Table 8](#tbl8) are used in the Monte Carlo simulation to generate the specific indirect effect estimates.
For example, to examine the intervening effect of *Harm* on the relationship between *Defect Detectability* and recall likelihood, we use β = −0.23 from column 1 (*Defect Detectability* predicting *Harm*) and β = 1.32 from column 4 (*Harm* predicting recall likelihood), and their associated standard errors.
We repeat this process to test if *Cost* mediates the influence of *Defect Detectability* and *Root Cause Understanding* on recall likelihood.
Because neither factor predicts *Attribution* (column 3) and because *Attribution* does not predict recall likelihood (column 4), it is not likely a mediator.
We therefore do not consider *Attribution* in the subsequent Monte Carlo simulation analysis.[<sup>12</sup>](#fn12_97 "Link to note")

We simulated 1000 runs in three separate Monte Carlo simulations for the three mediation candidates.
In the simulations, we incorporated the asymptotic covariances of the β coefficients of the experimental factors predicting the mediators, and the asymptotic correlations between the β coefficients of the mediators predicting recall likelihood ([Preacher and Selig, 2012](#bib50); [Rungtusanatham et al., 2014](#bib51); [Davis-Sramek et al., 2017](#bib16)).
We present the results of the specific indirect effects in [Table 9](#tbl9).
First, we find statistically significant evidence of mediation for *Defect Detectability*.
In row 1, we observe that the specific indirect effect of *Harm* between *Defect Detectability* and recall likelihood is negative and significant (−0.30) as the 95% confidence interval does not contain zero.
In row 2, we observe that the specific indirect effect of *Cost* between *Defect Detectability* and recall likelihood is positive and significant (0.08).
These mediation effects are of opposite signs and can be interpreted as follows.
A detectable defect leads to a low perception of patient harm, and low patient harm leads to low recall likelihood.
However, a detectable defect also leads to a low perception of recall costs, and low recall costs leads to high recall likelihood.[<sup>13</sup>](#fn13_102 "Link to note") While these mediation effects are opposing, the effect of *Harm* appears to dominate the effect of *Cost* when comparing the effect sizes of the two specific indirect effects (−0.30 vs. 0.08).
Because we captured the asymptotic covariance and correlation matrices from the SUR and logistic regression analyses in [Table 8](#tbl8), we are able to assess the total indirect effect by combining the specific indirect effects and their 95% confidence interval in the Monte Carlo simulation, similar to [Davis-Sramek et al. (2017)](#bib16).

<!-- para 24 -->
The results show that the total indirect effect of *Harm* and *Cost* between *Defect Detectability* and recall likelihood is −0.21, with a 95% confidence interval of −0.03 on the low end and −0.39 on the high end.[<sup>14</sup>](#fn14_105 "Link to note") Thus, the total indirect effect is negative and significant.
This result confirms that when *Defect Detectability* is high, the perception of reduced patient harm, which leads to a low recall likelihood, dominates the perception of reduced recall costs, which leads to a high recall likelihood.
Finally, in row 3 of [Table 9](#tbl9), we find that the relationship between *Root Cause Understanding* and recall likelihood is mediated by *Cost* with a significant indirect effect of 0.07.
In other words, as *Root Cause Understanding* increases, perceived recall costs decrease, and this reduction in perceived costs leads to a high recall likelihood.
We discuss the managerial and regulator implications of these mediation effects in the discussion section.
<!-- para 25 -->
As previously mentioned, *Attribution* does not mediate the relationship between *Root Cause Understanding* and recall likelihood.
Because this was counter to our expectations, we explored *Attribution* further using a *t* -test.
In [Table 7](#tbl7), we see that 69.4% of subjects (258 subjects) perceived *Attribution*, or “ *the manufacturing and supplier process review* ” (per [Appendix D](#appsec4)) to be either “Very important” or “Extremely important” in “ *determining if a product quality problem existed* ”, while 30.6% (114 subjects) did not.
We examined the influence of *Root Cause Understanding* on likelihood to recall, using this distinction among subjects.
In [Table 10](#tbl10), we see that *Root Cause Understanding* significantly predicts a higher likelihood to recall (p = 0.002) for the 258 subjects who consider the process review to be an important part of their recall decision.
In contrast, *Root Cause Understanding* had no effect on the decision (p = 0.689) for subjects who did not view the process review to be an important consideration.
In other words, the existence of a relationship between *Root Cause Understanding* and recall likelihood depends upon the level of importance subjects attach to attribution.

Across all studies, the coefficient that was greatest in significance and effect size was *CR-High*.
To better understand this persistent relationship between cognitive reflection and recall likelihood, we conducted three post-hoc analyses.
First, we ensured that the subject's treatment did not influence their cognitive reflection responses.
To test this dependence, we used a Chi Square test and found that there was no significant difference between the CRT response and the treatments subjects were exposed to.
This eliminates the possibility that *CR-High* mediates the relationships between our factors and recall likelihood.
<!-- para 29 -->
Second, we tested whether cognitive reflection was acting as a proxy for subject's attention to the experiment by examining the relationship between the time a subject took for the experiment and their CRT score.
Qualtrics records the time that each subject spends on the experiment by tracking the total time from when the individual opens the link to when the last question is answered.
The average time spent across all three studies was seven minutes.
Using three separate Chi Square tests for each study, we found that there was never a significant difference (e.g., p > 0.05) between the time taken for *CR-High* subjects, and *CR-Low* subjects in completing the experiment.
In other words, cognitive reflection does not appear to serve as a proxy for experimental attention or speed.
This substantiates previous cognitive reflection research which identifies CRT as an individual pre-dispositional characteristic, and not as an individual's response to an experiment ([Frederick, 2005](#bib20); [Oechssler et al., 2009](#bib44); [Moritz et al., 2013](#bib40), [2014](#bib41); [Paxton et al., 2012](#bib47); [Toplak et al., 2011](#bib58); [Guthrie et al., 2007](#bib24)).
<!-- para 30 -->
Finally, similar to previous studies, we examined if subjects in the high versus low CR groups behave differently in the experiment ([Frederick, 2005](#bib20); [Hoppe and Kusterer, 2011](#bib26)).
To test for potential differences, most of these studies divide the data into two or more categories by CR scores, and conduct regression analysis in the split groups separately.
We follow the same approach and present the results of logistic regression analyses in [Table 11](#tbl11) at low (columns 1–3) and high (columns 4–6) CR levels for Studies 1a, 1b and 2.
There are two takeaways.
First, demographic characteristics such as gender, functional area and perceived relationship with the FDA are predictive of the recall decision for *CR-Low* subjects.
In particular, subjects at the focal firm (Study 1a) that perceived a collaborative relationship with the FDA in the *CR-Low* group were significantly less likely to recall.
Second, *Defect Detectability* and *Root Cause Understanding* are not statistically significant for *CR-Low* subjects, but they demonstrate moderate (p < 0.10) to high (p < 0.05) statistical significance for *CR-High* subjects.
This indicates that *Defect Detectability* and *Root Cause Understanding* are influential for the more reflective subjects, as opposed to the more instinctive subjects.

## discussion
<!-- para 2 -->
The intent of this research is to take a first step in exploring an under researched behavioral operations domain, that of product-recall decision-making.
As a set, our research provides three main contributions that lead to practical implications for the FDA and medical industry managers.
<!-- para 3 -->
First, we find that the likelihood to recall is significantly influenced by the detectability of the defect.
The more detectable the defect is to the physician customer, the lower the recall likelihood.
This result contributes to quality management research that points to the risks associated with a culture that relies on inspecting-in, instead of building-in, quality ([Deming, 2000](#bib17); [Schmenner and Swink, 1998](#bib53)).
Prior studies have found that building quality into the product and process, instead of inspecting quality into the product, is the means by which high levels of quality performance is achieved ([Samson and Terziovski, 1999](#bib52); [Linderman et al., 2003](#bib34)).
Furthermore, the presence of a final quality inspection step can cause producers to relax their attention to product quality, because they expect someone else to catch their mistakes ([Deming, 2000](#bib17)).
This attitude of “expecting the inspector to catch mistakes”, seems to apply not only to a production line as past research has shown, but also to customers in the supply chain as well.
Specifically, managers seem to view the physician customer as a trustworthy final quality inspector who will screen out their defects and obviate the need to recall when the defect is detectable.
This relationship can be explained through two mechanisms; harm and cost.
Specifically, a defect that is detectable to the physician customer creates a perception of low recall costs and low patient harm.
Moreover, the total indirect effect of these two mechanisms is significant and negative, indicating the importance of patient harm, in comparison to recall costs.
Put another way, when a defect is not detectable, the perception of high patient harm dominates the perception of high recall costs, and leads to a high recall likelihood.
<!-- para 4 -->
The second contribution is related to root cause.
When managers better understand the root cause of a product failure, they are more likely to recall.
This relationship is mediated by perceived recall costs to the firm.
Hence, one reason why managers may seek to identify a definitive root cause before choosing to recall is because it implies low recall costs.
When the underlying causes of a recall are understood, a recall can be bounded to fewer units and potentially impact fewer customers.
Also, while root cause did not influence the importance of attribution in the recall decision, attribution serves as a boundary condition for when root cause matters.
<!-- para 5 -->
A final intriguing contribution is related to cognitive reflection.
Specifically, subjects with high cognitive reflection levels are less likely to recall compared to those with low cognitive reflection levels.
In addition, post-hoc analyses reveal that *CR-Low* managers are influenced by gender, functional area, and FDA relationships in deciding to recall, but are not influenced by the experimental factors.
Conversely, *CR-High* managers are affected by the experimental factors.
This result builds upon cognitive reflection research and the Dual Process Theory underlying it. [Stanovich and West (2000)](#bib56) and [Frederick (2005)](#bib20) indicate that low CR individuals, or System 1 thinkers, make decisions based on instinct, intuition, emotions and relationships and less upon analysis of data.
High CR individuals, or System 2 thinkers, are less likely to recall in general, possibly because they seek to reflect and to analyze more data before deciding, which may explain why they are influenced by root cause understanding and defect detectability in the recall decision.
This finding contributes to the stream of research using cognitive reflection as a means to understand how reflective individuals make decisions differently than instinctive individuals ([Frederick, 2005](#bib20)).
<!-- para 6 -->
Insights gained from this study have implications for both industry managers and regulators at the FDA, and have resulted in significant changes.
We first address recall decision-making process enhancements at the focal firm.
Because the focal firm is a leading manufacturer of medical devices, it is frequently emulated by other firms in the industry.
Consequently, recall process changes at the focal firm may affect how recall decisions are made industry-wide.
When we presented the findings of this study to the management team at the focal firm, the global vice-president of quality gravitated to the results related to defect detectability and cognitive reflection.
Specifically, the executive noted that trusting the physician customer to circumvent a defect is not desirable and that decision-makers in the firm were to be immediately made aware of its undesirable influence on recall decisions.
Additionally, the team was intrigued to find that *CR-High* individuals recalled less often.
To address these findings, the focal firm has instituted a recall decision-making training program that highlights the unwanted bias to use defect detectability as a factor in the recall decision.
The firm also requires all pertinent decision-makers to take the CRT, and informs them of the tendency of *CR-High* managers to recall less often, and their preference towards continued monitoring and investigating of the potential problem.
Regarding root cause understanding, the executive stipulated that recall costs should not be the dominant factor in the recall decision.
However, the team agreed that understanding the root cause before recalling was important because it provides the firm with confidence that it is recalling products only when necessary.
Finally, the team was pleased to see that the influence of one, although influential, physician customer, did not significantly sway managers in making recall decisions.
Though this is somewhat counter to research related to salience bias ([Tiefenbeck et al., 2016](#bib57)), it is perhaps less surprising in our setting, as a decision of this magnitude is pivotal and the voice of one customer may not be sufficient to tip the scales in either direction.
<!-- para 7 -->
Senior managers at the FDA were also quite interested in the findings.
Regulators at the FDA emphasized that root cause does not need to be determined before initiating a recall and that it should not serve as recall decision-making criteria, especially if it is being used as a cost-savings mechanism.
They were also concerned by the finding related to defect detectability, as making recall decisions using this criteria runs counter to their expectations of how firms manage their product quality in the market.
FDA leaders also noted that certain managers (*CR-Low*) were considerably less likely to recall when they perceived a collaborative relationship with the FDA.
The FDA conjectured that perhaps these individuals felt protected by their friendly relationship with regulators, and this perception lowered the likelihood to recall.
FDA leadership stated that future interactions with medical device managers across the industry would be informed by all three of these valuable findings.
<!-- para 8 -->
While this study provides the first foray into behavioral product recall decision-making, an important new field of study considering the prevalence and customer safety impact of product recalls across multiple industries, it is not without limitations.
Vignette studies, by design, reflect a stylized version of their decision context and so external validity is always a potential issue.
Our vignettes do not precisely relate to all possible medical device recall decision contexts.
As such, future research is needed to test the boundary conditions of our findings to other recall decision contexts, including contexts outside the medical device industry.
Another limitation of our research is that the primary factors of interest (i.e, *Physician Concern, Defect Detectability*, and *Root Cause Understanding*) were treated as binary rather than as having multiple levels.
This binary design allows us to identify the existence of factors effects, while keeping the number of treatments to a reasonable number.
Now that a subset of these factors have emerged as having an influence on the likelihood to recall, it would be interesting to delve deeper into the form of these relationships in future research through a more granular view.
The subject pools used for each study also introduce potential limitations.
Our managerial subject pool (Study 1a) is drawn from a single firm, which may raise endogeneity concerns due to single firm bias.
However, we replicate the results of this study with MTurk subjects (in both Study 1b and Study 2), which buttress our confidence in the findings.
Finally, the subject pool for Study 2 was limited to MTurk subjects (i.e., no managerial subject pool was used).
While our main experimental results appear consistent between the MTurk and managerial subject pools, there remains a possibility that the mediation results could differ with a managerial subject pool.
<!-- para 9 -->
Future studies may expand upon factors developed and studied here both within and beyond the medical device industry.
For example, determining recall decision-making factors in the automotive industry may vary as there is no counterpart to “physician intermediary” who can ascertain product quality attributes before product use.
In addition, other cognitive tests besides the CRT may provide additional insight into the relationship between cognitive reflection and recall decision-making.
Even so, our findings open an interesting avenue for future researchers examining product recalls on one hand, and exploring behavioral factors on the other hand.
