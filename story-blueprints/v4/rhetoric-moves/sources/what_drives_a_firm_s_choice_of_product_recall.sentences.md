# what_drives_a_firm_s_choice_of_product_recall 句子库存

## introduction
<!-- para 2 -->
Product-harm crisis refers to an incident in which a product fails to meet a mandatory safety standard, contains a defect that could cause substantial harm to consumers, creates an unreasonable risk of serious injury or death, or fails to comply with a voluntary standard of the industry (Dawar and Pillutla 2000).
When a crisis occurs, the company involved must take corrective actions to prevent further harm to the consumers.
Very often the crisis leads to a product recall, in which the company, jointly with a supervising government agency, informs consumers about the hazard, stops selling the product, and assists consumers in handling the product appropriately.
<!-- para 3 -->
In the recall process, companies make two critical decisions: timing and remedy.
Several recent studies have provided insights on the timing issue, that is, when to issue a recall and whether to issue a recall at all (Chen, Ganesan, and Liu 2009; Hora, Bapuji, and Roth 2011).
Our study focuses on remedy.
As a vital component of recalls, a remedy is the corrective or compensation measure that companies provide for the defective products.
For instance, companies that issue recalls with the U.S. Consumer Product Safety Commission (CPSC) can provide full remedies, in the form of a refund or replacement that completely compensates consumers, or partial remedies, such as repair or a discount for a future purchase.
<!-- para 4 -->
Companies’ legal obligation during product recalls is to ensure that the hazardous products are no longer sold and that the units already sold are either repaired or disposed of appropriately.
Beyond these basic objectives, whether a more complete remedy is provided to consumers is influenced by the companies’ incentives and goals.
Different companies often provide different remedies even when recalling the same type of product.
As an example, when recalling similar hair dryers, Big Lots offered a full refund, but World Dryer Corp chose to send a repair kit to customers.
<!-- para 5 -->
This article examines what drives companies to provide a better or worse remedy to consumers.
Substantively, understanding the drivers of recall remedy is critical because remedy has significant impacts on company performance metrics.
Scholars of service recovery and complaint management have consistently found that different compensation actions produce a variety of impacts on consumers.
These include cognitive and perceptual outcomes, such as customer satisfaction and loyalty (Roschk and Gelbrich 2014), and behavioral consequences, such as repurchase and word of mouth (Noone 2012).
<!-- para 6 -->
Our contribution to the literature is threefold.
First, despite the frequent occurrence of product recalls and the important implications of remedy for recall management, company performance, and consumer welfare, almost no research has examined recall remedy.
Our study fills this important gap.
<!-- para 7 -->
Second, a growing number of studies have provided valuable insights on the impacts of recalls and recall strategies.
However, few have paid attention to the determinants of company decisions during recalls.
For companies, different remedies imply different financial obligations.
It is usually more expensive to provide a full refund than a repair.
At the same time, more severe product hazards demand better remedies.
This is the first study to offer and test a theoretical framework that considers the trade-off between remedy cost to the company and consumer harm.
<!-- para 8 -->
Third, a central theme of our article is that remedy decisions are influenced not only by recall and company characteristics, but also by the incentives of top executives.
In recent years, these executives, especially CEOs, have taken on higher levels of responsibility in the recall process due to public pressure and the companies' desires for more effective crisis management (Miller and Littlefield 2010; Olaniran et al. 2012). [^1] Our results show that a CEO's financial interests not only directly influence the choice of remedy but also moderate the effects of recall characteristics.
Incorporating these “people” factors into the theoretical framework and studying their impacts on remedy constitute a novel addition to the literature. [^2]
<!-- para 9 -->
In the remainder of the article, we first present the background literature about recall remedy.
We then discuss the theoretical framework and develop the hypotheses for the effects of remedy cost, consumer harm, CEO characteristics, and their interactions.
This is followed by descriptions of the data, empirical methods, and findings.
We conclude with a discussion of implications and further research opportunities.

## theory
<!-- para 4 -->
Several scholars have focused on the impact of firm strategies or actions on postcrisis outcomes.
Cleeren, Dekimpe, and Helsen (2008) find that brand advertising can be used to counter the negative effects of product recalls.
Klein and Dawar (2004) study how corporate social responsibility influences the impact of a product-harm crisis on brand evaluation.
Dawar and Pillutla (2000) show that a company's crisis management strategy, ranging from unambiguous support to unambiguous stonewalling, affects its brand equity.
Chen, Ganesan, and Liu (2009) study how proactive versus passive strategies in recall handling can lead to different stock market reactions.
<!-- para 5 -->
Finally, some studies have investigated the issue of recall timing.
Hora, Bapuji, and Roth (2011) find it takes longer to recall when the defect is due to a design flaw (vs. a manufacturing problem) and when the company is at the upper end of the supply chain.
Chen, Ganesan, and Liu (2009) show that some companies issue recalls early (before any safety incidents are reported), and others do so late (after safety incidents have been reported).

It is noteworthy that none of the prior research has examined recall remedy or the impact of CEO characteristics.
<!-- para 8 -->
Remedies commonly adopted in product recalls range from those fully compensating the consumers to those that are less complete (i.e., partial).
In some recalls, companies allow consumers to return defective products for a full refund or a free replacement.
For example, when recalling Shelly's Diner Collectible Ceramic because "the diner's power adapter can overheat and melt the adapter's plastic housing, posing a fire hazard" (CPSC 2013), Enesco offered consumers the choice of a full refund or a new adapter.
<!-- para 9 -->
Companies sometimes provide free repair as remedy.
They instruct consumers to stop using the recalled products and to contact the companies or designated dealers for a free inspection and repair.
For example, Midea USA recalled a dehumidifier because “a component in the dehumidifier’s compressor can short circuit, posing a fire hazard” (CPSC 2014) and offered free repair.
In some recalls, companies do not provide the repair service.
Instead, they provide a kit so consumers can repair the faulty products themselves.
For instance, when four million Bumbo baby seats were recalled due to possible fall hazard, the company urged the consumers to “immediately stop using the products until they order and install a free repair kit” (CPSC 2012).
Finally, some companies provide a discount that consumers can apply to the future purchase.
For example, in a recent recall of dishwashers, Maytag offered consumers a $75 cash reimbursement following the purchase of a new Maytag dishwasher.

<!-- para 13 -->
To distinguish company strategies as being more versus less responsive to a crisis, prior research has classified them as “unambiguous support” versus “stonewalling” (Dawar and Pillutla 2000), “proactive” versus “passive” (Chen, Ganesan, and Liu 2009), and “supereffort” versus “denial” (Siomkos and Kurzbard 1994).
This type of dichotomous classification is desirable for the study of recall remedy, too.
Full refund or replacement constitutes a “full” remedy that is the most responsive.
The consumers can receive all their money back or a completely new product that is free of the defect.
These are also the most expensive remedy actions.
In the case of refund, companies take a loss equal to the amount of the product sales, whereas in the case of replacement they bear the full cost of the replacement products and the associated costs.
In contrast, “partial” remedies such as free repair, consumer self-repair, and discounts for future purchase are less costly to the company and compensate consumers to a lesser extent.
Even with free repair, consumers still need to keep the original products that had defects (which are hopefully repaired to be safe).
In the Maytag dishwasher recall mentioned earlier, the discount cost the company $75 per dishwasher, whereas a full refund would have cost as much as as$800.[^3]
<!-- para 14 -->
The distinction between full and partial remedy is fundamental and conceptually important, whereas the differences among the various partial remedies are more of varying degree.
Thus, in this article, we focus on company remedy decisions that differentiate between full and partial remedies as a group in our theoretical discussions and empirical tests.
<!-- para 15 -->
It is important to note that in addition to compensating consumers, remedy actions have long-term implications for companies.
Compared to partial remedy, full remedy makes consumers more satisfied and, by reducing consumers' loss, generates higher perceived distributive justice (Deutsch 1985).
Full remedy is also more likely to be perceived by consumers as responsible behavior because the company incurs higher compensation cost.
These benefits help restore consumers' trust and enhance their willingness to continue dealing with the company in the future (Siegel and Vitaliano 2007).
As a result, full remedy is more beneficial than partial remedy for companies' long-term value.

We now develop the theoretical framework (illustrated in Figure 1).
We first discuss two basic recall characteristics, remedy cost and consumer harm, which form the base for remedy choice.
We then discuss how the CEO's personal interests may influence a company's remedy decision, both directly and indirectly through interactions with remedy cost and consumer harm.
We focus on two CEO characteristics that the literature has frequently found to influence corporate decision making: the CEO's compensation and tenure status (Cheng 2004; Luo, Kanuri, and Andrews 2014).
<!-- para 20 -->
Recalls are costly to companies.
Under the U.S. Generally Accepted Accounting Principles, remedy spending is immediately expensed and thus decreases the company's reported earnings.
In addition to the higher cost described earlier, full remedy is more expensive than partial remedy for two important reasons.
First, consumers view full remedy as a more attractive compensation offering than partial remedy (Kelley, Hoffman, and Davis 1993).
Therefore, the response rate to the recall is higher, which increases total cost to the company.
Although this consequence better serves the public interest, it further widens the cost gap between full and partial remedy for the company.
<!-- para 21 -->
Second, recalls can induce unfavorable reactions from business collaborators.
Due to information asymmetry about the severity of the crisis, collaborators can use the company's remedy actions as a signal.
Full remedy, especially when the total product value involved is high, can be interpreted as meaning that the crisis is severe and the company is desperately trying to appeal to consumers (Chen, Ganesan, and Liu 2009).
The higher perceived risk of the crisis can result in collaborators taking actions to protect their own interests.
For instance, suppliers could tighten credit policy and require the company to issue interest-bearing notes as a guarantee of future payment.
These actions, in turn, impose additional financial burden on the recall company's earnings.
<!-- para 22 -->
Therefore, when the recall involves a higher total value of products, due to a higher unit price, greater quantity, or both, full remedy becomes increasingly more expensive than partial remedy.
We thus make the following prediction:
<!-- para 23 -->
$H_{1}$: The higher the total value of products to be recalled, the less likely is the company to use full remedy in product recalls.
<!-- para 25 -->
Because the fundamental purpose of recall is to protect consumers and to compensate them for the purchase of faulty products, remedy decisions should be commensurate with the potential harm to consumers (Roschk and Gelbrich 2014).
The more severe the hazard (e.g., a potentially fatal hazard vs. a malfunction that might only cause minor injuries), the greater the need for full remedy so that consumers will return or dispose of the products more effectively.
To the extent that companies are sensitive to consumer safety, full remedy is more likely to be provided when the recalled products are more hazardous.
<!-- para 26 -->
Besides attending to consumer safety, companies are more likely to adopt full remedy when product hazard is higher for two reasons.
First, the government agencies handling product recalls often gauge the severity of potential harm and put pressure on companies to adopt appropriate remedy actions.
Second, when the product hazard is more severe, public attention and the scrutiny on companies is greater.
Thus, we propose the following hypothesis:
<!-- para 27 -->
$H_{2}$: The more severe the product hazard, the more likely is the company to use full remedy in product recalls.
<!-- para 29 -->
Among all executive positions, the CEO holds the most power of discretion, faces the least restrictive oversight, and thus has the ability to manifest personal preferences into organizational decisions (Finkelstein and Boyd 1998).
In the context of product recalls, CEOs often possess private information due to the complexities in the production and distribution processes.
They know more about the nature and scope of the crisis than general shareholders and other stakeholders.
This makes remedy decisions difficult for others to scrutinize, and it leaves room for the CEO's personal interests to play a role.
<!-- para 30 -->
CEO compensation.
Compensation ties the CEO’s personal wealth to company performance.
The compensation package usually includes short-term and long-term components (Gibbons and Murphy 1992).
The short-term component is cash compensation of salary and bonus, which is generally based on short-term accounting-based earnings.
In contrast, the long-term component includes option and restricted stock grants, which are linked to the performance reflected in stock prices.
Because cash and equity compensations provide the CEO with different incentives, we expect them to have different effects on remedy choices.
<!-- para 31 -->
If the CEO receives a greater amount of cash compensation (vs. equity), we predict there is greater incentive to avoid full remedy.
It is well-known that linking CEO compensation to accounting-based performance encourages the CEO to work hard, but at the same time, this induces the CEO to be myopic and to put disproportional effort into boosting current earnings (Gibbons and Murphy 1992).
For instance, Coles, Daniel, and Naveen (2006) find that cash compensation motivates CEOs to decrease investment in R&D activities that benefit the company only in the long run.
For product recalls, full remedy is more expensive and reduces the company's short-term earnings to a greater extent than partial remedy.
Thus,
<!-- para 32 -->
$H_{3a}$: Companies whose CEOs receive greater cash compensation are less likely to provide full remedy in product recalls.
<!-- para 33 -->
On the other hand, we predict that full remedy is more likely to be provided when the CEO's long-term equity incentive is greater.
In contrast to cash compensation, options and restricted stocks that the CEO receives as compensation can be vested over time and will be valid for a number of years.
This effectively defers realization of the compensation into the future, motivating the CEO to focus more on long-term firm value and less on current earnings (Leone, Wu, and Zimmerman 2006).
As such, equity incentive is often adopted by shareholders as a key mechanism to align the CEO's interests with long-term firm value.
For example, Holthausen, Larcker, and Sloan (1995) show that an increase in long-term compensation stimulates innovation activities when the payoffs of those activities are unlikely to be realized immediately.
Gopalan et al. (2014) find a negative relationship between deferred compensation and the CEO's propensity to engage in behaviors that boost only short-term earnings.
<!-- para 34 -->
As discussed earlier, full remedy provides long-term benefits to the company through enhancing customer satisfaction and trust.
Therefore, equity incentive should motivate the CEO to provide full remedy, leading to the following prediction:
<!-- para 35 -->
$H_{3b}$: Companies whose CEOs receive greater equity incentive are more likely to provide full remedy in product recalls.
<!-- para 36 -->
CEO tenure.
The length of tenure in the position is a major influencer of a CEO's power and decisions (Pfeffer 1981).
A longer tenure generates advantages such as experience and knowledge about the company.
However, it has important drawbacks.
Previous research has shown that a longer tenure makes a CEO more likely to make myopic decisions (e.g., Luo, Kanuri, and Andrews 2014).
This can happen for several reasons.
First, from a motivation perspective, a CEO with longer tenure is more entrenched in the system and less interested in striving for innovation and long-term goals.
For example, Stevens, Beyer, and Trice (1978) find that long-tenured CEOs are resistant to changes, even to changes that drive competitive advantages.
Second, from a monitoring perspective, the greater power that comes from longer tenure makes it less likely that a long-tenured CEO will receive a penalty for inefficient decisions (Hambrick and Fukutomi 1991).
This makes it more likely for such CEOs to focus on the short-term and/or personal interests.
Third, a CEO with longer tenure often pays less attention to consumer needs (Wu, Levitas, and Priem 2005).
Along this line, Zhou et al. (2008) report that long-tenured CEOs are less active in using customer feedback and satisfaction data to create value and sustain customer relationships.
<!-- para 37 -->
Given that CEOs with longer tenure are less likely to take long-term and customer-oriented actions than CEOs with shorter tenure, we make the following prediction:
<!-- para 38 -->
$H_{4}$: Companies whose CEOs have had longer tenure in the position are less likely to provide full remedy in product recalls.
<!-- para 40 -->
Based on similar theoretical reasoning, we now explore how the three CEO characteristics (CEO cash compensation, equity incentive, and tenure) might moderate the impact of the two recall characteristics (remedy cost and consumer harm) on remedy.
This involves a total of six interaction effects.
<!-- para 41 -->
Because of the short- versus long-term orientation of CEO cash compensation versus equity incentive, these two CEO characteristics should moderate the impact of recall characteristics in opposite directions.
Greater cash compensation induces a greater focus on current earnings (Leone, Wu, and Zimmerman 2006), thus exacerbating the (negative) effect of remedy cost on the likelihood of full remedy.
In contrast, greater equity incentive induces more attention to long-term benefits (Coles, Daniel, and Naveen 2006), thus mitigating the effect of remedy cost.
<!-- para 42 -->
$H_{5}$: The negative impact of product value on the likelihood of full remedy is enhanced (reduced) when the CEO receives greater cash (equity) compensation.
<!-- para 43 -->
In terms of consumer harm, similar logic predicts that greater cash compensation reduces the (positive) effect of product hazard on full remedy, whereas greater equity incentive increases it:
<!-- para 44 -->
$H_{6}$: The positive impact of product hazard on the likelihood of full remedy is reduced (enhanced) when the CEO receives greater cash (equity) compensation.
<!-- para 45 -->
Because a CEO with longer tenure tends to pay less attention to the benefits of long-term strategies (Zhou et al. 2008), the interactions between tenure and recall characteristics resemble those for CEO cash compensation.
Specifically:
<!-- para 46 -->
$H_{7}$: The negative impact of product value on the likelihood of full remedy is enhanced when the CEO has a longer tenure with the position.
<!-- para 47 -->
$H_{8}$: The positive impact of product hazard on the likelihood of full remedy is reduced when the CEO has a longer tenure with the position.

## methods
<!-- para 2 -->
The data for empirical testing were collected from the CPSC website.
The recalls occurred from January 1996 to December 2007 and include a wide range of consumer products, such as toys, household products, and sporting products.
A CPSC recall announcement contains the product description, recall date, company that issued the recall, quantity and price range of the recalled product, time period when the recalled product was sold in the market, and the product hazard.
We focus on companies publicly traded on the New York Stock Exchange so that company information and stock return data can be reliably obtained.
This generated a sample of 259 CPSC recalls.
We collected CEO characteristics from Compustat's Execucomp database.
When we exclude companies with missing CEO data, the final sample includes 170 recalls.
<!-- para 3 -->
To test the hypotheses, we analyze the likelihood that full remedy is provided in the recall events.
We estimate the probit model as follows:
<!-- para 4 -->
(1)
<!-- para 5 -->
$$ \begin{aligned}Prob(Fullremedy=1)&=\alpha+\beta_{1}Prodvalue+\beta_{2}Hazard\\&\quad+\beta_{3}CEOcash+\beta_{4}CEOequity\\&\quad+\beta_{5}CEOtenure\\&\quad+\beta_{6}CEOcash\times Prodvalue\\&\quad+\beta_{7}CEOequity\times Prodvalue\\&\quad+\beta_{8}CEOcash\times Hazard\\&\quad+\beta_{9}CEOequity\times Hazard\\&\quad+\beta_{10}CEOtenure\times Prodvalue\\&\quad+\beta_{11}CEOtenure\times Hazard\\&\quad+\beta_{12}Covariates+\varepsilon\end{aligned} $$
<!-- para 6 -->
The dependent variable, Fullremedy, equals 1 if full remedy is provided and 0 otherwise.
Prodvalue is the logarithm transformation of the total value of recalled products, measured by the number of units recalled multiplied by the unit price.
Hazard measures the severity of the product harm.
Following the three-category system used by government agencies, including CPSC and FDA, Hazard = 1 indicates the minimum risk of injury, Hazard = 2 indicates a medium level of risk, and Hazard = 3 indicates the highest likelihood of death or grievous injury.
<!-- para 7 -->
The CEO compensation variables, CEOcash (for cash compensation) and CEOequity (for equity incentive), follow the common approach in finance (Armstrong and Vashishtha 2012).
CEOcash is the logarithm transformation of the total cash compensation received by the CEO in the year before the recall.
CEOequity is measured by the logged change in the value of the CEO's stock and option portfolio due to a 1% change in stock price.
CEOtenure is the logged number of days the CEO had been in the position at the time of the recall (Luo, Kanuri, and Andrews 2014). [^4] For interpretation purposes, the variables involved in the construction of interaction effects are mean-centered (Echambadi and Hess 2007).
<!-- para 8 -->
The covariates include the following variables obtained from the CPSC announcements.
Selltime is the logged number of days the particular product had been sold in the market when it was recalled.
For a product recalled, CPSC provides two time stamps on the announcement page: the date of the recall and the range of dates when the product was sold on the market.
The starting date of this range is when the product was first sold.
If there are multiple models of the recalled product and there are different starting dates, we adopted the earliest starting date.
We then calculated the difference between the starting date and the recall date to measure how many days had passed since the product was first sold.
Injury is the logged number of injuries (including deaths) reported on the CPSC website when the recall occurred.
In addition, a set of product category dummies following the CPSC categorization are included: Prod_toy for toy products, Prod_child for nontoy children's products, Prod_sports for sporting goods, and Prod_specialty for specialty products.
Household and other products are classified in the holdout (base) category.
These category fixed effects control for the different natures of products that could influence recall remedy.
For instance, companies that produce children products might have a different tendency to use full remedy than companies that produce household products.
Such differences could be caused by factors like technical complexity and industry customs.
<!-- para 9 -->
Our estimation includes company characteristics that may influence remedy strategies.
Size is the logarithm-transformed sales revenue in the year prior to the recall.
Brand is a dummy variable for whether the recalled product uses the company name as its brand name.
To control for the potential impact from company reputation, we follow previous research (Fombrun and Shanley 1990) and include the reputation scores from Fortune magazine's annual "America's Most Admired Companies" survey.
To cope with the correlation between company size and reputation, we regress the reputation scores on company size and use the residuals as the measure of reputation (Reputation).
The literature suggests that recall experience might be relevant for recall strategies (Kalaignanam, Kushwaha, and Eilert 2013).
We thus include recall experience (Recallexp) according to the number of previous recalls for the company.
To control for the potential impact of the timing of the recall, we follow Chen, Ganesan, and Liu (2009) to include a dummy variable (Earlyrecall) for whether the recall was announced before any safety incidents were reported to the company and the CPSC.
Finally, we include the dummy variable Retailer, which measures whether the recall company is a retailer or manufacturer (Hora, Bapuji, and Roth 2011).

In this section, we provide three analyses of the potential endogeneity in CEO compensation, the impact of recall remedy on firm value, and the effect of using proportions of cash versus equity incentives in the estimation.
Together, these analyses demonstrate the robustness of our results and lend empirical support to the point that remedy decisions are important considerations for the company.
<!-- para 39 -->
One may worry about the potential endogeneity in CEO compensation and whether this has influenced our estimation. [^6] Endogeneity could arise from either simultaneity or omitted variables.
Simultaneity should not be an issue for our analysis.
First, product recalls are incidents that occur unexpectedly and infrequently.
It is unlikely that boards of directors can conjecture the occurrence of a recall for a future year and systematically incorporate that into decisions about CEO compensation.
Not surprisingly, the common influencers of CEO compensation documented by the literature include factors such as growth opportunity, operation complexity, company ownership structure, managerial labor market, and corporate governance (Cordeiro and Veliyath 2003; Core, Holthausen, and Larcker 1999), but not product recalls.
Second, CEO compensation is usually a multiyear contract.
It is unlikely that the particular elements of the compensation package can be adapted in anticipation of a particular recall in a future year.
Third, and importantly, our analysis uses the CEO compensation from the year before the recall.
This directly breaks down the simultaneity link.
What remains in the analysis is only the potential impact of compensation on remedy; the reverse cannot occur.
This leaves omitted variables as the possible reason for endogeneity.
That is, there might be characteristics unobservable to the researchers that influence both CEO compensation and the remedy decisions.
We address this issue with instrumental variables as follows.
<!-- para 40 -->
We start by choosing appropriate instruments.
Core, Holthausen, and Larcker (1999) suggest three instruments for CEO compensation when firm strategies are analyzed: the ratio of earnings to total assets, cumulative annual stock returns, and the company's growth opportunities.
These variables are correlated with CEO compensation but are unlikely to be correlated with the error terms of remedy decisions in Equation 1 once the recall, company, and CEO characteristics are controlled. [^7] We further follow Hausman and Taylor (1981) to include the average CEO compensation from the same industry as an instrument.
These industry values correlate with company values but clearly have no bearing on a particular company's remedy decision.
We construct the corresponding instruments for the interaction terms following Wooldridge (2002).
<!-- para 41 -->
We then test the strength and validity of these instruments following standard procedures (Bascle 2008; Cleeren, Van Heerde, and Dekimpe 2013).
The conditional likelihood ratio test is conducted to examine the strength of the instruments due to the probit model specification and multiple variables being treated as potentially endogenous (Andrews and Stock 2007; Moreira 2003).
In the first-stage regression, the p-values corresponding to the likelihood ratio criterion are less than .05, rejecting the null hypothesis that the instruments are weak.
We then conduct the Hansen J test to assess the validity of the instruments.
The test cannot reject the null hypothesis that the instruments are uncorrelated with the error terms (p > .30). [^8]
<!-- para 42 -->
With the strength and validity of the instruments confirmed, we conduct the endogeneity test for the CEO compensation variables.
We do this in two different ways to demonstrate the consistency of the test result.
First, we conduct the Wald test (Wooldridge 2002) that accommodates the probit model specification.
The test statistics cannot reject the null hypothesis that CEO compensation is exogenous (p > .24).
<!-- para 43 -->
Second, we employ the control function approach and include the residuals from the first stage in the second-stage analysis.
One major benefit of the control function approach is that one can directly test the presence of endogeneity through the statistical significance of residual terms (Imbens and Wooldridge 2007).
None of our control function residuals is significant.
These tests provide strong evidence that endogeneity is not an issue in our analysis.
<!-- para 45 -->
As discussed earlier, there is ample evidence that remedy or other corrective actions during crisis events affect important company performance metrics.
A better remedy positively influences consumers and helps generate customer satisfaction, loyalty, and repurchase intention (Noone 2012; Roschk and Gelbrich 2014).
Even though our study focuses on the determinants of remedy, we perform an analysis on how remedy affects a different performance metric, firm value (Srinivasan and Hanssens 2009), to further demonstrate the managerial relevance of remedy strategy. [^9]
<!-- para 46 -->
Following the literature on firm value, we collected stock return data from the University of Chicago's Center for Research in Security Prices (CRSP) and calculated cumulative abnormal returns (CAR) for the commonly used event window: the recall day and the day after that (Geyskens, Gielens, and Dekimpe 2002).
Abnormal returns were calculated as daily returns minus the size-decile-matched CRSP Value-Weighted Index (Rogers and Stocken 2005).
When there are multiple recalls by the same company in the event window, the stock returns are influenced by all recalls simultaneously and it becomes impossible to isolate the stock return impact of a particular recall.
Thus, additional recalls need to be removed to avoid confounding effects.
This reduced the sample size from 170 to 157.
We also followed the event study literature to search the archives of the Wall Street Journal to ensure there was no other financially relevant event in the event window.
<!-- para 47 -->
Because remedy is driven by the determinants in Equation 1, the analysis of the impact of remedy (full vs. partial) on firm value is essentially a mediation test accounting for remedy selection.
We employ the Heckman model to do this.
We first construct the inverse Mills ratio, which serves as the self-selection correction parameter, from the probit model of Equation 1.
The dummy variable for full remedy, the full set of recall and CEO characteristics, and the inverse Mills ratio are then included in the second-step regression on CAR.
Model identification requires at least one variable that affects the remedy choice, but not stock returns, to be included in the remedy equation but not the performance equation (Hamilton and Nickerson 2003).
We thus exclude Reputation from the second-step regression because its correlation with CAR is extremely low (.017) and nonsignificant.
We further run a regression of CAR on all the independent variables in Equation 1.
Again, the parameter estimate of Reputation is nonsignificant. [^10]
<!-- para 49 -->
The estimations clearly show that across all three model specifications, recall remedy consistently has a significant impact on firm value.
Furthermore, the parameter estimate is negative—full remedy induces lower stock returns than partial remedy upon the announcement of recall.
This presents an interesting contrast between investor and consumer behaviors.
Whereas consumers react positively to responsive actions such as full remedy (Noone 2012; Roschk and Gelbrich 2014), investors appear to be more sophisticated, in that they often interpret a company's proactive action as a signal that the crisis is very severe.
Thus, as Chen, Ganesan, and Liu (2009) suggest in a different context, companies that adopt full remedy must communicate effectively to stakeholders to avoid inadvertent misinterpretation of the remedy decision.

Our theoretical development and empirical testing are based on how the amounts of cash and equity incentives motivate the CEO to favor certain remedy options during product recalls.
As stated earlier, we have followed the established accounting and finance literature to include the cash compensation amount and the sensitivity of the CEO's equity portfolio to a 1% change in stock price in the estimation (Armstrong and Vashishtha 2012; Coles, Daniel, and Naveen 2006).
However, one might suggest that the proportions of cash and equity incentives to their sum could be used as alternative measures for the analysis.
It is important to note that the dollar value of compensation is often the direct motivation for the executives.
For instance, a CEO will be likely to react to $10 million cash but is unlikely to be motivated when the cash amount is merely$10,000, regardless of the proportions.
Thus, it is important to examine the effects of cash and equity amounts on short- versus long-term behaviors.
<!-- para 59 -->
Nevertheless, to check the potential impact of cash versus equity incentives when they are measured as proportions, we conducted a separate analysis and found fairly consistent results.
The cash proportion has a negative impact on the probability of providing full remedy ( $\beta = -2.662, p < .01$), and its interactions with product value ( $\beta = -.847, p < .01$) and product hazard ( $\beta = -2.381, p < .10$) are both negative.
By the construction of these proportions, equity proportion has the opposite effects to those of cash proportion.

The number of product recalls has increased in recent years as a result of the globalization of supply chains, increasing technical and engineering complexity, greater consumer demand for safety, and higher safety standards (Chen, Ganesan, and Liu 2009).
Several large-scale recalls, such as the series of Mattel toy recalls between 2006 and 2009, Toyota automobile recalls during 2009–2011, and the automobile recalls by General Motors in 2014, have caught national attention.
Our study examines a vital but understudied aspect of product recalls: remedy to consumers, and, more specifically, the factors that influence companies' recall remedy choices.
We also delve into the "people" aspect of remedy strategy by analyzing the impacts of CEO compensation and tenure.
Although these CEO characteristics have been studied extensively in the finance, accounting, and management literature streams, little marketing research has accounted for their effects in critical events.
Our findings have important implications for recall strategy, consumer welfare and public policy, and leadership ethics.
<!-- para 62 -->
Implications for the cost and benefit trade-off in remedy strategy.
Our findings illustrate the fundamental conflicts companies have to balance between remedy cost and consumer harm.
On one hand, companies try to avoid full remedy when the total value of recalled products is greater.
This is a short-term-oriented decision motivated by the impact on current earnings.
On the other hand, consumer harm motivates companies to provide better remedy to consumers for longer-term benefit.
Companies are thus more likely to use full remedy when product hazard is more severe.
To the extent that the immediate financial loss is bearable, companies should put greater emphasis on full remedy so that the important long-term benefit through consumer satisfaction and trust can be obtained.
The responsible decision that Johnson & Johnson made in 1982 to unconditionally recall all Tylenol products provides a good illustration (Benson 1988).
Even though the company suffered major revenue losses in the short term, it maintained consumer trust and regained the lost market share within 12 months.
Managing the crisis proactively helped Johnson & Johnson establish itself as a responsible company.

Implications of CEO incentives for remedy strategy.
We find, in many ways undesirably, that CEOs appear to act out of their personal interests in making remedy decisions.
Companies (and boards of directors) must be aware of such influences on product recalls.
To facilitate more responsive remedy actions, companies will benefit by giving their CEOs greater equity incentive.
This benefit is in addition to the other motivating roles of equity incentive, such as inducing long-term R&D investment.
Companies with a CEO who has been in the position for a long period and who receives a large amount of cash compensation need to be especially cautious and exercise additional pressure on the CEO if full remedy is to be provided.
<!-- para 64 -->
Implications for consumer welfare and public policy.
Government agencies regulating product recalls have generally focused on product characteristics in the recall process.
Our study indicates a likely policy oversight with remedy regulation.
To truly protect consumers and motivate them to effectively dispose of hazardous products, policy interventions should be implemented on recall remedy to overcome the resistance induced by the short-term financial interest of the companies and the personal incentives of top executives.
The interventions could be through regulatory means or financial incentives such as tax relief.
For those companies that pay their CEOs heavily in cash or that have CEOs with very long tenure, government agencies should pay particular attention.
<!-- para 65 -->
Implications for leadership ethics.
Both within and beyond the marketing functions, business ethics and ethical behavior are increasingly recognized as an integral component of effective leadership (Bass and Steidlmeier 1999).
Product-harm crisis is one of the most critical moments that test the strength and effectiveness of leadership (Pillai 1996).
During a crisis, leadership ethics is challenged to the maximum extent, and its impact, both internal and external to the organization, can be substantial.
With other factors held constant, the negative impact of a CEO's personal interests on full remedy can be unethical.
Thus, companies adopting ethical management principles and managers trying to be ethical leaders must be mindful of the conflicts embedded in recall remedy decisions.

Limitations and further research.
This article is among the first to examine the effect of “people” factors in product recalls.
Our focus is on the CEO, who has the most influence on corporate decisions.
Future work could explore other corporate governance mechanisms and personnel.
For example, how does the CMO, as a key member of the executive team, influence recall strategies?
Also, are companies with a stronger marketing orientation more likely to take proactive recall actions?
Answers to these questions will further demonstrate the value of marketing within companies.
<!-- para 67 -->
This study distinguishes full remedy from partial remedies as a group, and our results should be interpreted within this scope.
We tried an ordered logit estimation that includes all remedy categories separately.
Some key results, such as the effects of product value, product hazard, CEO cash compensation, and the interaction between CEO equity incentive and product hazard, remain similar.
However, some other effects of recall and CEO characteristics are below traditional significance levels.
These results indicate that the impacts of recall and CEO characteristics, and their interactions, are more significant and predictive of whether the remedy is full or partial.
They are not highly useful in explaining the more subtle differences among the subcategories of partial remedy.
Future studies with richer data on remedy and CEO characteristics could explore these more nuanced differences.
