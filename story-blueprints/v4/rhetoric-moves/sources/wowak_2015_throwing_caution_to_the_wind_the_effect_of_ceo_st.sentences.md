# wowak_2015_throwing_caution_to_the_wind_the_effect_of_ceo_st 句子库存

## introduction
<!-- para 2 -->
A central premise of agency theory is that organizations can align the interests of chief executive officers (CEOs) and shareholders by designing compensation arrangements that reward CEOs for gains in shareholder value (Eisenhardt, 1989).
Such incentives are thought to be useful in counterbalancing a CEO's natural tendency toward risk aversion, which stems from a desire to avoid the substantial personal losses (including their income, reputation, and job) that can result from risky initiatives (Milgrom and Roberts, 1992).
This prescription has been widely heeded by boards of directors, who have consistently turned to stock options as a vehicle to motivate CEOs (Sanders and Hambrick, 2007).
Stock options, which give the recipient the right to purchase company shares at a predetermined price over a specified period of time, offer a lucrative proposition: the CEO benefits from future increases in the share price, but neither gains nor loses if the share price goes down.
This is supposed to encourage CEOs to aggressively pursue higher-risk initiatives with the potential to increase shareholder wealth.
Consistent with this logic, evidence suggests that options encourage risk taking such as increased acquisition activity (Sanders, 2001) and decreased hedging (Rajgopal and Shevlin, 2002).
But in an interesting paradox, options have also been linked to outcomes that are seemingly the opposite of what they are meant to achieve.
For example, CEO options have been shown to increase the likelihood of earnings manipulation (Harris and Bromiley, 2007) and shareholder lawsuits (Peng and Roell, 2008).
It seems that options do prompt aggressive responses by CEOs, but whether these responses reflect the best interests of the organization is less clear.
<!-- para 3 -->
Addressing this ambiguity, Sanders and Hambrick (2007) conducted a study of how CEO stock options influenced firms' strategic actions and subsequent performance outcomes.
In line with agency theory expectations, the authors found that abundant option pay led to larger and more high-variance investment outlays, as well as more extreme performance.
But they also found an intriguing result: option-loaded CEOs delivered more big losses than big gains.
The authors argued that because option-loaded CEOs benefit greatly from big increases in company share prices, but lose nothing if share prices go down, they will gravitate toward initiatives with the highest upside potential (“big wins”) while ignoring or downplaying the possibility of negative outcomes (“big losses”).
Sanders and Hambrick suggested that “the asymmetric payoff from stock options encourages risk taking that is more careless and uncontrolled than that envisioned in the theoretical models justifying stock options” (2007: 1063).
<!-- para 4 -->
Our study builds on this idea by highlighting a previously unexplored consequence of CEO stock option pay: a higher incidence of product safety problems.
Consumers can experience their own “big losses” when companies release unsafe products into the marketplace, and the pursuit of stock option payoffs may cause CEOs to overlook product safety concerns in their decision making.
Indeed, a variety of stakeholders can be affected by a CEO’s response to pay (Werder, 2011), but the existing literature has largely focused on how CEO pay impacts shareholder wealth.
Our central hypothesis is that CEO stock options also affect consumer well-being by inducing behaviors that increase the likelihood of product safety problems, which in some cases pose life-threatening risks.
In 2012, for instance, a contaminated batch of steroid injections produced by the New England Compounding Center spurred an outbreak of fungal meningitis that killed more than 60 consumers and caused almost 700 others to become ill (CDC, 2013).
<!-- para 5 -->
We also provide a counterpoint to the common assumption that incentives have relatively uniform effects across executives.
The literature on executive pay has largely ignored the role that CEO characteristics play in shaping, or moderating, the effects of pay on firm outcomes.
Yet a wealth of research has shown that executives vary widely in their individual attributes in ways that affect their motivations and actions (for a review, see Finkelstein, Hambrick, and Cannella, 2009).
Scholars have proposed that executive characteristics and incentives operate in tandem to influence organizational outcomes (Wowak and Hambrick, 2010), but only a few empirical studies to date have explored this possibility (e.g., Cho and Hambrick, 2006; Sanders, 2001).
We build on the theoretical model put forth by Wowak and Hambrick (2010) to examine whether CEO tenure and founder status moderate the relationship between option pay and product safety problems.
Results from a study of 386 CEOs of public companies regulated by the U.S. Food and Drug Administration (FDA) from 2004 to 2011 provide broad support for our theory.
<!-- para 6 -->
Our study makes several contributions.
First, we illustrate that the consequences of CEO stock options can spread beyond shareholders to directly affect consumer well-being.
This builds on recent scholarship arguing that managerial opportunism can affect a variety of stakeholders besides shareholders (Werder, 2011), as our results suggest that the option-driven pursuit of upside gains can lead to unsafe products being released into the marketplace.
Second, ours is among the first studies to illustrate that CEOs vary in their responses to incentives.
By showing that options' effects are far from uniform across CEOs, we underscore the idea that studying executive characteristics and incentives in tandem, rather than separately, allows for improved predictions about the consequences of executive pay arrangements.
Finally, our study is one of the first to focus on the causes of product safety problems.
Somewhat surprisingly in light of the societal implications, the sources of product safety problems have received little attention in the literature (for rare exceptions, see Haunschild and Rhee, 2004; Thirumalai and Sinha, 2011).
We show that CEO incentives may play a role in causing these sometimes tragic events.

## theory
<!-- para 3 -->
A central tenet of agency theory is that CEO and shareholder risk preferences tend to diverge (Jensen and Meckling, 1976).
Unlike shareholders, who can diversify their holdings across numerous investments, a CEO typically has a very concentrated stake in their firm.
Much or all of a CEO's future income, as well as their reputation, is contingent upon continued employment as CEO (Milgrom and Roberts, 1992).
This naturally predisposes the CEO toward risk-averse strategies with low probabilities of failure.
Shareholders, on the other hand, are more risk-neutral and are mainly interested in maximizing returns.
Out of a belief that big returns tend to require that big risks be taken (Sharpe,
<!-- para 4 -->
1964), shareholders generally favor more risk taking than do CEOs.
Among the various prescriptions advocated by agency theorists to overcome this problem, the use of equity-based pay—and specifically stock options—to promote risk taking has been widely embraced by corporate boards (Hall and Liebman, 1998).
While their use has declined somewhat from its peak in 2001, when options comprised more than half of a typical CEO pay package, options still accounted for almost a quarter of CEO pay in 2010 (Hay, 2011).
<!-- para 5 -->
Despite the proliferation of options over the past 30 years, it is only in the last decade or so that scholars have begun investigating in detail how CEO options affect company strategies.
Studies have shown, for example, that abundant CEO stock option pay leads to increased acquisition activity (Sanders, 2001), larger investments in research and development (R&D) (Ryan and Wiggins, 2002), and decreased use of derivatives to hedge financial risk (Rajgopal and Shevlin, 2002).
These outcomes are natural outgrowths of the underlying purpose of stock options; namely, they all have the potential to generate big gains in the share price if the initiatives ultimately bear fruit.
In this regard, options have had their intended effect.
<!-- para 6 -->
A growing literature also suggests that options can cause CEOs to engage in behaviors that are exactly the opposite of what shareholders want, however.
For instance, several studies have found a positive relationship between CEO option pay and corporate earnings manipulation (e.g., Harris and Bromiley, 2007; Zhang et al., 2008).
And in a surprising recent finding, option pay was linked to higher investments in short-term (as opposed to long-term) capital assets (Souder and Bromiley, 2012), seemingly contradictory to what options should encourage.
These studies lend strength to the argument that options, which offer unlimited upside and no downside, encourage an excessive focus on chasing big wins and a corresponding lack of caution in preventing big losses.
We build on this logic in the next section, outlining why stock options may contribute to a lack of caution in the area of product safety.
<!-- para 8 -->
In explaining why option-loaded CEOs delivered more big losses than big gains, Sanders and Hambrick suggested that the asymmetric nature of options would “cause CEOs to not be attuned to early signs of project failure and generally careless about risk mitigation” (2007: 1073).
This underpins our main argument, which is that abundant stock option pay will increase the likelihood of product safety problems.
By encouraging CEOs to ignore downside risks, options set the stage for mistakes in the design, production, and distribution of products.
CEOs have influence over a wide array of initiatives that affect company operations, including cost-reduction programs (e.g., downsizing, outsourcing, etc.); process-oriented programs (e.g., quality control, capital investments, etc.); and reward systems for employees at all levels of an organization (Yukl, 2008).
Option-loaded CEOs thus have many avenues through which to convey their priorities to employees, who will make decisions with these priorities in mind.
<!-- para 9 -->
We posit that the option-induced lack of caution in CEOs is driven by two related cognitive processes.
First, the disproportionate focus on upside potential could cause CEOs to ignore or otherwise be unconcerned with potential product safety problems.
In this scenario, the CEO unconsciously overlooks the downside possibilities that could result from their aggressive decisions.
This is akin to carelessness, and is consistent with Sanders and Hambrick's (2007) argument that option-loaded CEOs will exhibit impatience, noncomprehensive decision making, and a general disinterest in preventing downside outcomes.
<!-- para 10 -->
Second, a related possibility is that option-loaded CEOs might endorse company initiatives that they know could increase the odds of product safety problems, which are viewed as an acceptable risk that accompanies the aggressive pursuit of share price appreciation.
A sufficiently large dose of stock options may cause CEOs to rationalize that the heightened risk of product safety problems is worth the potential payoff that the stock options offer (if no problems occur or are discovered, that is).
Here, the CEO may believe it is in the best interests of shareholders to take calculated risks in the domain of product safety.
It is also plausible that CEOs may do so out of a belief that even if problems occur, the market and/or government penalties will not be overly severe (Bromiley and Marcus, 1989).
<!-- para 11 -->
Both of these processes align with our core idea that stock options will play an influential role in promoting a lack of caution that leads to product safety problems.
Whether this manifests through carelessness or a conscious decision to ignore downside risks, the asymmetric nature of options will predispose CEOs toward aggressiveness over thoroughness in their decisions.
We expect that an option-loaded CEO's upside-focused mentality will manifest in firm-wide policies on staffing, quality control, product testing, employee bonus criteria, and so on.
As such:
<!-- para 12 -->
**Hypothesis 1: CEO stock option pay will positively influence the likelihood of subsequent product safety problems.**
<!-- para 14 -->
Although we anticipate a positive association between options and product safety problems, we also expect that this relationship will depend upon attributes of CEOs themselves.
Because CEOs vary widely in their experiences, values, and motives (Finkelstein et al., 2009), it logically follows that they will vary in their responses to incentives.
Indeed, depending on the outcomes that CEOs seek (e.g., money, acclaim, security, etc.), incentives may or may not induce any changes in behavior.
Yet the literature on executive pay has largely ignored this possibility.
Highlighting this oversight, Wowak and Hambrick (2010) proposed a model of person-pay interaction detailing how executive characteristics and incentives operate in tandem to influence organizational outcomes, an idea that received support in a recent experimental simulation (Chng et al., 2012).
We focus on two elements of CEO experience—tenure and founder status—that have been shown to influence CEOs' motives vis-à-vis financial rewards (Sanders, 2001; Wasserman, 2006).
<!-- para 15 -->
The first of these moderators—CEO tenure—is thought to shape how CEOs perceive and act upon their environments (Hambrick and Fukutomi, 1991; Miller, 1991).
Studies have highlighted a general tendency toward conservatism as executives advance in their tenures (e.g., Berger, Ofek, and Yermack, 1997) that we expect will somewhat dampen the behavioral effect of options.
Our prediction is that longer-tenured CEOs will be less likely to fixate on upside outcomes while ignoring potential downside risks, therein diminishing options' incentive properties.
As they approach the end of their careers, long-tenured CEOs have "much to lose and little to gain" (Sanders, 2001: 481) by aggressively pursuing the payouts offered by options.
On this point, Sanders (2001) found that the positive relationship between option pay and acquisition activity was significantly diminished as CEO tenure advanced.
We propose that a longer-tenured CEO's preference for conservatism will partially (if not wholly) offset the lack of caution brought on by abundant option pay.
Thus:
<!-- para 16 -->
**Hypothesis 2: The influence of CEO option pay on subsequent product safety problems will be weaker for longer-tenured CEOs than for shorter-tenured CEOs.**
<!-- para 17 -->
We also contend that the influence of option pay on product safety problems will vary depending on whether a CEO founded the firm.
Here, we draw on the logic of stewardship theory (Davis, Schoorman, and Donaldson, 1997) to posit that founder-CEOs are motivated primarily by an intrinsic need to see their firm succeed, rather than by the potential for large payouts such as those provided by stock options.
Nonfounder-CEOs, on the other hand, are thought to behave more in accord with agency theory predictions (Wasserman, 2006).
In particular, nonfounder-CEOs should be more strongly motivated by extrinsic rewards (e.g., options) and will pursue the outsized payoffs with vigor.
But for a founder-CEO, the temptation to aggressively pursue the rewards should be less pronounced; the desire to protect the organization is simply not compatible with a negligent approach to product safety.
Therefore:
<!-- para 18 -->
**Hypothesis 3: The influence of CEO option pay on subsequent product safety problems will be weaker for founder-CEOs than for nonfounder-CEOs.**

## methods
<!-- para 2 -->
To test our ideas, it was important to select a study context in which there exists a likelihood of, but heterogeneity in, product safety problems.
We therefore chose to draw our sample from the population of firms regulated by the FDA, whose primary responsibility is ensuring product safety across a variety of industries including foods, pharmaceuticals, and medical devices.
We focused on product recalls as indicators of product safety problems, as a recall is a clear sign that a firm's product poses a danger to consumer safety.
The firms in our sample thus faced similar regulatory environments, but exhibited substantial variance in both the independent and dependent variables (described in the next section).
<!-- para 3 -->
We began constructing our sample by hand-collecting historical annual lists of companies classified by the FDA as “significantly regulated” over the period 2004–2011. [^1] Because its employees are not permitted to hold financial interests in companies subject to its regulation, the FDA maintains detailed lists of all publicly traded companies that fall into this category.
We cross-referenced these annual lists with the Execucomp database, which contains executive pay data on a comprehensive set of large public firms in the United States.
We constrained the sample to include only the two broad industry sectors most actively regulated by the FDA: the consumer staples (e.g., foods, beverages, and personal care products) and health care (e.g., medical devices, pharmaceuticals, and life sciences) sectors, which together accounted for over 85 percent of all recall activity reported by the FDA over the time period studied.
The two sectors were defined as Global Industry Classification Standard (GICS) codes 30 and 35.
The calculation of CEO option pay (described below) was such that we needed at least two years of pay data for each CEO in our sample; also, we limited our sample to firms with sales and assets of at least 10 million.
Subsequent to these screens, our final sample comprised an unbalanced panel of 1,314 CEO-year observations across 386 CEOs over the period 2004–2011.
<!-- para 4 -->
Next, we gathered recall data using the weekly Enforcement Reports issued by the FDA to identify all Class 1 and Class 2 product recalls that occurred during our sample frame.
These two recall classes reflect instances where there exists a reasonable probability that a product will cause serious harm or death (Class 1) or where exposure to a product may cause temporary or medically reversible health consequences (Class 2).
Class 3 recalls, which reflect instances where exposure to a product is not likely to cause any health consequences, were not included in our sample.
Finally, we merged these data with company financial and performance data from COMPUSTAT and the Center for Research in Security Prices (CRSP).
We Winsorized all continuous variables at the one percent level to lessen the influence of extreme observations (Dixon, 1960) and inflation-adjusted all financial and pay measures to 2010 dollars using the U.S. Consumer Price Index.
<!-- para 6 -->
To ensure robustness, we tested our hypotheses on two distinct measures of product recall activity.
The first dependent variable (DV), likelihood of a product recall, was a binary (1/0) measure of whether a company experienced a recall in year t.
The second DV, number of product recalls, was a count of recalls in year t.
This variable ranged from 0 to 27 in our sample.
<!-- para 8 -->
We measured CEO stock option pay as the proportion of total pay comprised of stock options.
This variable represented the ex ante value of options granted (as reported by Execucomp) divided by total compensation (measured as TDC1 in Execucomp) in the same year. [^2] Following prior research (Sanders and Hambrick, 2007), we calculated this as a two-year average  $(t-1$ and  $t-2)$ to reflect the reality that companies may grant options periodically rather than annually and also the fact that options are meant to motivate behavior over multiple years.
<!-- para 10 -->
Unless otherwise noted, all control variables were lagged by one year.
First, we included lagged DVs in all models to account for the possibility that companies with a history of product recalls may be more likely to subsequently experience problems (e.g., Rhee, 2009).
We controlled for company size (number of employees, natural log-transformed to correct for skew), company age (years since inclusion in CRSP, natural log-transformed), R&D intensity (R&D expense/sales), diversification (calculated using the Palepu (1985) entropy measure), recent market performance (total shareholder return [TSR], calculated as the 12-month compounded return), recent accounting performance (return on assets [ROA], calculated as net income divided by total assets), and financial distress (measured as Altman's Z (Altman, 1968)).
Industry munificence and industry dynamism were calculated following Dess and Beard (1984).
We also controlled for time-invariant industry effects by including five industry sector dummies based on four-digit GICS codes.
CEO tenure was measured as the number of years since becoming CEO (natural log-transformed to correct for skew).
CEO founder status was a binary indicator of whether the CEO was listed as the founder of the firm in S&P Capital IQ.
CEO functional background was a binary indicator of whether the CEO's main functional background was in a "throughput" (vs. "output") function (e.g., Hambrick and Mason, 1984).
CEO duality was a binary indicator of whether the CEO served as board chair in t-1.
CEO stock ownership was measured as the percentage of outstanding shares owned by the CEO (natural log-transformed), while CEO exercisable option value was the value of exercisable in-the-money options in t-1 (natural log-transformed).
We also controlled for CEO total other pay (non-option compensation in t-1, natural log-transformed).
Year dummy variables were included in all models.
<!-- para 12 -->
To accommodate the non-independent nature of our longitudinal sample, we analyzed our data using generalized estimating equations (GEEs).
GEEs help account for unobserved differences across CEOs as well as intertemporal correlations among outcome variables for individual CEOs (Liang and Zeger, 1986), and have been used in prior studies of product recalls over time (e.g., Rhee, 2009).
GEEs require specifying the distribution of the DV, the link function, and the intertemporal correlation structure.
For tests involving the binary DV, we specified a binomial distribution with a logit link function; and for tests involving the count DV, we specified a negative binomial distribution with a log link function.
In all models we specified an exchangeable correlation structure (grouped by CEO) and robust standard errors, which account for any misspecification in the correlation structure (Hardin and Hilbe, 2003).

## results
<!-- para 3 -->
Although we did not expect endogeneity to influence our results, we examined whether certain conditions might influence both the occurrence of product recalls and CEO option pay.
As a robustness test, we ran two-stage models that accounted for this possibility.
Following Wiersema and Zhang (2011), we created a first-stage model regressing CEO option pay proportion in t-1 and t-2 on a set of lagged predictors known to influence option pay (our model was largely identical to the one used by Sanders and Hambrick (2007) for a similar purpose): company size in t-3, prior performance (TSR in t-3 and ROA in t-3), R&D intensity in t-3, CEO stock ownership in t-3, CEO age in t-3, and dummies for industry and year.
As expected, firm size, performance, and R&D intensity were all significant positive predictors of option pay; stock ownership and age were negative and significant.
The industry and year dummies were also significant.
We then used the residuals from this model as our revised CEO option pay measure, which yielded qualitatively similar results to the models reported below using the raw measure.

In Hypothesis 3, we posited that the effect of options would be weaker for founder-CEOs.
The significant negative coefficient on the founder interaction term in Model 4 (p<0.01) and Model 9 (p<0.01), as well as in the full Model 5 (p<0.05) and Model 10 (p<0.05), indicates that the link between option pay and recalls was more negative for founders than for nonfounders.
Here, too, we calculated marginal effects.
For the binary model, our analysis showed a significant positive effect of option pay for nonfounders (dy/dx = 0.20, p<0.01) but no effect for founders (dy/dx = -0.10, n.s.), who comprised 12 percent of our sample.

Hypothesis 3 was thus supported.

## discussion
<!-- para 2 -->
Our aim in this study was to explore the idea that stock options promote a lack of caution in CEOs that manifests in an increased incidence of product safety problems.
Prior research has demonstrated that paying CEOs with stock options can result in outcomes that are exactly the opposite of what shareholders want, but ours is the first study of which we are aware to show that options can lead to product safety problems that threaten consumer well-being.
Our primary contribution is in demonstrating that stock options may cause CEOs to overlook or otherwise de-prioritize the internal practices that go into making a safe, high-quality product.
Our study thus underscores the idea that managerial opportunism can affect a variety of organizational stakeholders (e.g., Werder, 2011).
In moving beyond the usual focus on shareholders, we demonstrate the potential in studying the effects of executive incentives on a broader array of firm constituents.
<!-- para 3 -->
An example from our dataset helps place this idea in context.
In the years following the famous Tylenol recall of 1982, during which seven people died after ingesting poisoned capsules, Johnson & Johnson (J&J) developed a strong reputation for prioritizing product safety above all else—including quarterly earnings (Deutsch, 1988).
The company flourished over the next two decades, rarely experiencing product safety problems.
But things began to change in 2002 when the company hired William Weldon as CEO.
Weldon, who received 56 percent of his pay in stock options (which is approximately 1 standard deviation above the mean in our sample) over the first four years of his tenure, embarked on a multiyear cost-cutting initiative that subsequently increased the company's operating margin to 26 percent by 2006 (compared to an average of 20% during his predecessor Ralph Larsen's tenure).
With less financial emphasis on quality control, product quality began to suffer, and J&J found itself recalling an increasing number of products because of safety concerns.
This excerpt from a 2010 Fortune investigative report describes the shift in attitudes toward product quality:
<!-- para 4 -->
Then, not long after Larsen retired in 2002, it began to slowly weaken.
The culprit was a familiar one—cost cutting—but in a subtler form.
There were no wholesale layoffs in quality control.
Instead, experienced staffers were repeatedly laid off and replaced with newbies who mostly lacked technical pharmaceutical experience.
By 2008 the analytical laboratory, formerly staffed almost entirely by full-time scientists, was half-full of contract workers, according to a former manager there.
Once stricter than a schoolmarm, the department grew lax.
The team that tested the production lines was dubbed the 'EZ Pass system,' according to a former quality-control employee ...
'The whole dynamic was very stressful,' she adds.
'It wasn't Do your job the right way, it was Do your job fast [italics in original].
Make it look good, and get it done as fast as possible.' (Kimes, 2010)
<!-- para 5 -->
All told, J&J experienced more than 70 product recalls from 2007 through 2011, and the ongoing stumbles contributed to Weldon stepping down as CEO in 2012.
Could high levels of option pay have played a role in the problems experienced by the company?
Our study lends support to this possibility.
<!-- para 6 -->
We also build on the idea that the consequences of incentive pay depend greatly on the characteristics of the executive to whom the pay is granted (Wowak and Hambrick, 2010).
Our results reinforce the notion that executive pay and executive characteristics should be studied in tandem, rather than separately, as the effects of pay are far from uniform across executives.
We specifically show that the influence of option pay on product recalls becomes less pronounced as CEO tenure advances.
Similarly, we show that the effect of options depends on whether the CEO founded the firm.
These findings suggest that individual motives can importantly influence how a given executive will respond to incentive compensation.
<!-- para 7 -->
Our study was not without limitations.
In particular, we were unable to examine the internal mechanisms through which the CEOs' lack of caution manifested in their firms.
We proposed several possibilities, such as process controls, quality standards, and employee bonuses, but future research could provide insight by investigating the mediating mechanisms through which executive motives are transmitted to the rest of the organization.
Additionally, more research is needed to shed light on whether our results generalize to different contexts.
For instance, the option-induced lack of caution in CEOs may manifest in insufficient due diligence when engaging in mergers and acquisitions; less emphasis on employee development; and lower investments in information security and data privacy.
Finally, we examined only two of the many individual attributes that could influence executive responses to pay.
CEOs' personalities, values, experiences, and abilities will all affect how they respond to incentives, and the idea of person-pay interactions remains a wide open area for future strategic management research.
