# wowak_2020_the_influence_of_female_directors_on_product_reca 句子库存

## introduction
<!-- para 2 -->
On September 30, 2018, California became the first state to require all public companies headquartered in the state to have at least one female director on their board (Fuhrmans 2018).
The European Commission goes further by mandating that European companies have at least 40% of their corporate board members be female (Zillman 2017).
Academic research and the popular press have long been curious about the impact of having female directors on historically male-dominated boards (Adams and Funk 2012, Post and Byron 2015, Stellings 2015); most of this curiosity has centered on the financial or strategic benefits to the firm or its stakeholders (Stellings 2015).
Research on how women on boards might affect a firm's operational decisions has explored employee layoffs and financial operating performance (Ahern and Dittmar 2012, Matsa and Miller 2013, Levi et al. 2014) but has not addressed, as we do here, operational decisions related to product quality problems, some of which have the potential to harm customers.
However, it is reasonable to expect that if female directors affect firm financial or strategic performance, they may also influence critical operational decisions dealing with product quality.
This influence may be especially prevalent in highly regulated industries, such as in the medical products industry regulated by the U.S. Food and Drug Administration (FDA) and when the decision can have an impact on customer safety, such as a recall decision for a defective medical product.
Examples of such products include prescription and over-the-counter pharmaceuticals, medical devices such as cardiac stents and hip implants, and biologics used to treat cancer.
In this study, we examine how adding female directors to a firm's board influences recall decisions in the FDA-regulated medical products industry.
<!-- para 3 -->
Central to our theorizing is that boards are specifically established to set the tone for how managers make critical firm decisions (Westphal 1999, Burns 2003), of which recall decisions are one important example.
Exploring how board-level variation influences manager-level decision making, as we do, is consistent with studies that explore how board guidance impacts managerial decisions such as acquisition decisions (Kroll et al. 2008), resource allocation decisions (Haynes and Hillman 2010), and firm growth decisions (Kor and Sundaramurthy 2009).
Although no academic studies, to our knowledge, have investigated whether or how boards influence recall decisions, anecdotal examples indicate that they may.
One prominent example is Merck's decision to recall its popular drug, Vioxx.
Merck's drug safety studies pointed to a heightened risk of cardiovascular diseases from Vioxx.
Although Merck could have simply added label warnings to keep the drug on the market, they instead recalled the drug to protect customers, though it cost the company $2.5 billion in lost revenue (Gilmartin 2011).
According to the former head of Merck, Raymond Gilmartin, this decision was heavily influenced by Merck's board of directors, who established clear expectations for managers regarding how, and how quickly, to recall defective products when the need to do so arises (Gilmartin 2011).
<!-- para 4 -->
Our hypothesizing that an increase in female directors influences how managers make recall decisions is grounded in scholarship demonstrating that female directors bring a sharper focus on rule-following in decisions (Muller-Kahle and Lewellyn 2011, Portillo 2012), such as FDA regulations dictating when medical product recalls are warranted, and a broader concern for all stakeholders (Jaffee and Hyde 2000), including a heightened awareness of customers who may be harmed by defective products.
Consequently, an increase in female directors on a firm's board may alter the tone and expectations for how managers make recall decisions.
Thus, our overarching research question is: Does adding female directors influence product recall decisions?
<!-- para 5 -->
Although we study how adding female directors is associated with recall decisions, we a priori contend that this relationship is fully contingent upon the severity of the underlying product defect being recalled.
In particular, we do not theorize that adding female directors should influence all types of recalls equivalently; doing so might lead to the false implication that adding female directors worsens product quality.
Instead, we theorize that adding female directors affects recalls contingent upon recall severity.
The FDA classifies recalls into three categories of severity: class 1 (high severity), class 2 (moderate severity), and class 3 (low severity).
Examples of medical product recalls in popular press (Zajac 2010, Latson 2014, Embry 2016) and in academic research (Thirumalai and Sinha 2011) have demonstrated that class 1 recalls, which are associated with life-threatening product defects, offer managerial discretion regarding when these recalls are announced, but not if they are announced.
In other words, although firms may at times drag their feet in taking action on severe problems, the inherent life-threatening nature of these types of defects creates a setting in which the firm will be required to eventually recall (Mukherjee and Sinha 2017).
Conversely, class 3 recalls, which are associated with nonharmful issues such as labeling or packaging nonconformities, have significant discretion in whether they are ever initiated by firms and represent an ideal context to examine how changes in board gender composition influence the firm's tendency to either take accountability for, or overlook, product quality problems that contain significant initiation discretion.
We leverage this difference in severity and how it may influence recall timing and initiation by arguing that female directors may have alternative effects on disparate recall classes.
For class 1 recalls, where the need to recall is quite apparent, the broad and responsive focus on stakeholders that female directors bring to the board may lead firms with more female directors to take faster action in removing those defective products from the market and, hence, make faster class 1 recall decisions.
For class 3 recalls, which by definition have no impact on customer health, the stricter rule-following focus that female directors bring to the board may lead firms with more female directors to decide to recall even when there is a reasonable chance of successfully hiding the problem from the public and regulators, as there is for this class of defects (FDA 2019).
<!-- para 6 -->
Our primary means of examining the influence of female directors on recalls is the proportion of board members that are female, commonly referred to as female board representation (FBR).
We use this measure in our main econometric tests, because it is one of the most commonly used means to examine the influence of female directors on firm outcomes (Post and Byron 2015) and because there is a previously developed instrumental variable for FBR that we leverage to examine potential effects of endogeneity in our models (Adams and Ferreira 2009).
We analyze 4,271 medical product recalls across 92 firms from 2002 to 2013 with negative binomial fixed effect (FE) and ordinary least squares (OLS) FE regression models.
We have two dependent variables: recall count and time-to-recall.
The former is well established in the literature (Haunschild and Rhee 2004, Thirumalai and Sinha 2011, Wowak et al. 2015, Mukherjee and Sinha 2017, Shah et al. 2017).
Using it allows us to examine how adding female directors influences future recall counts at the firm for recalls that may be easier to hide and not announce when the opportunity arises, as in the case of class 3 recalls.
The latter—time-to-recall—is an objective measure of recall decision-making timeliness that has not been made available by the FDA or used in prior academic research to our knowledge.
Specifically, we obtained proprietary dates for all medical product recalls in our panel from the FDA for the purpose of explaining firm-level variation in recall timing.
These time-stamped data include the date the firm first became aware of the defect and the date the firm chose to voluntarily recall the defective product.
The comparison of these dates allows us to calculate a recall decision-making time measure.
<!-- para 7 -->
Because we use FE models, we study how increasing female directors, as measured by an increase in FBR, within a firm across time influences changes in our recall outcome measures.
We find that a one standard deviation increase in FBR, or a 10% increase, is associated with a subsequent 30% increase in the number of class 3, low-severity recalls a firm initiates, equating to three additional recalls of that type.
We theorize that this is because female directors set a tone for managers to closely follow rules while making recall decisions, and thus, managers will decide to recall even when there is an opportunity to not do so, as is the case with class 3 recalls.
Further, a one standard deviation increase in FBR is also associated with a 20% decrease in the time-to-recall for class 1, high-severity recalls, or a 16-day acceleration in removing the most dangerous products from the market.
We theorize that this is due to the heightened concern for stakeholders that female directors bring to bear on managerial decisions, which should be most prevalent for recalls that are associated with serious injury and customer death, as is the case with class 1 recalls.
To enhance the interpretability of our results, we repeat our analyses using two alternative measures for female directors: a female director dichotomous measure and the actual count of female directors.
Using the dichotomous measure, we find that, when a firm moves from having no female directors to one or more female directors, class 3 recalls increase 120%, equating to 12 additional class 3 recalls.
Relatedly, changing from no female directors to one or more speeds up the time-to-recall for class 1 recalls by 35%, or a 28-day acceleration in recalling the most dangerous defects from the market.
The analysis using the actual count of female directors is consistent with the female dichotomous analysis and the FBR analysis with one exception.
A minimum of two female directors may be required to speed up class 1 recall decisions.
This finding is informative, as moving faster to recall the most severe and dangerous defects represents both a significant benefit to society but also a great risk for firms, as these recalls expose firms to the greatest public and regulatory scrutiny.
In other words, one lone female on the board may not be sufficient to push firms to make these difficult choices in a more timely manner.
<!-- para 8 -->
We address identification issues in robustness checks in three ways.
We first include results of an instrumental variable (IV) analysis that leverages a previously established IV for FBR.
The IV analysis leads to consistent results.
We then report results of a sample selection model that controls for selection bias that may explain both the decision to appoint more female directors and how the firm makes recall decisions.
Results hold here as well.
We also include results of a reverse causality regression that examines if past recalls at the firm explain the appointment of female directors.
Our results suggest they do not.
<!-- para 9 -->
In addition, we conduct a robustness check to examine the appropriateness of our level of analysis.
In particular, top managers rather than directors may set the tone for how recall decisions are made.
If so, the gender of the vice president (VP) of quality, as opposed to the gender of the directors, may be a more appropriate measure for our study.
We test if the gender of the VP of quality predicts recall decisions and if it serves as a mediator in our model such that FBR influences recall decisions through the gender of the VP of quality.
We find no such evidence.
In fact, the gender of the VP of quality has no significant relationship with either of our recall outcome measures, substantiating our contention that the board of directors, and not managers, set the tone for recall decision-making.
This contention is consistent with other board-level studies examining managerial level-decisions (Kroll et al. 2008, Kor and Sundaramurthy 2009).
<!-- para 10 -->
We conclude the study with a discussion on how our findings associating board gender diversity and operational decisions may be able to influence board appointment initiatives by firms.
We also extend these findings to the broader domain of operational decisions and propose that adding female directors may help firms become more sensitized to the societal impact of operational decisions in other areas such as inventory decisions, facility location decisions, and humanitarian logistics decisions.
<!-- para 12 -->
To articulate our hypothesized relationships, we first explain how medical product recall decisions are made.
By doing so, we explain how the board is involved in, and sets the tone for, if and how quickly managers recall defective products.
To acquire this contextual knowledge, we (1) studied literature on the recall process (Thirumalai and Sinha 2011, Mukherjee and Sinha 2017, Ball et al. 2018); (2) examined FDA documents on how firms are expected to make recall decisions (FDA 2014); (3) interviewed two managers—a VP of quality and a director of manufacturing—at two FDA-regulated Fortune 500 medical product firms; and (4) interviewed a senior director at the FDA tasked with overseeing firm recall decisions.
The following description of the recall decision-making process integrates information from these four sources.
<!-- para 13 -->
Product quality engineers continuously monitor quality issues that may signal a systemic problem requiring a recall.
Information sources monitored include internal operations performance measures such as product testing and inspection results as well as customer or regulator complaints external to the firm.
When a systemic issue is identified, a product quality manager convenes a managerial recall committee meeting.
These committees typically consist of senior directors or managers from manufacturing, research and development (R&D), quality, legal, clinical, and regulatory functional areas.
These committees are tasked with discussing whether a recall is needed and subsequently making a recommendation to the appropriate VP who ultimately makes the recall decision—usually the VP of quality.
On a recurring basis, the VP of quality reviews recall decisions, both those that were initiated and those that were not, with the board of directors so the board can provide feedback and calibration on how managers are making recall decisions.
In other words, boards do not make the recall decisions, but instead they set the tone and expectations for how managers are to make these decisions.
We illustrate this point further with anecdotal evidence obtained via interviews with our industry informants below.
<!-- para 14 -->
The VP of quality mentioned that, at her firm, the recall decision focuses keenly on customer harm and that managerial recall committees are only allowed three days to prove that a recall is not warranted once a product quality issue comes to their attention.
If unable to do so in three days, a recall is initiated.
The default at this firm is to recall and to do so quickly.
This expectation for quick and deliberate action prioritizing customer safety was established by the firm's board.
In fact, it is the female directors on the board at this firm who are particularly concerned with customer safety.
The VP of quality shared with us that, when she reviews recall decisions with the board, female directors in particular are quite vocal and press her for information on the extent of customer harm that is associated with the problematic medical product.
This line of questioning sets the tone for how recall decisions are made at this firm.
<!-- para 15 -->
Conversely, the manufacturing director we spoke with indicated that, at his firm, the default is the opposite.
There, managerial recall committees have the burden of proving that a recall is absolutely necessary, and if unable to do so, no recall is initiated.
Recall committees can take as long as they deem appropriate, and the deliberations center on cost-benefit analyses more than customer safety.
This cost-benefit prioritization is driven by the board.
In this firm, the male directors often inquire about who is going to be fired and how quickly they will be fired following a recall announcement.
The risk of being fired for a recall is not lost on the managers who make recall recommendations or decisions; it sets the tone for and influences their view on how these decisions are made.
In fact, in one specific case we were made aware of, a manufacturing manager had his board-approved annual bonus, with a potential of reaching $30,000 a year (approximately 25% of his annual compensation), tied to an objective of announcing no recalls for his product line that year.
In other words, the tone set by this board is to avoid recalls or delay them as long as possible.
<!-- para 16 -->
Finally, our interview with a senior director at the FDA validated that divergence in the recall decision-making process as indicated above is very common.
Firms are provided significant leeway in recall decisions for two reasons: (1) the FDA has limited human resources to oversee all potential quality problems; and (2) the FDA lacks the same depth of firm-proprietary product-level failure data and resultant insight regarding whether or when a recall is warranted.
Because of these factors, the FDA relies on firms to recall when and how they deem appropriate (FDA 2018), and firms vary in their approaches about how they handle these operational product quality decisions.

## theory
<!-- para 3 -->
Recalls differ in severity, which leads to variation in if and when firms initiate recalls.
This distinction underlies our hypotheses, as we contend that adding female directors will have a varying influence on recall decisions contingent upon the severity of product defects.
We begin with low-severity defects requiring a class 3 recall.
This type of defect can be hidden and not recalled by the firm if the firm opts for such an approach.
Consequently, we treat class 3 recall counts as a measurement of managerial discretion in product quality decisions and not as a measure of product quality per se.
We find support for conceptualizing class 3 recalls in this manner from our discussions with the FDA.
FDA individuals we spoke with for this study indicated that the FDA views an increase in class 3 recalls as stricter, more risk-averse rule-following in recall decisions and not necessarily as a degradation of product quality.
<!-- para 4 -->
To illustrate the amount of discretion present in whether to initiate a class 3 recall, we provide an example of a recall that was initiated by a firm and then an example of a defective product that was not recalled by another firm, though it should have been according to FDA guidelines.
In May 2006, Boston Scientific initiated a recall on one of its drug-coated stents because the instruction manual was accidentally omitted from the package (FDA 2006).
The FDA assigned it a class 3 designation because the FDA determined that omission of the manual would not harm patients.
However, Boston Scientific recalled the stents regardless because a recall was warranted according to FDA rules.
It is unlikely that the FDA, or even physicians using the product on patients, would have been made aware of this defect had it not been recalled.
In our discussions with industry managers, we learned that physicians rarely use the manuals included in such products because they are highly trained and already know how to use them.
<!-- para 5 -->
Next, we offer an example provided by a manufacturing manager at a different firm.
It involved a product labeling mistake that was not recalled, though it should have been according to FDA recall guidelines.
His firm, whose name must be kept confidential, manufactures several medical products including drugs, biologics, and medical devices.
In one instance, a quality engineer identified a marking on a product's box that indicated an incorrect size of the product inside the box.
However, inside the box, the product included a secondary label that had the correct size.
This warranted a recall according to FDA rules and would have been a class 3 recall if initiated.
However, the firm did not recall the affected products and instead simply fixed the box information on future lots of the product.
This illustrates that, in cases of low-severity defects, there is discretion regarding whether to initiate a recall.
<!-- para 6 -->
We theorize that firms with more female directors will be more likely to initiate recalls for these low-severity issues.
This expectation is grounded in research on gender differences in rule-following behavior.
Research dating back to the 1950s finds that women are more likely to follow rules and "go by the book" than their male colleagues (Green and Melnick 1950, Lee et al. 2017).
This is especially true at higher organizational levels (Portillo and DeHart-Davis 2009, Gupta et al. 2019) and in the application of ethical standards (Franke et al. 1997, Pan and Sparks 2012, Gupta et al. 2019), suggesting that boards with more female directors create stricter rule-following expectations for managerial decisions (Lee et al. 2017).
Male directors, on the other hand, are more likely to encourage managers to bend rules and not report rule-breaking violations, especially if there is a possibility that the outcome may benefit the company (Tibbetts 1999, Veiga et al. 2004, Short et al. 2016).
Compared with their male counterparts, females also tend to view the negative consequences of risky behaviors, which include breaking or bending rules, to be both more likely to occur and more severe (Darley and Smith 1995, Harris et al. 2006).
This difference in rule-following tendencies may be especially prevalent when females are in the minority (Kanter 1977, Sekaquaptewa and Thompson 2003, Chang et al. 2019), as they are on every board in our sample, and under conditions of high uncertainty, such as those created by product defects (Olsen and Cox 2001), because under these conditions, female directors tend to receive more blame than male directors for poor firm outcomes (Park and Westphal 2013).
Taken together, these arguments bring us to the following hypothesis:
<!-- para 7 -->
$H_{1}$: An increase in female board representation is positively associated with the count of low-severity recalls.
<!-- para 9 -->
We now transition to high-severity recalls that have little discretion in whether they are initiated, but nonetheless contain discretion in regard to when they are initiated.
In these cases, customers' well-being—and often their lives—are at stake though there still remains variation in how quickly firms decide to recall.
This is evident from many examples in the popular press of firms taking what seems to be an excessive amount of time to recall highly hazardous products (Zajac 2010, Latson 2014, Embry 2016).
We provide two such medical product examples below.
<!-- para 10 -->
In August 2017, St. Jude Medical recalled several lots of implantable cardiac pacing defibrillators due to faulty batteries (FDA 2017).
If these devices wear out prematurely, they cease delivering life-saving heart pacing and the patient will quickly die.
In another serious case, in April 2019, Pfizer recalled a product after realizing that glass particles were present in one of their injectable prescription drugs (Tumolo 2019).
One does not require a medical degree to appreciate that injecting glass into a sick patient's artery is serious and life-threatening.
In both cases, the firm's decision is less about if a recall is necessary and more about how quickly the firm should act.
This is because in many cases, these two examples included, the firm discovers a defect before the FDA or customers.
Consequently, the firm has discretion in regard to how quickly to recall.
It can either move quickly and decisively or delay the decision.
In cases that are most severe, ironically, it is difficult for firms to recall quickly, because doing so exposes them to the most significant financial and customer-loyalty ramifications (Gilmartin 2011, Shah et al. 2017).
Because this creates a dilemma for firms attempting to balance customer safety with serious negative financial ramifications, we contend that variation in how quickly firms respond to such high-severity product quality defects is driven by the tone set by the board and, in particular, by female directors.
<!-- para 11 -->
We propose that, as a firm adds more female directors to its board, managers will make recall decisions for high-severity problems more quickly.
In particular, we theorize that female directors influence the tone set by the board to be more responsive to a diverse set of stakeholders that includes at-risk customers, particularly when customers could be severely harmed or even die from the product quality issue.
This is because female directors, on average, tend to be community influencers such as politicians, university representatives, and other social leaders (Hillman et al. 2002, Singh et al. 2008).
Female directors also typically have more experience with philanthropy and community service outreach (Groysberg and Bell 2013), positioning them to have a broader stakeholder viewpoint of how the firm's products help or harm society.
Female board members also exhibit more care orientation, which includes a responsibility not to harm in their reasoning and decision-making, whereas male directors focus more keenly on profits (Gilligan 1982, Jaffee and Hyde 2000).
Women are also more likely than men to be more aware and considerate of the needs of others, which may make them particularly sensitive to decisions that can lead to individual or societal harm (Nielsen and Huse 2010, Byron and Post 2016).
Finally, female directors may judge the probability of fallout from waiting too long as higher than male board members do (Darley and Smith 1995, Harris et al. 2006).
Combining these arguments leads us to our second hypothesis:
<!-- para 12 -->
$H_{2}$: An increase in female board representation is negatively associated with the time-to-recall for high-severity recalls.

## methods
<!-- para 3 -->
We collected data from all publicly traded medical product firms regulated by the FDA who manufacture some or all of the following types of products: diagnostic biologics, pharmaceuticals, medical devices, and radiation-emitting products.
We focused on medical product firms because these firms frequently experience defects that have significant health and safety effects on customers.
Additionally, although the FDA has legal authority to mandate recalls, they seldom do so and did not do so for the firms and years we examined.
<!-- para 4 -->
We collected recall data in two ways.
Recall counts were collected via a Freedom of Information Act (FOIA) request, whereas recall timing was provided by senior FDA leaders.
In combination, these recall data include the date the firm was first made aware of the defect, the date the recall was initiated, the firm responsible for the recall, and the recall classification.
The FDA categorizes recalls into three classes of severity.
Class 1 recalls involve defective products with a high risk of serious customer harm or even death.
Class 2 recalls reflect moderate harm potential such as temporary or reversible adverse health effects.
Class 3 recalls are related to defects not associated with adverse health consequences but still requiring recall (FDA 2009).
Board composition data were obtained from Institutional Shareholder Services (ISS), which provides information on a firm's board composition as of the firm's annual shareholder meeting.
Financial control variables were obtained from Compustat.
The intersection of these three datasets led to a sample of 835 firm-year recall observations from 2002 to 2013 that spanned all 92 publicly traded medical product firms that existed during our focal years.
<!-- para 7 -->
4.2.1.1.
Recall Count.
Consistent with past scholarship, one of our dependent variables is the count of recalls initiated by a firm (Haunschild and Rhee 2004, Thirumalai and Sinha 2011, Wowak et al. 2015, Shah et al. 2017, Ball et al. 2017).
Recall count is the number of recalls initiated by a firm in the 12 months following the annual shareholder meeting, because this is when board composition is determined for the upcoming year.
This approach measures the influence of board composition on recalls initiated until the next annual shareholder meeting.
<!-- para 8 -->
4.2.1.2.
Time-to-Recall.
We measure time-to-recall as the number of days between when the firm was first made aware of the defect and when the firm decided to recall the defective product.
Because this variable was not normally distributed, we used the natural log.
A positive beta coefficient for time-to-recall indicates more time, or a slower recall decision, whereas a negative beta coefficient indicates less time, or a faster recall decision.
<!-- para 9 -->
To test our hypotheses, we distinguish our dependent variables by recall severity.
To do so, we created subgroups of the dependent variable.
This approach is similar to other recall studies that segregate the dependent variable to examine the influence of recall severity (Thirumalai and Sinha 2011, Kini et al. 2016).
We test all classes of recalls for completeness.
Support for Hypothesis 1 would be indicated by a positive and significant beta coefficient for FBR on class 3 recalls, whereas support for Hypothesis 2 would be indicated by a negative and significant beta coefficient for FBR on class 1 time-to-recall.
<!-- para 11 -->
4.2.2.1.
Female Board Representation (FBR).
FBR is the percent of board members who are female at each firm's annual shareholder meeting; FBR ranges from 0% to 45% across our sample with an average of 14%.
This is consistent with the average FBR across companies in general during our focal time period (Stuart 2012).
The number of female board members is the numerator of this measure, and the number of total board members is the denominator; we use their ratio and thus neither measure is directly in the model.
The mean number of female board members is 1.4, with a minimum of zero, a maximum of 5.0, and a standard deviation of 1.0.
The mean total number of board members is 9.9, with a minimum of 5.0, a maximum of 18.0, and a standard deviation of 2.3.
In Figure 1, we summarize the within-firm variation of FBR across time in our study.
The y-axis represents the average within-firm percentage change in FBR for each year, and the x-axis is the years we examine.
Figure 1 shows that FBR experiences fluctuation within firms across time, which is pivotal for our examination of how within-firm changes in FBR influence future recall decisions.
<!-- para 13 -->
4.2.3.1.
Year.
We control for year because FDA policies, which change across time, may influence the number of recalls a firm announces.
We incorporate year FEs and treat 2013 as the reference category.
<!-- para 14 -->
4.2.3.2.
Sales.
A firm’s sales may also influence recalls (Thirumalai and Sinha 2011); thus, we include the natural log of firm sales in each year as a control (Ln sales).
<!-- para 15 -->
4.2.3.3.
Number of Products.
We control for the number of products that the firm had on the market in a given year (Mukherjee and Sinha 2017).
This is one means to control for how many recall opportunities a firm faces each year.
We use the natural log of this measure in our regression analysis (Ln products).
We obtained this measure directly from the FDA for the purposes of this study.
<!-- para 16 -->
4.2.3.4.
R&D Intensity.
A secondary means to control for recall opportunities is a firm's R&D intensity (R&D divided by sales) as this measure has been shown to predict medical product recalls (Thirumalai and Sinha 2011).
<!-- para 19 -->
4.2.3.6.
Tenure.
When board members have been on a firm's board for an extended period of time, the firm may make recall decisions differently than if board members are new.
Similar to other board studies, to control for tenure, we collected the number of years that each board member has been on the board of the firm and averaged this value for all members of the firm's board within each year (Beasley 1996).
<!-- para 20 -->
4.2.3.7.
Board Independence.
Board independence is the percentage of board members that are independent in that they are not employed by the firm.
Board independence is a common control variable in board composition research (Gupta and Wowak 2017) and a predictor of corporate misconduct (Neville et al. 2019).
<!-- para 21 -->
4.2.3.8.
CEO-Chair Separation.
The CEO is sometimes also the chair of the board.
When the CEO and chair are different individuals, the board may exercise more oversight and engage in more monitoring, which may change the tone for how managers make recall decisions.
We use a dummy variable to measure CEO-chair separation, with a one signifying separation and a zero signifying that the CEO and the chair are the same individual (Kim and Lu 2017).
We also sought to control for CEO gender, but only two companies had female CEOs in our data set, so there was not sufficient variation in this measure.
All independent and control variables are centered and standardized for enhanced interpretability.
<!-- para 23 -->
We have two dependent variables: Recall count and Time-to-recall.
Recall count is an over-dispersed count variable, and thus, we estimate this model using a negative binomial regression model (Wooldridge 2010).
Time-to-recall is a skewed measure of time, and thus, after taking the natural log of it, we use an OLS regression model to predict time-to-recall.
In both analyses, because our data contain repeated measures across time for the same firm, we use a FE demeaning regression approach.
Panelized data enable demeaning, which reduces time-invariant omitted variable bias leading to endogeneity.
Hence, we aggregate our measures at the firm-year level and test our relationships using a negative binomial FE panelized model for recall count and an OLS FE model for time-to-recall (Haunschild and Rhee 2004, Thirumalai and Sinha 2011).
Factors that do not change across time for a given firm, such as a firm indicator measure, are accounted for by design in such models and are thus not included.
Because we use an FE model, we are conducting a within-firm analysis, which influences how we interpret coefficients.
The regression coefficients in our results are interpreted as the effect of increasing FBR, within a firm, across time, on changes in future recall measures within a firm, across time.
In other words, our model, by design, examines how changing the female board composition within a firm influences how that firm makes subsequent recall decisions.

In presenting our regression results, we first analyze recall count in Table 3 and then explore time-to-recall in Table 4.
Table 3 displays the results of our negative binomial FE model predicting recall count.

We conducted five robustness checks to more deeply examine the relationship between female directors and recall decisions.
These include three tests that explore potential endogeneity bias, one that examines the level of analysis of our study, and one that uses alternatives to our FBR measure.
<!-- para 27 -->
A commonly used means to examine IV relevance is to report a Cragg-Donald Wald F-statistic (Briscoe and Rogan 2015, Bavafa et al. 2018), which is a “weak identification” test statistic, and compare it to the 10% critical value of Stock and Yogo (2005).
The critical value we are required to use is 16.38, which comes from table 5.2 in Stock and Yogo (2005) as we have one IV for one potentially exogenous regressor.
This means that, to have no more than 10% of the bias of the regression estimate come from our main FE analyses in Tables 3 and 4, our Cragg-Donald Wald F-statistic needs to be larger than 16.38.
The Cragg-Donald Wald F-statistic for the IV analysis is 378.163, which indicates that our IV is relevant and unlikely to be weak, at least from a statistical standpoint, similar to previous studies that have used this instrument (Levi et al. 2014, Sabatier 2015, Chen et al. 2017).
<!-- para 28 -->
Although the exclusion criteria cannot be explicitly tested, we propose that BFC is unlikely to be directly correlated with our dependent variables.
First, BFC has been used in prior FBR studies that predict similar dependent variables (Adams and Ferreira 2009, Levi et al. 2014, Sabatier 2015, Chen et al. 2017).
Second, a direct correlation between BFC and whether and how quickly a firm recalls a medical product is less likely due to the characteristics of the “other boards” that the male board members in our study sit on.
More specifically, the argument for nonexclusivity would be that male board member connectedness with females on other boards not only influences those male board members to vote for and support more females on boards in our study, but also influences how the board members in our study set the tone for recall decisions and thereby predicts our recall outcome measures.
Although we cannot completely rule out this possibility, we believe the risk of this is low based upon the small percentage of “other boards” that are medical product firms that the male directors sit on.
In particular, there are 579 “other boards” that the male board members in our study sit on across the time period of our panel.
Of those, only 50 are medical products firms, which is only 8.6% of them.
In other words, well over 90% of the “other boards” are from nonmedical product industries in which recall decisions as examined in our study are very uncommon.

We now describe results of a sample selection model that provides a secondary means to control for time-variant omitted variable bias.
It is possible that, across time, firms self-select such that those that have high FBR are fundamentally different from those with low FBR and that this difference, and not FBR, explains our FBR-recalls relationship.
Sample selection models, in particular, propensity score matching (PSM) models, use the control variables in the model to predict the likelihood of an observation receiving a certain treatment and then match paired observations according to an equal treatment propensity.
After matching, the PSM model compares across equivalent treatment pairs how actually receiving the treatment, compared with not receiving the treatment, influences a certain outcome measure (Wooldridge 2010).
We use high and low levels of FBR for our treatment measure, and we use the insight gained from Tables 3 and 4 (e.g., FBR predicts class 3 recall counts and class 1 time-to-recall) for our outcome measure.
We split FBR at the median for the treatment variable to ensure a reasonably equivalent number of observations in the treated and nontreated groups.
The treatment indicator is a one if a firm-year observation is above the median of FBR (median FBR = 0.13) and zero if a firm-year observation is at or below the median of FBR.
The outcome measures for the two separate PSM models are the count of class 3 recalls and class 1 time-to-recall.
This PSM analysis performs a one-to-one match of all firm-year observations on equal propensity of being above the median of FBR as predicted by our control variables.
Thus, the PSM analysis determines how actually being treated with an above median value of FBR, compared with not being treated, across a pair of firm-year observations with equivalent likelihood of receiving such a treatment, influences our recall dependent variables.
Finally, we note that to predict this treatment propensity, we not only used our control variables from Tables 3 and 4, but we also included the BFC measure, as Table 5 indicates that BFC has a strong explanatory power over FBR.
<!-- para 41 -->
These PSM results are included in Table 6.
In row 1 of Table 6, we observe a significant and positive average treatment effect of “FBR above median” on class 3 recall count (Average treatment effect = 0.43, p < 0.05), and in row 2, a significant and negative average treatment effect of “FBR above median” on class 1 time-to-recall (Average treatment effect = -0.28, p < 0.05).
Both results indicate that, after controlling for factors that may explain the selection of a firm choosing a high value of FBR, high FBR predicts both more low-severity, high-discretion class 3 recalls and faster high-severity, low-discretion class 1 recalls.
In aggregate, our IV analysis and sample selection model indicate that endogeneity created by time-variant omitted variable bias is unlikely to be creating significant bias in our results.

We next consider the possibility that female directors may be too far removed from the recall decision to be a theoretically tractable explanation for our results.
In other words, our analyses might be at too high of a level by studying directors and not firm managers.
Because we argue that females and males differ in their influence on how recalls decisions are made, a logical robustness check is to examine the gender of senior management.
<!-- para 52 -->
In a medical products firm, the VP of quality is the manager most often responsible for making recall decisions.
Consequently, we identified the gender of every VP of quality for every firm-year in our sample by examining every annual report for each firm in each year.
Among the 835 firm-year observations, there are 101 instances in which the VP of quality was female (12%) and 734 in which the VP was male (88%).
We used this dichotomous variable (female = 1, male = 0) in a robustness check that examines if the gender of the VP of quality influences recall decisions directly and if perhaps the gender of the VP of quality mediates the relationship between FBR and recall decisions.
Demonstrating mediation requires three elements (Baron and Kenny 1986).
First, the explanatory variable (FBR) must predict the outcome variable (recalls).
Second, the explanatory variable (FBR) must predict the mediator variable (VP of quality female).
Finally, the predictive power of the explanatory variable (FBR) on the outcome variable (recalls) must be weakened when the mediator (VP of quality female) is included in the main regression equation.
We present the results of this test in Table A1 in the online appendix.
We find no evidence that we study our problem at too high of a level of analysis.
Using a logistic regression FE model, we first observe that FBR has no predictive power over the dichotomous VP of quality female measure (column (1) of Table A1), and further, when the VP of Quality Female measure is included in our main regression results similar to Tables 3 and 4, it does not predict any recall measures.
This result is supportive of our theorizing that the board sets the tone for how recall decisions should be made.

First, we use a dichotomous measure of the presence or absence of female directors.
This measure is a one if there are any females on the board for a given firm-year observation and a zero otherwise.
As Table A2 in the online appendix indicates, there are 158 observations in which there are no females on the board, and 677 observations in which there is at least one female on the board.

Next, we repeat the dichotomous analysis in Table A3, but this time we use the actual counts of female directors as predictor variables, and we use Wald $\chi^{2}$ tests to explore coefficient differences.
We create indicator variables for one female, two females, and three or more females on the board, and treat no females on the board as the reference category.
We group the categories of three females, four females, and five females into the same statistical category because of the low number of observations in each of these groups as shown in Table A2.
We include this additional model because research indicates that having just one female in a senior leadership team may not be sufficient to change decision-making, but that a tipping point may be reached when multiple females are added (Kramer et al. 2006, Konrad et al. 2008, Torchia et al. 2011).
Identifying such a tipping point is not practical using the FBR measure; hence, we use the discrete counts of female directors in this model.
As shown in Table A4, we observe consistent results for class 3 recall counts in column (3), and the effect of female directors increases as each woman is added to the board.
Specifically, the coefficient for one female ( $\beta = 0.81$, $p < 0.05$) is statistically different from and less than the coefficient for two females ( $\beta = 1.09$, $p < 0.05$) and for three or more females ( $\beta = 1.25$, $p < 0.05$).
Additionally, the coefficient for two females is statistically different from and less than that for three or more females.
This means that, whereas changing from no females on the board to one female on the board is associated with an increase in class 3 recalls, the increase in class 3 recall counts continues to climb as a second female is added and a third female or more are added.

The results for recall timing are shown in column (4) of Table A4 in the online appendix.
Compared with no female directors, we see no effect of having only one female on the board.
However, two females on the board leads to faster class 1 recalls ( $\beta = -0.60$, $p < 0.05$), and three or more females on the board results in even faster class 1 recalls ( $\beta = -0.97$, $p < 0.01$).
The coefficient for two females is negative and significant compared with the reference category of zero female directors, and the coefficient for three or more females is also greater than and statistically different from the coefficient for two females and from the reference category.
This suggests that it takes at least two female directors to influence the time-to-recall for the most severe problems and that adding a third female moves such decisions along even faster.
We explore possible reasons for this result in the discussion section.

## discussion
<!-- para 2 -->
Research on how female directors influence operational decisions is limited (Ahern and Dittmar 2012, Matsa and Miller 2013, Levi et al. 2014), and no inquiry of which we are aware examines how female directors influence product recall decisions.
Given that there has been heightened attention and even new regulations regarding adding females to corporate boards (Fuhrmans 2018), our study is timely and may provide insights to academics, practitioners, and regulators regarding how the addition of female directors may influence operational decisions that impact customer health and safety.
<!-- para 3 -->
More specifically, we find that increasing female directors on a firm's board results in more low-severity recalls being initiated.
This finding is consistent with extant research suggesting women in general are more likely to follow rules than their male counterparts (Green and Melnick 1950, Lee et al. 2017) and supports our theorizing that boards with more female directors will set a stricter rule-following tone than boards with few or no female directors.
Additionally, leveraging a time-to-recall measure, we find that adding female directors leads to faster high-severity recalls.
This builds upon past scholarship suggesting female directors may adopt a broader, more diverse perspective of stakeholders based on their background and experiences (Hillman et al. 2002, Singh et al. 2008), which may set a tone for greater responsiveness when external stakeholders could be harmed.
<!-- para 4 -->
When examining the actual count of female directors in Table A4, we find that, unlike low-severity recalls, in which just one female director appears to significantly influence recall decisions, at least two female directors are required to influence how quickly firms recall the most severe, life-threatening products.
This conclusion builds upon female director research that indicates one female voice may be inadequate to shape decisions that can be risky for the firm (Kanter 1977, Sekaquaptewa and Thompson 2003, Torchia et al. 2011, Chang et al. 2019).
Class 1 recalls have very high stakes, because their high severity is closely tied with the negative effects the firm feels after announcing them.
The firm has much to lose by announcing these recalls (Embry 2016), but society has much to gain.
Female directors may help create better balance in this decision by helping managers consider customer safety and societal benefits in addition to financial losses resulting from recalls.
<!-- para 6 -->
Although we focused specifically on how female directors influence product recall decisions, we extend the discussion by proposing that female directors may influence other operational decisions as well.
Consistent with our theorizing (Nielson and Huse 2010; Byron and Post 2016), we contend that female directors may help firms elevate discussions above traditional operational decision-making paradigms and more purposefully incorporate societal implications into operational decisions.
Inventory decisions, facility location decisions, and capacity management decisions exemplify operational decisions that are often made with cost optimization as the guiding principle.
However, strictly numerical considerations, although necessary, may not always be sufficient in these contexts.
For example, many firms have chosen cost-optimal outsourcing locations that have been shown to rely on unsafe labor practices (Levine et al. 2012).
Relatedly, research indicates that quantitatively driven solutions alone may fail to capture the full complexity and nuances of the societal impact of humanitarian logistics decisions (Holguín-Veras et al. 2012, 2013) and sustainable operational decisions (Kleindorfer et al. 2005).
In cases such as these, the addition of female directors may help firms more comprehensively integrate societal implications into operational decision-making processes, similar to how we propose they influence product recall decisions.
<!-- para 8 -->
Our findings should be viewed in the context of our study design's limitations.
One limitation is that we are not measuring the underlying product quality level at the firm that foreshadows the need for a product recall.
This underlying product quality level may create endogeneity in our model, as it may simultaneously explain the addition of female directors and a subsequent increase in recalls.
This is because research suggests that female executives may be hired prior to revelations of poor firm performance (Rockefeller Foundation 2016) as female directors may be perceived as better suited to deal with crises (Ryan et al. 2011) and may send positive signals to external observers about a firm's ethical behavior (Terjesen et al. 2016).
Hence, it is possible that firms appoint female directors because firm leaders are aware of the need for future recalls and seek to minimize ramifications of anticipated recalls by adding females to the board before the recalls are announced.
<!-- para 9 -->
We believe the risk of this is reasonably low in our data for the following reason.
If this were the case, preemptive female board appointments would most likely occur in anticipation of serious product quality problems.
In other words, we would expect an increase in female directors to predict an increase in class 1 recalls but not necessarily class 3 recalls.
By finding that the addition of female directors predicts more class 3 recalls, but not class 1 recalls, we offer that our results align more closely with a stricter rule-following approach in recall decisions and less with the possibility that female director appointments occurred in anticipation of future recalls.
However, we cannot completely rule out bias created by the unmeasured underlying product quality level.
Future researchers may be able to address this limitation using novel measures of this product quality level.
For instance, in the medical products industry, there are publicly available websites that collect consumer complaints on medical products that could be mined for product complaint data prior to recall announcements.
WebMD.com, the world's largest medical products online forum, is an example of a potential data source that collects medical product complaint data (WebMD 2019).
<!-- para 10 -->
A second limitation is related to our IV for FBR, in particular, the argument we put forth for exclusivity.
Although exclusivity is not testable, we contend that based upon the types of “other boards” these male directors sit upon, it is unlikely that their connectedness with other female board members will simultaneously influence their approval of female board members and firms’ recall decisions.
However, this contention is not without reasonable challenge.
It is possible that male directors who are concerned with board gender diversity are also concerned with product quality transparency and consumer safety.
If true in our data, this would weaken the validity of our IV.
Future research may more fully examine this “correlation of preferences” of male board members through either a natural or laboratory experiment as described below.
<!-- para 11 -->
Regarding a natural experiment, regulations similar to the September 2018 California law requiring boards to include certain numbers of female directors (Fuhrmans 2018) may be used as exogenous shocks in a secondary-data study exploring how mandated female director additions influence recall decision-making.
A laboratory experimental setting could also shed new light.
More precisely, a controlled experiment in which male and female subject recall decision-making preferences are examined under various randomized product quality problem scenarios may be particularly useful.
Finally, we acknowledge the possibility that the relationship we found between female directors and an increase in product recalls may be explained by a worsening of product quality as female directors are added to a board.
Although this remains a potential alternative explanation for our findings, it is inconsistent with studies reporting that female directors monitor management more carefully (Post and Byron 2015) and are more risk-averse (Berger et al. 2014, Levi et al. 2014) than male directors.
<!-- para 13 -->
Product recall decisions are unique in that they can have a considerable impact on society; thus, factors that result in companies recalling defective products more frequently and faster may increase customer safety and societal welfare.
Indeed, enhanced financial performance, greater dividend payouts, higher CEO pay, enhanced employee morale, and improved environmental performance have all been cited as outcomes of having female directors (Soares et al. 2011, Peach 2015, Elsesser 2016, Hersh 2016, Bawden-Davis 2018, Skypala 2018).
However, demonstrating that female board members can improve societal welfare through people-centric operational decisions, in particular, recall decisions, made by firms they oversee has not previously been addressed.
Hence, our study adds a layer of insight into both operations management and female director studies.
