# wowak_et_al_2025_the_politics_of_product_safet 句子库存

## introduction
<!-- para 2 -->
In a speech at Loyola College in 1958, the late U.S. President John F.
Kennedy proclaimed, "Let us not seek the Republican answer or the Democratic answer but the right answer" (JFKLibrary 2022).
It is rare to encounter such compromising perspectives in today's politically charged environment.
In fact, political division in the United States is at an all-time high (Wall Street Journal 2020), with 25% of Americans strongly preferring liberal positions on key political topics, 36% strongly preferring conservative positions, and 35% claiming some portion of the middle ground (Saad 2021).
Regardless of where a person falls on the political ideology spectrum, political leanings tend to influence their stance on a number of issues, including education (Meyer 2017), healthcare (Herwartz and Theilen 2014), and employment practices (Johnson and Roberto 2018), among others.
Although politics have long been a cornerstone of the American society and way of life, political involvement by executives within corporate America has largely been orchestrated outside of the public view.
<!-- para 3 -->
More recently, however, senior executives have become increasingly transparent and vocal about their political leanings, even making public statements about whether they embrace or oppose certain political issues (Wall Street Journal 2021).
It may be expected that executives will speak out about issues—including political ones—that impact their organizations, but a growing number of executives are taking public stances on issues that have no direct impact on their companies and, instead, reflect their personal political beliefs (Wall Street Journal 2021).
Given executives' willingness to speak out about political issues that have little direct impact on their organizations, it is important to examine if their political leanings influence key decisions that not only impact their own organizations, but also public health and safety.
Along these lines, research has found that political ideology—defined as “deeply rooted values, beliefs, and preferences about the ideal goals for the society and convictions about how to achieve them” (Gupta et al. 2019, p. 862)—is a meaningful predictor of various organizational outcomes, such as corporate entrepreneurship (Chin et al. 2021), engagement in corporate social responsibility (CSR) (Chin et al. 2013, Gupta et al. 2017), and chief executive officer (CEO) compensation (Gupta and Wowak 2017), among many others.
Although political views and operational outcomes are important and pervasive topics that dominate public discourse (Wall Street Journal 2020), questions about how firm leaders’ political views influence product quality outcomes that have public health and safety implications has eluded academic attention.
Although existing research is silent on the relationship between firm leadership political ideology and product quality, the recent hyperpoliticization of the U.S. culture in general (Kolbert 2021), and business leaders in particular (Wall Street Journal 2021), highlights the value of exploring this relationship using a rigorous econometric approach.
<!-- para 4 -->
Our study addresses this research gap by examining how the political leanings of a firm's top management team (TMT) impact serious medical product recalls that have consumer health and safety, as well as firm performance, implications.
Medical products, or more specifically pharmaceutical drugs and medical devices, present an ideal setting for examining the relationship between TMT political ideology and product recalls, as it is a high-risk, high-reward industry.
It is high-risk because serious medical product defects can severely harm or even kill consumers (Keller 2022).
However, it also offers high rewards for companies in the form of substantial profit margins, reaching as high as 90% gross margin on certain medical products (Chen 2015, Keown 2020).
With this risk-reward profile, the medical products sector can have considerable social and economic consequences.
Interestingly, the political ideology literature indicates that liberals and conservatives tend to prioritize social and economic consequences quite differently.
Whereas liberals typically emphasize social welfare over economic returns (Gupta and Briscoe 2020), the opposite is true for conservatives (Briscoe et al. 2014).
This prioritization juxtaposition may contribute to more liberal TMTs approaching product quality decisions differently than more conservative TMTs, leading us to the following research question: How does the political ideology of a firm's top management team influence serious medical product recalls?
<!-- para 5 -->
We focus on serious medical product recalls, classified by the Food and Drug Administration (FDA) as Class I and Class II recalls, because they are related to severe product quality issues that can cause substantial harm or even kill consumers, thus having the highest public health and safety implications.
These recalls also tend to have the largest financial consequences for firms (Trievr 2020), and prior research suggests that once serious product defects emerge, firms have very limited discretion in whether to eventually recall such products.
However, because the FDA depends on firms to voluntarily recall, firms do possess significant discretion on recall timing (Wowak et al. 2021).
To develop a comprehensive understanding of how TMT political ideology influences medical product recalls, we examine (1) firms' count of serious medical product recalls and (2) the time to initiate these recalls.
This focus on serious recalls allows us to leverage recall count as a measure of the firm's emphasis on product quality throughout the product design and manufacturing stages and the time to initiate such recalls as a measure of how quickly a firm reacts once a serious product quality problem emerges in the market.
<!-- para 6 -->
We collect data on political donations from thousands of TMT members across 88 firms that had 4,072 serious medical product recalls from 2002 through 2015.
We find that firms with more liberal TMTs experience fewer serious product recalls than those with more conservative TMTs.
Specifically, firms with TMTs one standard deviation more liberal than the mean experience approximately 1.10 fewer serious recalls per year.
In contrast, we find that firms with more liberal TMTs are slower to recall products with serious quality issues than firms with more conservative TMTs.
Specifically, firms with TMTs one standard deviation more liberal than the mean take approximately 22 days longer to initiate serious recalls.
Given that we study serious defects, which have the risks of severely harming or killing consumers, each additional recall, and each additional day that a defective product is on the market, can have significant firm and societal implications.
<!-- para 7 -->
Two important post hoc analyses examine why we find what we do, as well as what may be done about it.
First, we examine our proposed mechanism at work between TMT political ideology and the count of serious recalls by measuring the count of product quality-related adverse events for medical products using the FDA's adverse event databases.
We find that firms with more liberal TMTs experience fewer product quality-related adverse events and that this lower level of product quality-related adverse events partially mediates the relationship between TMT political ideology and recall count, helping to explain why firms with liberal TMTs have fewer serious recalls: they have higher-quality products, necessitating fewer recalls.
Second, because firms with liberal TMTs experience fewer recalls, and firms with conservative TMTs recall defective products faster, it may be the case that firms with politically diverse TMTs benefit from the positive effects of both liberals and conservatives.
Additional post hoc analyses validate this conjecture by highlighting that political diversity on the TMT leads to fewer and faster recalls, capturing the best of both political ideologies.
<!-- para 8 -->
Our study offers important theoretical contributions and managerial implications.
Although past research has examined the influence of CEO turnover (Mayo et al. 2022), female directors (Wowak et al. 2021), and CEO compensation (Wowak et al. 2015) on recall count and time-to-recall, the influence of TMT political ideology on operations management, in particular medical product recalls, remains unexplored.
Similarly, research has found that the political ideology of firm leaders influences many dimensions of firm performance, including CSR initiatives (Chin et al. 2013, Gupta et al. 2017), executive dismissals (Park et al. 2020), and social activism (Gupta and Briscoe 2020), but its influence on product quality outcomes has not been examined until now.
Given that the political ideologies of executives are increasingly emerging as a cornerstone of organizational initiatives (Wall Street Journal 2021), the politicization of businesses is becoming more common (Lemieux 2021), and product recalls are on the rise (Keller 2022), our study answers a particularly timely and relevant research question at the interface of organizations and operations management by exploring the politics of product safety.
<!-- para 9 -->
In terms of implications, our study provides both an awareness of, and a mitigation for, the heretofore-unknown influence of TMT political ideology on serious product recalls in the medical products industry.
Making TMTs aware that their political leanings may be influencing both the number of serious product quality failures that a firm experiences and how quickly it responds when faced with such failures can have considerable financial and public safety consequences.
Beyond awareness, another key implication of our study is demonstrating how firms may be able to mitigate the negative consequences of political ideology on product safety by embracing politically diverse TMTs.
<!-- para 11 -->
To understand how the political ideology of a firm's TMT may influence recall count and the time to initiate those recalls, it is important to discuss how drugs and medical devices are designed, manufactured, and, when necessary, recalled.
Recall counts, especially for serious medical product recalls (Wowak et al. 2021), are a consequence of the degree to which product quality is prioritized, as opposed to other potentially conflicting priorities, such as functionality and profitability, in product design and manufacturing.
In contrast, time-to-recall is a function of executive decision making that occurs once a potential product quality defect emerges (Wowak et al. 2021).
The contextual background presented in this section is based on information acquired through multiple sources, including: (1) interviewing senior executives who are responsible for designing and manufacturing drugs and medical devices; (2) interviewing a senior director at the FDA whose duties include overseeing firm recall decisions; (3) studying FDA guidance documents on how products are designed and how recall decisions should be made (FDA 2018a, 2020a); and (4) examining research on the recall process (Thirumalai and Sinha 2011, Ball et al. 2018, Wowak et al. 2021).
We first discuss the five basic steps in the design and manufacturing processes for drugs and medical devices and then explain the recall decision-making process.
<!-- para 12 -->
Step one is discovery and development, which occurs when a firm identifies an unmet medical need and then refines an initial idea to address that need.
For drugs, discovery is often prompted by an event such as an emergent disease, unanticipated effects of existing drugs, or novel insights about a disease that enable researchers to design a new drug that stops or reverses effects of the disease (FDA 2018c, Lansdowne 2020).
Development for promising drugs consists of conducting experiments to better understand how drug compounds may be absorbed, potential benefits and side effects, and possible interactions with other treatments (FDA 2018c).
For medical devices, discovery occurs when a firm identifies an unmet clinical need that can be addressed by a medical device, and development is when researchers create proof of concept to help determine feasibility (FDA 2018b).
Step two is preclinical research (FDA 2018a).
This involves studies to understand potential dosing and toxicity levels of new drugs or building prototypes of new medical devices and refining them via testing in laboratories or animal subjects (FDA 2018b, 2018c).
Step three is clinical research (FDA 2018a), where new drugs are tested on humans in controlled trials (FDA 2018c, Lansdowne 2020), and medical devices are assessed for safety and effectiveness in humans (FDA 2018b, Marešová et al. 2020).
Step four is FDA review, which is when drug and medical device companies file an application to market the product to the public (Corr and Williams 2009, FDA 2018a).
Here, the FDA reviews data from steps two and three and decides whether to approve the product.
If approved, the firm can begin manufacturing and distributing the product to consumers.
The fifth step is postmarket safety monitoring, which is an ongoing process where the firm and the FDA monitor product quality on the market (FDA 2018a, 2022c).
<!-- para 13 -->
The product recall decision-making process occurs after the product is on the market and is similar for drugs and medical devices (FDA 2020c, 2022a), although firm idiosyncrasies exist.
Firms have dedicated teams that continuously assess product quality.
These teams collect and analyze internal data from product testing and inspection reports, as well as external data, such as healthcare customer (e.g., physician and nurses) quality complaints.
The teams are also tasked with determining if product quality issues are due to random sources of variability in the manufacturing process or systemic issues that may necessitate a recall.
If it is the latter, a recall committee consisting of various senior leaders (e.g., quality, research and development (R&D), manufacturing, legal, clinical, functional areas, etc.) is convened to determine if a recall is warranted.
If a recall is deemed necessary, the recall committee makes a formal recommendation to the senior executive who is responsible for making the final recall decision (Burton 2014).
Although there is structure and formality to the recall decision-making process within the firm, there is also a reasonable amount of variation, as the aforementioned description indicates.
Such variation is due to the fact that although the FDA sets the policies and guidelines for how and, broadly, when, to recall, it leaves the actual recall decision to the firm (FDA 2020b).
<!-- para 14 -->
A firm's TMT maintains significant discretion over how drugs and medical devices are designed, manufactured, and recalled, which could result in substantial variation across firms in both recall count and time-to-recall (FDA 2020c, 2022a).
For example, the TMT decides which drugs and medical devices to design and manufacture and which ones to abandon early in the process (FDA 2018b, 2018c; Castellanos 2021).
This decision is, at least in part, influenced by the TMT's emphasis on the potential economic returns from the product being developed or their focus on possible safety risks and the resulting social welfare implications.
The TMT also determines where to allocate resources when designing and manufacturing new products.
They are tasked with weighing investments in quality control initiatives that minimize safety risks to consumers vis-à-vis innovating in ways that could lead to new products that command exceptionally high profit margins, but also come with a high risk of quality failures (Castellanos 2021).
Additionally, the TMT oversees the managers who continuously assess product quality to identify if defects are due to random variability or systemic issues that require a recall.
As such, even in the tightly regulated drug and medical device industries, there is considerable discretion in terms of quality investments in product design and manufacturing that influence recall counts, as well as in how long a recall decision is deliberated, which influences the time-to-recall.
When firms have such discretion, the decisions are likely influenced by individual characteristics or values, of which one important indicator is the TMT's political ideology.

## theory
<!-- para 3 -->
Research on political ideology has longstanding roots in the political science and political psychology literatures (Jost 2006).
Scholarship in this domain has primarily focused on the formation and evolution of a person's political leanings, as well as how political ideology influences their actions (Jost et al. 2007).
The key themes emerging from this body of work are that a person's political ideology is established early in their life, highly stable over time, and a representation of their overall values (Jost et al. 2003).
The vast majority of research on political ideology has invoked the liberal-conservative spectrum propagated by political psychology research (Swigart et al. 2020) because it has been identified as the single most useful way to classify political attitudes and values (Chin and Semadeni 2017; Gupta and Wowak 2017; Gupta et al. 2017, 2019).
<!-- para 4 -->
On one end of the spectrum are liberals, who tend to be more sensitive to and aware of social issues than conservatives and see responsibilities to society as collective rather than individual (Gupta et al. 2017).
As such, more liberal executives often take into consideration a broader array of organizational stakeholders, including secondary stakeholders like consumers who may be impacted by a firm's product offerings (Gupta and Briscoe 2020).
When making decisions, more liberal executives are often attuned to how their firm's actions impact society as a whole, and they often take actions that lead to the betterment of society (Gupta et al. 2019).
Accordingly, more liberal executives are apt to embrace social change that benefits a broad array of stakeholders (Chin et al. 2013) and focus on how the firms' practices impact the broader macroenvironment (Gupta et al. 2017).
In sum, more liberal executives tend to prioritize minimizing harm to consumers and contributing to society, even at the expense of economic outcomes (Briscoe et al. 2014, Gupta et al. 2017, Gupta and Briscoe 2020).
<!-- para 5 -->
On the other end of the political ideology spectrum are conservatives, who, on average, emphasize individual agency and wealth creation over societal welfare (Gupta et al. 2019).
Consequently, more conservative executives emphasize creating financial value for shareholders (Briscoe et al. 2014).
More conservative executives also tend to be risk-averse and uncertainty-avoidant in their decision making, particularly when such decisions have financial implications (Jost et al. 2007).
In addition, conservative executives prioritize the optimal use or allocation of organizational resources and often make such decisions with maximum return on investment in mind (Gupta et al. 2019).
Research also indicates that conservatives view responsibilities as individual, rather than collective (Briscoe and Joshi 2017), which can lead to them deemphasizing the societal consequences when making firm-related decisions.
Given their contrasting values and prioritization of various outcomes, it is reasonable to assume that the political leanings of the TMT may influence product quality outcomes that impact product safety.
<!-- para 7 -->
Although the recall literature has grown considerably in the last few years due to the increased frequency and heightened societal impact of recalls (Ball et al. 2022), there is limited research that considers the influence of TMT composition on product recalls and no research, to our knowledge, that examines the influence of TMT political ideology on a firm's recall count and time-to-recall.
A firm's recall count is a frequently examined quality outcome in recall research (Haunchild and Rhee 2004, Rhee and Haunchild 2006, Ball et al. 2018) and may be influenced by the political ideology of the TMT.
However, the literatures on recalls and political ideology offer potentially conflicting arguments as to the influence of TMT political ideology on recall counts.
<!-- para 8 -->
On the one hand, firms with more liberal TMTs may experience fewer recalls because of their socially oriented quality prioritization during product design and manufacturing.
Research suggests that more liberal TMTs are more aware of how their firm's actions impact society and, correspondingly, place greater emphasis on their firms minimizing harm to consumers (Briscoe et al. 2014, Gupta et al. 2017, Gupta and Briscoe 2020).
In other words, this research argues that more liberal TMTs tend to engage in firm activities that minimize detrimental outcomes to society.
These arguments suggest that more liberal TMTs would design and manufacture products that are higher in quality and require fewer serious recalls.
<!-- para 9 -->
On the other hand, firms with more conservative TMTs may experience fewer serious recalls because they appreciate the long-term financial value of prioritizing product quality during the design and manufacturing phases, reducing the risk that products subsequently fail in the market and require a recall.
Indeed, research indicates that product recalls can be particularly expensive, so more conservative TMTs who tend to focus on maximizing shareholder wealth may be more motivated to design and manufacture safer products in an effort to avoid recalls, which can erode shareholder value (Jarrell and Peltzman 1985, Davidson and Worrell 1992, Darby et al. 2023).
Product recalls can also introduce considerable risk and uncertainty to a firm's future performance and executive job security, which risk-averse and uncertainty-avoidant conservatives may proactively try to mitigate by investing in product quality from the onset (Jost et al. 2007).
Given these competing arguments, we put forth the following hypotheses for how liberal and conservative TMTs may influence a firm's recall count:
<!-- para 10 -->
> [!Hypothesis]
> Hypothesis 1(a).
There is a negative relationship between top management team liberalism and the count of recalls.
<!-- para 11 -->
> [!Hypothesis]
> Hypothesis 1(b).
There is a positive relationship between top management team liberalism and the count of recalls.
<!-- para 12 -->
Research on the time-to-recall is notably sparser (Hora et al. 2011, Eilert et al. 2017), particularly in the medical product industry (Wowak et al. 2021).
One could argue that more liberal TMTs would recall products faster because not doing so might harm consumers, particularly in cases involving serious product quality issues.
More liberal TMTs may also view acting swiftly when recalling products as the firm's responsibility and the right thing to do for society (Gupta et al. 2019).
Conversely, if more liberal TMTs prioritize quality during product design and manufacturing, it could foster a sense of overconfidence that the product is better (i.e., of higher quality) than it actually is (Schwartz 2019, Reis 2020), resulting in slower response time when making recall decisions.
This line of thinking is consistent with studies that have shown that overconfidence can result in individuals falling prey to detrimental decision-making biases (Reis 2020), which, in our context, may result in slower recall decisions.
In other words, firms with more liberal TMTs may simply be slower to recognize that the product quality issue is due to the firm's product design and/or manufacturing decisions, instead attributing it to random failures, because the TMT believes its product quality investments should lead to higher-quality products and, thus, require fewer recalls.
<!-- para 13 -->
On the other hand, more conservative TMTs may recall products faster than their liberal counterparts because a responsive recall strategy offers the firm the best chance to lower the monetary penalty associated with the recall (Briscoe et al. 2014).
When firms respond slowly to product recalls, it can lead to increased financial consequences (Darby et al. 2023) due to accumulated damages, legal fees, and financial penalties (Department of Justice 2010, 2011).
Firms, for example, have a better idea of what a recall will cost if they initiate it quickly or before consumers are severely harmed, whereas the cost can increase exponentially if firms delay recalls, especially for serious product quality issues.
Given the focus of more conservative TMTs on creating financial value for shareholders (Briscoe et al. 2014), it is reasonable to assume that they would adopt a recall decision-making approach that would minimize the expected financial damage to the firm.
<!-- para 14 -->
Conversely, firms with more conservative TMTs may recall products more slowly because of their tendency to be risk-averse and uncertainty-avoidant (Jost et al. 2007).
In our context, these tendencies for risk aversion and uncertainty avoidance may result in more conservative TMTs recalling more slowly to avoid the risk of a “false alarm,” or issuing a recall when it is not necessary.
Issuing a recall when one is not needed would result in the firm unnecessarily experiencing the financial penalties associated with a recall, which conservative TMTs who focus on wealth creation (Briscoe et al. 2014) will want to avoid.
Firms with more conservative TMTs also tend to maintain hierarchical, internally structured processes (Gupta and Briscoe 2020), which may slow the recall decision-making process as the decision to recall passes through the organization’s hierarchy.
Given the above competing arguments, we put forth the following hypotheses for how liberal and conservative TMTs may influence a firm’s time-to-recall:
<!-- para 15 -->
> [!Hypothesis]
> Hypothesis 2(a).
There is a negative relationship between top management team liberalism and time-to-recall.
<!-- para 16 -->
> [!Hypothesis]
> Hypothesis 2(b).
There is a positive relationship between top management team liberalism and time-to-recall.

## methods
<!-- para 2 -->
Our sample consists of all publicly traded medical product firms regulated by the FDA.
To be as comprehensive as possible, we collect data on all publicly traded firms that did (88 firms) and did not (41 firms) experience a recall during the time period spanning 2002 through 2015.
We obtained recall data from the FDA via a Freedom of Information Act (FOIA) request, product approval data from the openFDA platform, product sales data from Medicaid.gov for drug sales volume and MedMine LLC [^1] for medical device sales volume, book-based financial data from Compustat, top manager data from Execucomp, capital market information from the Center for Research in Security Prices (CRSP), security analyst data via the Institutional Brokers' Estimate System (I/B/E/S), board data from Institutional Shareholder Services (ISS) and BoardEx, and demographic characteristics of the locales where firms are headquartered from the United States census data repository.
To measure TMT political ideology, we collected data on managers' political donations from the Federal Election Committee (FEC) database called Open Secrets using a procedure detailed later in this section.
As we will describe later in this section, our primary empirical analyses use firm fixed effects, so firms with zero recalls in total during our sampling frame are excluded from our analysis because they do not exhibit any within-firm variance on the dependent variables.
Our final sample contains 4,072 serious drug (819 observations) and medical device (3,253 observations) recalls across 88 recalling firms over the 14 years in our data.
<!-- para 4 -->
**4.2.1.
Dependent Variables.** Recall count measures the number of serious recalls that a firm initiated in each year. [^2] The FDA categorizes recalls into one of three classes based on defect severity.
Class I and II recalls are for serious product quality issues that can either lead to death or medically reversible harm to consumers, whereas Class III recalls involve defects that pose no health risk to consumers (FDA 2019).
We focus on serious recalls (i.e., Class I and II) because they have the greatest consumer health and safety implications and the largest financial consequences (Trievr 2020). [^3] These are also the recalls that are hardest to avoid initiating, serving as a reliable proxy for actual underlying product quality at the firm (Ball et al. 2018).
<!-- para 5 -->
Consistent with the literature (Wowak et al. 2021), we measure time-to-recall as the natural log of the number of days between when the firm first becomes aware of a product quality issue (i.e., defect awareness date) and when the firm initiated a recall to address the issue (i.e., recall initiation date).
Firms with lower time-to-recall are more responsive to product defects than firms with higher values for this variable.
Although the recall initiation date is publicly available on the FDA's recall website, the defect awareness date is available only via FOIA, and it is validated by the FDA for usage in its recall analyses.
<!-- para 6 -->
**4.2.2.
Independent Variables.** Top management team political ideology is calculated as the average annual political ideology across members of a firm's TMT (Chin and Semadeni 2017, Gupta and Wowak 2017).
To compute this measure, we carefully followed the procedure documented in political ideology research (e.g., Chin et al. 2013, Briscoe et al. 2014, Gupta and Wowak 2017, Bermiss and McDonald 2018).
We first used Execucomp to identify the top managers in each organization in all the firm-years in our sample (Ridge et al. 2015, Chin and Semadeni 2017).
The top managers featured in Execucomp are typically the CEO, CFO, COO, and two other C-suite executives depending on the type of firm (e.g., Chief Information Officer, Chief Health Officer, etc.).
<!-- para 7 -->
Next, we identified each TMT member's political ideology by accessing political donation records from the FEC website called Open Secrets.
The FEC requires individuals making any donation in excess of $200 to submit a regulated form in which the donor lists their name, occupation, employer, address, and other details (Chin et al. 2013, Gupta and Wowak 2017, Bonica 2019).
We used information derived from the FEC donor forms to identify political donations made by
<!-- para 8 -->
TMT members in our sample.
Consistent with past research, we reconciled donor addresses, middle names, employment histories, and other relevant details about executives with the information contained in Open Secrets.
<!-- para 9 -->
Using the political donation data, we then calculated four indicators that have been shown to collectively reflect an individual's political ideology along the liberal-conservative spectrum (Bermiss and McDonald 2018, Bonica 2019, Swigart et al. 2020): (1) the total number of donations made to Democrat candidates divided by all donations to Republicans and Democrats; (2) the total dollar value of donations made to Democrat candidates divided by the total value of all donations to Republicans and Democrats; (3) the number of distinct Democrat candidates who received a donation divided by the total number of distinct Republican and Democrat candidates who received donations; and (4) the number of discrete years in which a donation was made to a Democrat candidate divided by the total number of discrete years in which a donation was made to either Republican or Democrat candidates. [^4] Each of these four indicators ranges from 0 to 1; 1 represents donations only to Democrats or pure liberalism, 0 represents donations only to Republicans or pure conservatism, and values between 0 and 1 reflect different gradations of political ideologies (Chin et al. 2013, Wowak et al. 2022).
<!-- para 10 -->
Following research precedence, we averaged the indicators to calculate each TMT member's political ideology (Briscoe and Joshi 2017, Chin and Semadeni 2017, Gupta and Wowak 2017).
Chin et al. (2013) found that using the average of these political indicators is the most appropriate method to aggregate them, as they demonstrate high reliability and internal consistency ( $\alpha = 0.95$).
In line with this literature, we assign a score of 0.5 to individuals with no political donations, indicating that they are politically moderate (Chin et al. 2013, Gupta and Wowak 2017, Gupta et al. 2018).
That said, in robustness checks we remove nondonors from our sample and demonstrate that assigning a value of 0.5 to them does not meaningfully influence our results.
It is important to emphasize that we calculate this variable using every TMT member's political donation in each year of our sample, allowing an executive's ideology to vary from year to year.
<!-- para 11 -->
**4.2.3.
Control Variables.** Although past studies have shown that one's political ideology is largely exogenous and unlikely to be informed by factors that may also impact firm outcomes (Jost and Andrews 2011, Chin et al. 2013), it is possible that the selection and retention of managers may be influenced by their political ideologies (Chin and Semadeni 2017, Bermiss and McDonald 2018, Busenbark et al. 2023).
Because of this, we control for several factors that may influence TMT political ideology, recall count, and/or time-to-recall at multiple levels, including at the CEO/TMT, board of directors, and firm level.
The complete list of control variables is provided in Table 1, along with the variable description, possible factor(s) of influence, and citation(s) of extant work supporting the inclusion of the control variable.
We include firm and year fixed effects in all models.
<!-- para 12 -->
We control for several CEO/TMT characteristics, including CEO age, TMT mean age, and TMT tenure, as past research illustrates that age and tenure can affect managers' strategic decisions and their political ideologies (Hambrick and Fukutomi 1991, Morey et al. 2012, Gupta and Wowak 2017, Chin et al. 2021).
We further control for CEO compensation, TMT stock option ownership, and TMT compensation dispersion because political ideologies can influence such income disparities (Wowak et al. 2015, Chin and Semadeni 2017, Gupta and Wowak 2017).
We also control for TMT female percent and female CEO because female representation among executives can influence recall decisions and political ideologies (Briscoe and Joshi 2017, Wowak et al. 2021).
CEO duality may influence the independence of the other TMT members from the CEO and consequently influence recall decisions (Chakravarty et al. 2022, Martins and Pires 2023).
<!-- para 13 -->
We control for various board-of-director characteristics, including nonwhite board representation and female board representation, which may influence political ideology or recall decisions (Wowak et al. 2021, Chow et al. 2022), as well as mean external boards and independent directors percent because both shed light on the ability of the TMT to shape firm decisions as a function of their own preferences or political ideologies (Daily et al. 1998, Boivie et al. 2016, Chakravarty et al. 2022).
Similarly, we control for mean director tenure, as firms may make different recall decisions if directors are new (Beasley 1996, Wowak et al. 2021).
<!-- para 14 -->
We also control for myriad firm characteristics, which include stock returns and analyst recommendation, as managers with stronger performance may perceive more discretion in recall decisions and may feel more discretion to allow their individual prerogatives to influence organizational actions (Davidson and Worrell 1992, Schulz and Wiersema 2018).
We also include analyst recommendation dispersion, as firms with less analyst consensus may have a more opaque information environment and may try to obfuscate negative events like recalls (Baginski et al. 1993, Chao et al. 2009).
We similarly control for total assets, as well as return on assets (ROA), because firm size and book-based performance may affect managers' perceived discretion (Josefy et al. 2015).
We also control for R&D intensity, as this may influence recalls (Thirumalai and Sinha 2011).
We further control for two characteristics of the firm's recall environment that could affect the hypothesized variables in our specifications.
First is recall experience, as firms with more previous recalls may be differentially sensitive to future recalls (Ball et al. 2018).
Second is sales volume, because as the sales volume increases, so does the chance of product defects, necessitating more recalls (Lichtenberg and Philipson 2002, Shah et al. 2017).
Finally, we control for the college education per capita and annual income per capita of the counties where the firms are headquartered, as employees' education and socioeconomic statuses could affect product quality outcomes (Jun et al. 2006).

We examine the influence of TMT political ideology on each of our dependent variables using similar forms of two-stage instrumental variable (IV) fixed effects regression.
Specifically, we use a two-stage negative binomial fixed effects model for recall count and a two-stage least squares fixed effects model for time-to-recall (Kennedy 2008, Wowak et al. 2021).
The level of analysis for the recall count model is the firm-year, and the level of analysis for the time-to-recall model is the individual recall.
<!-- para 19 -->
We adopt this estimation technique—both firm fixed effects and our IV design—to account for various sources of unmeasured heterogeneity that could potentially impact both TMT political ideology and each of our recall-related outcomes, thus inducing bias from endogeneity (Kennedy 2008, Greene 2018).
For instance, research on political ideology in organizations contends that employees are attracted to firms based on the extent to which their political ideology aligns with various facets of the organization that are often unmeasurable (Bermiss and McDonald 2018), including sociopolitical dynamics in the boardroom (Busenbark et al. 2023) and the degree to which they agree with firm leaders' espoused political positions beyond their political donations (Wowak et al. 2022).
It is also possible that our estimates could exhibit bias from simultaneity if TMT members donate to a political party to curry favoritism.
Such simultaneity is unlikely in our setting for two key reasons.
First, prior research shows that personal political donations, particularly by TMT members, represent actual ideologies and are not used strategically to influence regulators (Chin et al. 2013, Gupta and Briscoe 2020).
Relatedly, it is important to note that the literature on firms seeking political influence or favoritism focuses on the lobbying dollars spent on behalf of the firm, instead of personal political donations from individual actors within the firm (Kim and Siegel 2024).
Second, studies have consistently shown that a person's political ideology is highly stable over time and not apt to fluctuate based on seeking political favors (Gupta and Briscoe 2020).
We observe this characteristic as well; 92% of the TMT members in our sample do not transition across the political party that they donate to during our sampling period.
<!-- para 20 -->
However, out of an abundance of caution, and to further ameliorate concerns related to endogeneity bias that may be caused by this type of simultaneity, or other sources of endogeneity, we use IV estimation.
Specifically, IVs predict the focal independent variable (i.e., TMT political ideology), but not the error term of the corresponding second stage.
These two characteristics of valid instruments are referred to as relevance and exogeneity, respectively, and are requisite features of appropriate IVs (Kennedy 2008, Greene 2018).
Identifying valid instruments that adhere to these characteristics in our recall setting is quite challenging, as it is difficult to identify observable measures that predict TMT political ideology, but are theoretically unrelated to all unmeasured indicators that could also affect our recall-related dependent variables.
<!-- para 21 -->
To address this challenge, we use an IV approach that has emerged from the econometrics literature called the heteroskedastic identified instrument technique.
This technique, which has recently been adopted in operations and management research (Campbell et al. 2021, Hasan et al. 2022, Wang et al. 2022), is designed to accommodate a setting "when no external instruments or other such information is available" (Baum and Lewbel 2019, p. 757).
This procedure allows us to generate valid instruments via three steps (Lewbel 2012, 2018; Bun and Harrison 2019).
First, we use the potentially endogenous independent variable (TMT political ideology) as the dependent variable in a first-stage equation that features all our controls as regressors.
Just as Lewbel (2012) theorized and Baum and Lewbel (2019) emphasize, we include all of our control variables as the regressors in this first-stage equation because doing so is the preferred specification, unless including a subset of the controls better upholds the assumptions of the model.
In our setting, as we describe below, the model assumptions hold when using all the control variables.
In the second step, the technique calculates the residuals associated with each of those control variable regressors and transforms the heteroskedasticity into potentially valid IVs, but only when the assumptions of the model that we detail next are exhibited (Lewbel 2012, Baum and Lewbel 2019, Bun and Harrison 2019).
Finally, we incorporate the valid generated instruments into the two-stage IV fixed effects estimators.
<!-- para 22 -->
Scholars indicate that the heteroskedastic identified instrument procedure can generate valid instruments under two assumptions (Lewbel 2012, Baum and Lewbel 2019, Lim et al. 2024).
First, Lim et al. (2024) note that the instruments generated from the heteroskedastic identified technique must not be correlated with the covariance in the error terms from the first and second stage equations (i.e., the models predicting TMT political ideology and then our recall dependent variables, respectively).
Just as Baum and Lewbel (2019) prescribe, Lim et al. (2024, p. 9) emphasize that this assumption is upheld by “failing to reject homoskedasticity with respect to [the first-stage regressors]” via the Pagan-Hall test.
For our data, the Pagan-Hall diagnostic fails to reject the null (Pagan-Hall = 4.961; p = 0.084), thereby adhering to this first assumption.
Second, Lim et al. (2024) state that the generated instruments must be meaningfully correlated with the endogenous independent variable, which in our case is TMT political ideology.
In line with Baum and Lewbel (2019), Lim et al. (2024, p. 9) argue that this assumption can be supported when scholars “reject homoskedasticity with respect to the selected [regressors]” via the Breusch and Pagan (1979) test.
Our variables uphold this condition by rejecting the null of homoskedasticity (Breusch-Pagan = 127.340; p < 0.001), thus adhering to this second assumption.
<!-- para 23 -->
It is worth underscoring that our generated instruments also conform to the traditional diagnostic tests pertaining to relevance and exogeneity for any type of IV.
Indeed, the partial F-statistic exceeds the thresholds that scholars suggest represent relevance (partial F-stat = 59.534; p < 0.001), and the two-step identification test from Andrews (2018) does not contain zero [-15.390, -3.943], reflecting relevant instruments (Stock et al. 2002).
Similarly, diagnostic tests for exogeneity suggest our instruments are unrelated to the structural error terms pertaining to recall count (Sargan  $\chi^{2} = 0.100$; p = 0.905) and time-to-recall (Sargan  $\chi^{2} = 0.456$; p = 0.499), indicating that our instruments are not endogenous (Semadeni et al. 2014).
Taken together, our instruments appear to be properly identified and valid.

**6.1.1.
Control Variables.** In our primary specification, we included numerous control variables that prior research has shown may influence political ideology, recalls, or both.
To help examine the sensitivity of our statistical inference, and the strength of our hypothesized relationships, we include a regression in which we exclude all control variables.
Table 5, columns (I) and (II) indicate that, even in the absence of any control variables, more liberal TMTs issue fewer recalls ( $\beta = -0.047$; p < 0.10), but take longer to initiate them ( $\beta = 0.394$; p < 0.001), demonstrating the robustness of our results as they pertain to the control variables we include.
<!-- para 19 -->
**6.1.2.
Instrumental Variables.** We next repeat our analyses without instrumental variables.
Table 5, columns (III) and (IV) suggest that TMT political ideology is negatively related to recall count ( $\beta = -0.109$; p < 0.001) and positively associated with time-to-recall ( $\beta = 0.432$; p < 0.01).
Although we employed IVs that met the relevance and exclusion criteria in our main analysis, the consistency of our results from the noninstrumented approach indicates that bias from endogeneity may be relatively low in our setting.
<!-- para 20 -->
**6.1.3.
Recall Rates.** In our primary specification, we measure recall count as the number of recalls in a given year, and we control for the sales volume of each firm in a given year.
We made this decision because prior research has shown that bias can be introduced into a model when using a ratio (e.g., recalls per sales) as a dependent variable (Pearson 1896), whereas controlling for the variable can help scale the dependent variable in ways that do not produce such spurious estimates (Certo et al. 2020).
Nevertheless, to ensure that our results are robust to alternate specifications, we also examine the rate of recalls.
Specifically, in this analysis, we measure recall rate as the number of recalls in a year divided by the sales volume of the firm in that year.
We use fractional response regression for this analysis, as research has shown that it is the most appropriate model to estimate relationships when the dependent variable is a percentage (Papke and Wooldridge 1996, Wooldridge 2011, Villadsen and Wulff 2021).
Our results in Table 5, column (V) show a negative association between TMT political ideology and recall rate ( $\beta = -1.454$; p < 0.001), lending further support to our results.

**6.1.5.
TMT Political Ideology Measure.** In our main analysis, we follow prior political ideology literature by assigning a score of 0.5 to individuals with no political donations, indicating that they are politically moderate (e.g., Chin et al. 2013, Gupta and Wowak 2017, Gupta et al. 2018).

As such, in this analysis, we remove TMT members with no political donations and repeat our analyses.
Our results in Table 7, columns (X) and (XI) demonstrate consistent results: TMT political ideology is negatively related to recall count ( $\beta = -0.106$; p < 0.001) and positively associated with time-to-recall ( $\beta = 0.395$; p < 0.05).

**6.1.6.
Non-Recalling Firms.** Our primary analyses use firm fixed effects.
Although doing so ensures that time-invariant and firm-level omitted variable biases do not influence our results, this approach omits firms with no variance on the dependent variable.
As such, those firms that never initiated a recall in our sample time period are econometrically excluded from the analysis.
To ensure that this does not bias our results, we employ firm random effects in place of firm fixed effects.
This allows us to include an additional 41 firms that did not experience any recalls in our sample.
As can be seen in Table 7, column (XII), in which we employ a random effects estimator instead of fixed effects like we did in Table 4, column (III), our results remain consistent.
Note that we only conduct this test to examine recall counts, as time-to-recall is contingent upon a firm initiating a recall.

We present the results of two post hoc analyses that answer two important questions: Why are our results as they are, and what can be done about them?
Our first analysis explores a more granular measure of actual product quality—namely, product quality-related adverse events.
The second analysis examines whether embracing TMT political diversity may help firms extract the benefits of both more liberal and more conservative TMTs, while sparing firms the downsides of each.

**6.2.1.
Product Quality-Related Adverse Events.** Our theorizing proposes that more liberal TMTs initiate fewer recalls because they focus more deliberately on quality during product design and manufacturing than their more conservative counterparts.
We examine this theorizing further by leveraging product quality-related adverse events, which are an even more granular measure of product quality.
We employ adverse events in two ways.
We first use them in a mediation test, exploring if the relationship between TMT political ideology and recall counts is explained by a reduction in adverse events.
If so, this would lend further credibility to the notion that more liberal TMTs initiate fewer recalls because their firms make higher-quality products.
Second, we exploit this adverse event data in a more restrictive recall count analysis.
We replicate the recall count regression from Table 4, but using only those recalls that experienced adverse events prior to the recall initiation.
We preface both additional tests with a detailed explanation of the adverse events data.
<!-- para 39 -->
The FDA maintains two databases that delineate information about product quality-related adverse events.
The FDA Adverse Event Reporting System (FAERS) is the database for drug product quality adverse events (Ahuja et al. 2021), and the Manufacturer and User Facility Device Experience (MAUDE) is the database for medical device product quality adverse events (Mukherjee and Sinha 2018). [^6] For all tests in this post hoc, we only used adverse events that span the same timeframe as our main analysis (i.e., 2002–2015), are actual product quality problems, are serious in nature, and are unrelated to customer satisfaction or common product side effects.
We do so by leveraging specific data fields in each database.
In FAERS, we focused on two data fields.
The first was the “Drug Characterization” data field, or the category (1) field (FDA 2024b).
This category is used for adverse events when “the drug was considered by the reporter to be the cause” of the adverse event (FDA 2024b).
The second data field is titled “Expedited.” This data field is for adverse events that are not described on the product’s labeling (FAERS 2024).
This data field excludes adverse events that are due to common product side effects and, further, only considers adverse events that are serious in nature (FDA 2015, Sonawane et al. 2018).
In the MAUDE database, we focused on the “Product problem flag” data field (FDA 2024a).
The product problem flag is used to indicate an adverse event in which a medical device malfunctions because of a quality defect.
It is also reserved for serious adverse events that could lead to consumer death or serious injury and are thus unrelated to common side effects (FDA 2022b).
<!-- para 40 -->
To examine mediation, we first regress the natural log of adverse event count on TMT political ideology in Table 8, column (I).
Consistent with our recall count analysis in Table 4, we find that TMT political ideology is a significant and negative predictor of the natural log of adverse event count ( $\beta = -0.246$; p < 0.001), supporting the quality-improving premise of more liberal TMTs.
We then regress recall counts on the natural log of adverse event count and TMT political ideology in Table 8, column (II).
We find that adverse event count is positively associated with recall count ( $\beta = 0.018$; p < 0.01), and TMT political ideology continues to be negatively associated with recall count ( $\beta = -0.110$; p < 0.001), although to a lesser degree, when adverse event count is included in the model.
With the estimates in Table 8, columns (I) and (II), we compute the indirect effect of TMT political ideology on recall count as mediated by adverse event count following the indirect effect method, as explained in Beer and Qi (2024).
We find that adverse event count significantly mediates the relationship between TMT political ideology and recall count (indirect effect = -0.130; p < 0.01).
This analysis provides empirical support for our theorized mechanism; by prioritizing product quality in design and manufacturing, firms with more liberal TMTs experience fewer serious recalls because they have higher-quality products.
<!-- para 41 -->
Next, with the knowledge that adverse events are lower in firms with more liberal TMTs, and that they help to explain recall counts, we examine the impact of TMT political ideology using a more nuanced measure of recalls.
That is, we only count the number of firm-year recalls that were associated with at least one adverse event before the recall was initiated. [^7] By restricting our recall count measure to the subset that have experienced at least one prior adverse event, we help to build confidence that our recall count measure serves as a reliable proxy for actual product quality.
In Table 8, column (III), we reproduce our two-stage IV fixed effects estimator with this version of recall count that is contingent on at least one prior adverse event for the product recalled.
As we show in column (III), our results remain consistent ( $\beta = -0.266$; p < 0.001).

**6.2.2.
Political Diversity on the TMT.** A logical conclusion from our research is that firms may seek to diversify their TMTs politically in the hopes of having both fewer recalls and faster ones.
In this post hoc analysis, we examine the influence of political ideology diversity on both recall counts and time-to-recall in order to provide actionable and tested managerial implications.
Following research precedence on capturing dispersion within a group (Baginski et al. 1993, Busenbark et al. 2017, Chin and Semadeni 2017), we measure political ideology diversity using the standard deviation of TMT members' political ideologies in each firm-year, scaled by the mean political ideology of the TMT—the coefficient of variation of TMT political ideology.
Thus, political ideology diversity takes larger values when the ideologies of the TMT members at a given firm are more dispersed away from a central tendency and smaller values when the TMT members tend to share political views.
In this way, two firms with the same mean TMT political ideology could have different values for political ideology diversity.
For instance, this variable takes on a larger value for a firm that has an equal number of staunch conservatives and ardent liberals (high dispersion) compared with another firm that has all TMT members with similar political views (low dispersion).
The goal of this analysis is to determine whether TMTs comprising both liberal and conservative members exhibit the most favorable product quality outcomes in terms of fewer recalls and faster recalls relative to TMTs with less political ideology diversity.
<!-- para 47 -->
The results from our two-stage IV fixed effects models specified with TMT political diversity for both recall count and time-to-recall are shown in Table 9, columns (IV) and (V), respectively.
Consistent with the implication from our study, our estimators suggest that political diversity is associated with both fewer and faster recalls, capturing the positive product safety aspects of both liberal and conservative TMTs, as TMT political diversity is negatively related to recall count ( $\beta = -0.230$; p < 0.001) in column (III) and time-to-recall ( $\beta = -0.462$; p < 0.01) in column (IV).

## discussion
<!-- para 2 -->
Our results offer theoretical contributions to the political ideology and recall literatures, as well as confer important practical implications.
Research on political ideology is burgeoning, as scholars have investigated how the political leanings of organizational leaders influence various firm outcomes, including CSR initiatives (Chin et al. 2013), executive turnover after organizational misconduct (Park et al. 2020), executive compensation (Gupta and Wowak 2017, Gupta et al. 2017), and responsiveness to various forms of activism (Briscoe et al. 2014, Gupta and Briscoe 2020), among others.
No research of which we are aware has examined if or how the political ideology of a firm's TMT directly influences product quality—namely, recall count and time-to-recall.
Our study makes valuable theoretical contributions to the political ideology literature by being the first to demonstrate how the political leanings of the TMT influence product quality in general, and product recalls in particular.
Whether it is conscious or subconscious, the political bent of a firm's TMT leads to significant differences in serious product recalls in an extremely high-risk, high-reward industry.
Stated plainly, political ideology influences product safety.
<!-- para 3 -->
Our study also makes theoretical contributions to the recall literature.
Scholarship on organizational factors that influence recalls is sparse, but growing (Ball et al. 2022).
The limited research in this area has considered how CEO turnover (Mayo et al. 2022), CEO compensation (Wowak et al. 2015), and gender diversity on the board (Wowak et al. 2021) influence a firm's recall count and time-to-recall.
Our focus on how TMT political ideology impacts medical product recalls fills two important gaps in the recall domain.
The first is consideration of the TMT.
Past recall research has focused on the impact of a single person within the TMT—the CEO (Wowak et al. 2015, Mayo et al. 2022)—or on a different echelon—the board of directors (Wowak et al. 2021).
Both undoubtedly offer important insights, but they fail to capture the influence of executives who actually make product design and manufacturing decisions (which impact recall counts) and recall decisions (which impact the time-to-recall).
The second is the consideration of political ideology.
Given that executives are becoming increasingly more transparent about their political leanings (Wall Street Journal 2021) and product recalls are on the rise (Keller 2022), our study offers important and timely contributions to the recall literature by highlighting a relatively unknown and inherently intrinsic attribute of firm leaders that may play a pivotal role in medical product recalls.
<!-- para 4 -->
Our findings have important managerial implications for firms that operate in the high-risk, high-reward medical products industry.
One such implication is awareness.
Our study's findings can make senior leaders who oversee pivotal product design, manufacturing, and recall decisions more aware that their inherent political leanings may influence recall outcomes in previously unknown ways that may have benefits and downsides.
The key question is: what can firms do about this revelation?
Beyond awareness, political diversity within the TMT may be beneficial.
In post hoc analyses, we examine this implication and find that political diversity on the TMT is associated with the best of what TMT political ideology has to offer; fewer and faster recalls.
Thus, by balancing a firm's TMTs with both liberal and conservative senior leaders, they may be able to extract the benefits of each, which is a core managerial implication of our study.
A key reason for this finding may be that when the TMT comprises people on either end of the political ideology spectrum (i.e., high dispersion), they are more likely to have insightful discussions or debates around product quality investments and recall timing and are less likely to fall victim to groupthink than teams comprising people who have similar political ideologies (i.e., low dispersion).
With this in mind, boards may want to intentionally, albeit subtly, diversify the political ideology of the TMT to optimize the firm's recall count and recall timing behavior.
<!-- para 5 -->
Our research also offers implications to the FDA.
The FDA, like many federal agencies, has resource constraints and limitations regarding how much oversight or monitoring it can provide to any given organization.
Thus, a possible implication of discovering that firms with more liberal TMTs tend to manufacture higher-quality, less recall-prone medical products, whereas firms with more conservative TMTs tend to react more quickly when problems arise, could be that the FDA may be able to more precisely oversee medical product recalls.
Although this is certainly a potential implication of our research, additional nuances would need to be examined before the FDA were to use political donation data to guide policy.
This, however, presents an excellent opportunity for future research to build on our study.
<!-- para 6 -->
Our study is the first, to our knowledge, to bridge research on political ideology and product recalls, opening the door for new research.
For example, it may be valuable to examine if the role of politics in product recalls varies across industries.
Automobile or consumer products industries, two other recall-prone settings, may demonstrate similar or divergent results.
Our study, like all research, is not without limitations.
One limitation is that we only consider publicly traded firms.
Although this was needed in order to gather requisite data, it presents an opportunity for future research.
Similarly, although we undertake several steps to address concerns about biases from endogeneity, and although political ideology is not known to be notably fluid in individuals, all secondary data studies contain some risk of endogeneity.
Lastly, although we leveraged multiple data fields in the FAERS and MAUDE databases to pinpoint product quality-related adverse events, we cannot confirm with complete certainty that every single adverse event in our analysis is due exclusively to product quality issues, rather than a patient's underlying health condition or drug misuse (i.e., overdose).
Nonetheless, this study breaks new ground in exploring how political ideology influences areas of our life that we might be unaware—namely, the safety of medical products.

More specifically, openFDA has APIs for drug enforcement reports, which return data from the FDA’s Recall Enterprise System (RES), [^11] as well as APIs for medical device recalls, which return data from the FDA’s Device Recall Dataset.
Leveraging both data sources enabled us to extract as many NDCs, PMAs, and 510(k)s as possible, thus allowing us to determine the product(s) pertaining to each recall in our main sample.
Because the NDCs, PMAs, and 510(k)s are extremely unstandardized, we manually went through each NDC, PMA, and 510(k) to ensure that it was in the correct format and address all formatting issues.
