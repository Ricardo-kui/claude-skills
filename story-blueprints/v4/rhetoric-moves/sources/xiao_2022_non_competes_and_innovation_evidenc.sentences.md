# xiao_2022_non_competes_and_innovation_evidenc 句子库存

## introduction
<!-- para 2 -->
For firms in knowledge-intensive industries, employee mobility presents a major threat to their competitive advantages because the proprietary information about their distinctive, innovative ideas and practices is likely to be divulged by mobile employees at rival firms (Aime et al., 2010; Zander and Kogut, 1995).
Thus, it is common for firms to seek to prevent the mobility of key employees via non-compete agreements (henceforth non-competes) (Garmaise, 2011; Lavetti et al., 2020; Marx, 2011; Marx et al., 2009).
However, although non-competes help the firm protect existing, valuable knowledge assets from the risk of mobility-induced information spillover, whether such protection drives the firm to be more innovative remains an open question despite some studies with divergent arguments and mixed findings (Barnett and Sichelman, 2020; Conti, 2014; Garmaise, 2011; Gilson, 1999; Samila and Sorenson, 2011).
<!-- para 3 -->
The relationship between non-competes and innovation is theoretically ambiguous.
On the one hand, non-competes have long been criticized as an institutional impediment to innovation (Gilson, 1999; Marx et al., 2015; Samila and Sorenson, 2011).
For example, Gilson (1999) attributes the emergence of Silicon Valley as the world's preeminent hub for innovation to California's Business and Professions Code Section 16, 600—which precludes enforcement of non-competes—as the crucial "legal infrastructure" for knowledge to be disseminated across individuals and organizations and recombined in different, novel ways.
On the other hand, by foreclosing the mobility channel of competitive imitation (Zander and Kogut, 1995), non-competes may correct for market failure in the protection of innovative ideas and create incentives for firms to take more risks in R&D activities (Conti, 2014) and develop more innovations (Barnett and Sichelman, 2020).
<!-- para 4 -->
The controversy over the implications of non-competes for innovation can be further attributed to the empirical limitations of prior work.
Although existing studies have examined how non-competes affect R&D investments (Garmaise, 2011), new venture creation (Samila and Sorenson, 2011), and patent applications (Conti, 2014) as indirect proxies for innovations, they do not capture actual innovations.
According to the U.S. Small Business Administration, innovation is defined as “a process that begins with an invention, proceeds with the development of the invention, and results in the introduction of a new product, process or service to the marketplace” (Edwards and Gordon, 1984:1).
Thus, the ultimate goal of innovation is to introduce something new that can serve market needs (Acs and Audretsch, 1988).
Indeed, patents that embody new inventive knowledge have been widely used in the literature as a proxy for innovation but are also criticized as a flawed measure of innovation (Cohen et al., 2000; Fleming, 2002; Moser, 2005).
As Griliches (1990: 1669) concludes, “Ideally, we might hope that patent statistics would provide a measure of the (innovation) output...
The reality, however, is very far from it”.
This is consistent with Schumpeter’s (1939: 85) description: “The making of the invention and the carrying out of the corresponding innovations are, economically and sociologically, two entirely different things”.
<!-- para 5 -->
This study aims to address these issues from a new theoretical perspective in an empirical context of new product introductions.
To disentangle alternative mechanisms through which non-competes shape innovation in opposite ways, I draw from the exploitation-exploration literature (Benner, 2002; Benner and Tushman, 2003; Gilsing and Nootboom, 2006; Greve, 2007; Jansen et al., 2006; March 1991) and recognize the differential nature of innovations; an innovation is identified as exploitative if it builds on a firm's prior experience or competence and as exploratory if it involves a departure from a firm's existing knowledge base.
Empirically, I focus on the U.S. medical devices industry and obtain the measure of actual innovations by exploiting the regulatory context of this industry.
Medical device companies must obtain regulatory approval from the Food and Drug Administration (FDA) before introducing new products to the market.
Thus, FDA approvals of new medical devices can be regarded as an ex-post measure of innovations in the medical devices industry.
Moreover, medical devices are classified by the FDA into multiple specialty categories.
The classification system enables the identification of the exploitative or exploratory nature of new medical devices by contrasting the category of the new medical device introduced by the firm against the categories of the medical devices previously launched by the firm.
<!-- para 6 -->
To test how non-competes affect innovations depending on their exploitative or exploratory nature, I employ staggered legal changes regarding the enforceability of non-competes across the U.S. states between 1995 and 2010 as a natural experiment that introduces exogenous variations in the effectiveness of non-competes as the mobility restraint on employees.
The results show that an increase in the enforceability of non-competes promotes exploitative innovations but hinders exploratory innovations.
I conduct a battery of sensitivity tests and find consistent results.
<!-- para 7 -->
This paper contributes to a better understanding of how non-competes affect innovation.
By focusing on the exploitive or exploratory nature of innovations, this study identifies the knowledge content embodied in innovations as a key contingency for non-competes to affect different types of innovations in different ways.
Such a contingent perspective helps clarify the theoretical ambiguity regarding the alternative mechanisms underlying the opposite impacts of non-competes on innovation.
The findings that non-competes promote exploitative innovations but hinder exploratory innovations have implications for stimulating innovation from both policy-making and managerial perspectives.

## theory
<!-- para 3 -->
A non-compete is a restrictive covenant in the employment contract that prohibits an employee from entering into or starting a similar profession or trade in competition against the employee's former employer for a specified period after the employee leaves the firm.
Non-competes have been commonplace in the U.S., especially among those who hold key positions at firms, such as technical workers (Lavetti et al., 2020; Marx, 2011) and senior managers (Garmaise, 2011).
As shown in a survey conducted by Bishara et al. (2015), approximately 80% of CEO employment contracts of S&P1500 firms contain non-competes.
<!-- para 4 -->
Non-competes can prohibit employee mobility by increasing the cost of mobility for employees.
To avoid breach of a non-compete, the departing employee has to either move to a different industry or stay unemployed until the non-compete expires.
However, both options are costly.
On the one hand, as an employee moves to another industry, her existing knowledge or skills that she has accumulated in the previous industry become less relevant, reducing her competitiveness in her new workplace.
Balasubramanian et al. (2020) show that employees whose career paths are constrained by non-competes have lower compensation and slower career progression than their counterparts whose career choices are not so restricted.
On the other hand, staying unemployed is not realistic because non-competes may last for several years (Bishara et al., 2015).
Thus, the high cost of mobility reduces employees' incentive to depart from their current employers.
<!-- para 5 -->
Despite the ubiquity of non-competes, whether they can effectively prohibit mobility eventually depends on their enforceability, which is determined by state law.
The landscape for non-compete enforceability is rugged and in flux.
For example, California is well-known for prohibiting employers from enforcing non-competes according to California's Business and Profession Code Section 16,600, which states that "every contract by which any one is restrained from engaging in a lawful profession, trade, or business of any kind is to that extent void." Moreover, the enforceability of non-competes may vary over time as state law changes (Ewens and Marx, 2018; Marx et al., 2009).
Using Michigan's legal change in 1985 as a natural experiment, Marx et al. (2009) show that the state law regarding the enforceability of non-competes is an important institutional condition that shapes labor mobility across firms.
<!-- para 7 -->
Despite extensive studies, how non-competes affect innovation remains ambiguous.
On the one hand, legal research has long cautioned that non-competes may limit free knowledge flow (Gilson, 1999).
Because knowledge is embodied in individuals, limited mobility of people results in limited mobility of knowledge.
Building on this insight, Samila and Sorenson (2011) find that non-competes hinder the creation of new ventures by limiting the mobility of individuals with novel ideas.
Moreover, Marx et al. (2015) show that after Michigan's legal change in 1985 that strengthened the enforceability of non-competes, firms in Michigan experienced increased difficulty in attracting external talents because the new state law induced external talents to anticipate greater restrictions on their post-employment career flexibility.
Because successful innovations hinge on the assimilation and recombination of new knowledge (Katila and Ahuja, 2002; Leiponen and Helfat, 2010; Rosenkopf and Nerkar, 2001), non-competes may hinder innovation by limiting the firm's access to the external knowledge embodied in individuals outside the firm.
<!-- para 8 -->
On the other hand, non-competes may also promote innovation by limiting knowledge spillover.
Knowledge spillover has an unfavorable outcome; if a firm's proprietary knowledge embodied in its distinctive, innovative ideas and practices is obtained by rivals, then the firm cannot fully reap the profits from its innovations because rival firms may take similar actions or provide similar offerings (Katz and Shapiro, 1987).
The concern over the reduced return on innovation in the presence of knowledge spillover harms the firm's incentive to innovate in the first place (Pisano, 2006).
Research shows that employee mobility is a key channel of knowledge spillover; the employee who moves from one firm to another may disclose proprietary information about her former employer at new workplace (Aime et al., 2010; Zander and Kogut, 1995).
Thus, by limiting the free mobility of employees, non-competes help firms protect their knowledge assets from the risk of knowledge spillovers, which potentially corrects for the disincentive to innovate.
In line with this expectation, Conti (2014) finds that firms are more likely to invest in risky R&D projects when non-competes are more enforceable.
<!-- para 9 -->
Whereas prior studies have shown different channels through which non-competes may hinder or promote innovation, which channel dominates and how they interact remain unanswered.
This study aims to answer these questions by drawing from the notion of exploitation and exploration (Benner, 2002; Benner and Tushman, 2003; Gilsing and Nootboom, 2006; Greve, 2007; Jansen et al., 2006; March 1991), which provides a unified theoretical framework to recognize the dual role of non-competes in limiting both knowledge inflow and outflow and clarifies how the impact of non-competes on innovation varies with the knowledge content (i.e., exploitative or exploratory) of innovation.
<!-- para 11 -->
The notion of exploitation and exploration was developed to theorize two broad gestalts of organizational behaviors (March 1991).
Exploitation refers to execution and refinement of the organization's existing competencies, while exploration refers to experimentation with new, alternative practices to expand the organization's competence repertoire.
While exploitation is usually preferred to exploration because exploitation engenders less uncertainty and generates returns more immediately than exploration (Ahuja and Lampert, 2001; Levinthal and March 1993), exploration is also an important channel for organizations to renew capabilities to cope with the ever-changing environment that may render the organization's existing competencies obsolescent (Benner and Tushman, 2003).
Accordingly, those new products or services that are essentially improvements of existing offerings and are developed to meet the needs of existing customers can be regarded as exploitative innovations, while those new products or services that depart from a firm's traditional expertise and are developed to explore new markets can be regarded as exploratory innovations (Danneels, 2002; Jansen et al., 2006).
<!-- para 12 -->
The distinction between exploitative innovation and exploratory innovation helps disentangle the intervened mechanisms through which non-competes may affect innovation in opposite ways by recognizing the knowledge content of innovation as a key contingency.
On the one hand, non-competes promote exploitative innovations by protecting the firm's existing knowledge base—the source of its exploitative innovations.
When non-competes are strictly enforced, the firm can retain existing, key employees effectively by enforcing their non-competes (Marx et al., 2009) and successively harness their knowledge and skills in organizational tasks, such as innovation activities, without much concern about the risk of mobility-induced knowledge spillover that erodes the value of the firm's existing knowledge base.
Moreover, the firm's knowledge resides in not only its employees but also their established pattern of coordination and interaction (Kogut and Zander, 1992; Levitt and March 1988; Reagans et al., 2005); the employees who have been familiar with each other's expertise and role in the intra-firm network can build on such common, collective knowledge and cooperate with one another efficiently.
Because innovation requires a joint effort of multiple individuals through deliberate coordination and cooperation (Eisenhardt and Tabrizi, 1995; Faraj and Sproull, 2000; Tatikonda and Montoya-Weiss, 2001), the knowledge about one another—which emerges from the experience of repeatedly working together—can substantially affect the productivity of the firm's innovation activities (Jain, 2013).
Consistent with this view, Tzabbar and Kehoe (2014) find that employees' outbound mobility is followed by a decline in the firm's exploitative innovations because the remaining employees cannot further exploit the knowledge embodied in their former colleagues and prior organizational procedures in subsequent innovation activities.
Therefore, by insulating the firm's existing knowledge base from being deprived or disrupted by employee mobility, non-competes create a favorable condition for the firm to develop exploitative innovations by continually building on its existing knowledge.
Because the effectiveness of non-competes in prohibiting employee mobility is contingent on their enforceability as determined by state law, the prediction is that the extent to which non-competes promote the firm's exploitative innovations may increase as non-competes can be enforced more strictly.
<!-- para 13 -->
**H1.** The enforceability of non-competes is positively associated with the firm's exploitative innovations.
<!-- para 14 -->
On the other hand, non-competes hinder exploratory innovations by precluding the firm from exploring external new knowledge.
Because external talents are reluctant to join the firm when they perceive a high risk that their post-employment mobility will be restricted by non-competes (Marx et al., 2015), firms in the state in which non-competes are strictly enforced are less able to obtain the knowledge embodied in external talents through the “learning-by-hiring” approach (Rosenkopf and Almeida, 2003; Tzabbar, 2009; Zucker and Darby, 1997).
Given that the firm’s ability to understand and assimilate external new knowledge hinges on its possession of prior related knowledge (Cohen and Levinthal, 1990), the firm that lacks adequate exposure to external new knowledge may not be able to realize the need to explore the external, unfamiliar knowledge space.
Moreover, research shows that although the firm may tend to prioritize the exploitation of existing knowledge or competencies over the exploration of the unknown, unfamiliar alternatives, employee mobility—either inbound or outbound—helps the firm overcome the tendency to exploit by introducing disruptions to its existing knowledge base and forcing it to explore new ideas or practices in order to accommodate the personnel change and ensuing organizational structural change (Jain, 2016; Tzabbar and Kehoe, 2014).
However, such a mechanism to stimulate new knowledge exploration is suppressed if the firm can strictly enforce non-competes and effectively prohibit employee mobility.
Accordingly, I predict that the firm is less prone to explore external new knowledge that departs from its existing knowledge base because its non-competes can be enforced more strictly, leading to fewer exploratory innovations.
<!-- para 15 -->
**H2.** The enforceability of non-competes is negatively associated with the firm's exploratory innovations.

## methods
<!-- para 3 -->
In this study, these hypotheses are tested in the context of medical device companies, a highly knowledge-intensive context in which firms compete to successfully introduce new products in response to the rapidly changing technological landscape and market demands (Barbash and Glied, 2010; Perednia and Allen, 1995).
Therefore, non-competes that considerably affect inventors' mobility (Marx et al., 2009) and knowledge flow within and across states (Marx et al., 2015; Samila and Sorenson, 2011) strongly impact the innovation activities of medical device companies.
Moreover, research suggests that the healthcare sector is a typical context in which non-competes are widely used to restrict employee mobility and prevent knowledge spillover to competitors (Lavetti et al., 2020).
Finally, the development of medical devices is subject to regulatory scrutiny and approval, which guarantees the availability and reliability of the information on the measure of innovations—FDA approvals of new medical devices.
In line with prior research (Coad and Rao, 2008; Theeke et al., 2018), I identify medical device companies according to Standard Industrial Classification (SIC) codes and focus on U.S. listed firms corresponding to SIC 384.
<!-- para 4 -->
The initial sample contains all the medical device companies listed on the U.S. stock exchanges with available financial information in the CRSP-COMPUSTAT merged database.
Information on new product approvals by the FDA is obtained from the Center for Devices and Radiological Health (CDRH), which provides the list of all FDA approvals from the 510(k) and premarket approval (PMA) processes from 1900.
Then, I manually match the CDRH data to the CRSP-COMPUSTAT merged database by company names.
Following Ewens and Marx (2018), I focus on the legal changes between 1995 and 2016 that resulted in variation in non-compete enforceability across U.S. states.
I also obtain patent data from the United States Patent and Trademark Office (USPTO) to control each firm's technological capability.
I match patent data to firms according to the concordance table compiled by Arora et al. (2021), who match patents granted through 2015 to firms in the CRSP database.
Although patents can be used to capture firms' technological capability, the patent-firm concordance table is subject to right truncation bias (i.e., fewer patent applications are included near 2015 because some patents are still under review at the USPTO until 2015, but the concordance table only covers those patents that had been granted through 2015).
The right truncation of patent applications will result in measurement errors of firms' technological capability near 2015 and biased estimation results.
Following prior work facing a similar issue (e.g., Phelps, 2010), I minimize the impact of right truncation bias by focusing only on patents that were filed through 2010, which leaves sufficient time for those patent applications to be examined and granted by the USPTO and included in the patent-firm concordance table.
<!-- para 5 -->
I exert particular caution in identifying the historical locations of firms.
Specifically, I use the original annual financial statements (i.e., Form 10-K) filed by firms with the Securities and Exchange Commission (SEC) to identify their historical location.
The original financial statements are obtained from the SEC's EDGAR database.
Although several commonly used databases such as CRSP and COMPUSTAT also report the locations of firms, they usually report only their most recent location; however, a firm may move from one place to another through its lifespan.
Unlike those popular databases, a firm's original SEC filings contain accurate information on its location for each reporting period.
This approach enables accurate identification of each firm's historical location and the impact of local legal change.
After retrieving the historical locations of firms, I further obtain state-level economic and demographic statistics from the Bureau of Economic Analysis and the Census Bureau.
The final sample consists of an unbalanced panel that includes 357 medical device companies and 2513 firm-year observations between 1995 and 2010.
<!-- para 8 -->
Many studies on innovation use patent counts as a proxy for innovation because of the lack of information on actual new product introductions.
However, patents are only intermediate products of the innovation process and are criticized for their limitations in measuring innovation (Arundel and Kabla, 1998; Griliches, 1990; Moser, 2005).
I focus on the medical device industry and directly measure each firm's new product introductions by the number of FDA approvals issued for new medical devices.
<!-- para 9 -->
Since this study distinguishes between exploitative and exploratory innovations, I decouple the aggregate measure of innovations—the total number of new medical devices introduced by a firm—into categories of exploitative and exploratory innovations by referring to the FDA classification scheme for medical devices.
In the U.S., medical devices are classified according to their descriptions according to Title 21 of the Code of Federal Regulations (CFR), Parts 862–892.
Under the CFR, medical devices are grouped into 16 medical specialties referred to as "panels," such as "cardiovascular" or "dental".
Each panel is overseen by a specialized advisory committee consisting of experts with recognized expertise and judgment.
This classification scheme is a critical component of the regulation because it determines the technical class of a device and its regulatory class.
If a device is classified as Class I or II, it must be marketed via the premarket notification pathway according to Section 510(k) of the Food, Drug and Cosmetic Act.
For Class III devices, PMA is normally required.
Accordingly, the classification scheme assures that applications for introducing new medical devices to the market are reviewed by experts who have relevant expertise and can assess the safety and effectiveness of such devices while following prescribed regulatory procedures.
<!-- para 10 -->
The classification scheme provides a way to determine whether a new medical device is primarily exploitative or exploratory.
Specifically, I define a new medical device as exploratory innovation if its medical specialty is new to the firm; otherwise, I define it as an exploitative innovation.
For instance, if a firm has never offered cardiovascular devices previously but introduces a cardiovascular device in year t, this cardiovascular device represents the firm's exploratory innovation in year t because it explores a new knowledge domain (i.e., cardiovascular device) that is distinct from its existing knowledge or prior experience in other knowledge domains.
This operationalization is consistent with the definition of exploration as experimentation with alternative ideas or practices that significantly depart from one's existing competence repertoire (Benner and Tushman, 2003; March 1991).
For instance, in 2003, Anika Therapeutics introduced a new orthopedic device ORTHOVISC, and this is the first time in the company's history to introduce an orthopedic device.
Because Anika Therapeutics had never introduced orthopedic devices before 2003, ORTHOVISC counts as an exploratory innovation of this company in 2003.
On the other hand, launching a new product within a domain in which a firm has prior experience indicates the firm's exploitation of the existing knowledge and expertise that it has accumulated in the past.
After identifying the exploitative or exploratory nature of each new medical device introduced by the firm, the number of exploitative and exploratory innovations introduced by the firm each year is counted.
<!-- para 12 -->
Following Ewens and Marx (2018), I focus on the legislative, judicial, or policy changes across states that resulted in variations in the enforceability of non-competes.
Specifically, during the sample period of this study, the enforceability of non-competes was strengthened in six states, including Florida (1996), Ohio (2004), Vermont (2005), Idaho (2008), Wisconsin (2009), and Georgia (2010), and was weakened in three states, including Louisiana (2001), Oregon (2008), and South Carolina (2010).
Following the literature (Conti, 2014; Ewens and Marx, 2018; Garmaise, 2011), I use a three-level  $(-1, 0, 1)$ measure to indicate the local legal change's implication for the enforceability of non-competes, where  $-1$ indicates that the legal change reduced the enforceability of non-competes and 1 indicates that the legal change increased the enforceability of non-competes.
For example, for Florida, this measure equals 0 for years through 1995 and 1 for years since 1996.
For those states that did not experience relevant legal changes, this measure equals 0 throughout the sample period.
<!-- para 14 -->
A series of covariates may be correlated with the variation in non-compete enforceability and innovation.
Firm size is measured as the natural logarithm of total assets.
Return on assets (ROA) is calculated as net income divided by total assets.
Since a firm's capital structure may influence its tradeoff between exploitation and exploration (Choi et al., 2016), I include the leverage ratio, which is calculated as total liabilities divided by total assets.
I control for each firm's financial slack measured as its cash and cash equivalents scaled by its total assets (Bruneel et al., 2016).
R&D intensity is measured as R&D expenses divided by sales.
Patent base, which is measured as the natural logarithm of one plus the total number of patents filed by the firm by the end of the focal year, captures the firm's technological capability (Silverman, 1999).
Diversification is the entropy index to indicate the distribution of the firm's sales across industries at the four-digit SIC level (Palepu, 1985).
Moreover, the firm's innovation behavior changes as it ages (Sorensen and Stuart, 2000).
Because information about the firm's actual founding year is not available, I use the number of years since the firm has been listed in the CRSP database as the proxy for firm age.
In addition to these firm-level variables, I include state-level covariates to control for the local socioeconomic environment, such as the GDP growth rate by state (GDP growth); population, which is measured as the natural logarithm of year-by-year state-level estimated population; median age of the population by state in each year (median age); local female-to-male ratio (sex ratio); educational attainment (education), which is measured as the proportion of the local population with a bachelor's degree; and state-level unemployment rate (unemployment).
A series of year dummies are included to control for time effects.
<!-- para 16 -->
I test the hypotheses using the ordinary least squares (OLS) model with firm fixed effects, which help control for unobserved, time-invariant firm heterogeneity and enhances the validity of the empirical results.
I adopt the following model to estimate the impacts of changes in local non-compete enforceability on different types of innovations:
<!-- para 17 -->
$$
I_{i,t}=f(E_{i,t},\mathbf{X}_{i,t},F_{i},T_{i},e_{i,t})
$$
<!-- para 18 -->
where I is the measure of exploitative or exploratory innovations, E is the explanatory variable of interest (i.e., enforceability increase), X is a vector of control variables, and F and T are firm and year fixed effects, respectively.
To account for the serial correlation of state-level variations in the enforceability of non-competes, standard errors are clustered by state.
While the current fixed-effect OLS model helps control unobserved firm heterogeneity and offers a clear interpretation of estimates, one potential concern is that the linear model specification does not account for the count characteristics of dependent variables.
Therefore, I further test the robustness of the results by using negative binomial models with industry dummies in additional analyses.

I test H1 in Model 2, where the dependent variable is the number of exploitative innovations.
Consistent with H1, the coefficient of enforceability is positive and statistically significant ( $\beta = 1.158$ and  $p < .05$), suggesting that an increase in the enforceability of non-competes is followed by an increase in exploitative innovations.
Specifically, the number of exploitative innovations of an average firm increases from 1.8 to 3 following an increase in the enforceability of non-competes.
I test H2 in Model 3, where the dependent variable is the number of exploratory innovations.
The coefficient of enforceability is negative and statistically significant ( $\beta = -0.224$ and  $p < .05$), suggesting that an increase in non-compete enforceability is followed by a decrease in exploratory innovations.
This finding shows that the legal change that increases local non-compete enforceability decreases the number of exploratory innovations of an average firm from 0.1 to 0.
Therefore, H2 is also supported.
<!-- para 15 -->
Altogether, the results of Models 2–3 jointly illustrate the mechanism underlying the positive relationship between non-compete enforceability and total innovations as depicted in Model 1.
In a regime in which non-competes are strictly enforced, firms are induced to focus on exploitative innovations rather than exploratory innovations.
Because exploitation is more cost-efficient, less difficult, and less uncertain than exploration, the shift in attention from exploratory innovations to exploitative innovations allows firms to deploy their resources more efficiently and present higher productivity in producing total innovations.

Although the previous analyses support both hypotheses, the empirical strategy hinges on the parallel trend assumption; that is, the outcome variables of the treatment and control groups must follow a similar trend before treatment.
Following prior studies (e.g., Kong and Qin, 2021), I conduct a formal test of the validity of the parallel trend assumption.
Specifically, I use a series of time variables, namely, Year-5, Year-4, Year-3, Year-2, Year-1, Year 0, Year+1, Year+2, Year+3, Year+4, Year+5, Year+6 and afterward to indicate the years relative to the year of local legal change.
Although prior studies typically use dummy variables to indicate the time before and after the treatment, it is inapplicable here because the treatment variable in this paper has three values (i.e., -1, 0, 1).
To accommodate the three-value treatment variable, I assign -1, 0, and 1 to those time variables depending on the outcome of the local legal change.
For example, Year -1 equals 1 for firms in Florida in 1995 and 0 otherwise because the legal change in Florida in 1996 resulted in an increase in the enforceability of non-competes.
Then, I replace enforceability increase with these time variables and re-test hypotheses with all the control variables previously included.
The coefficients on these time variables represent the differences between firms eventually affected by the legal change in year t (i.e., treatment firms) and other firms not affected by the legal change in year t (i.e., control firms) in their innovation outputs before and after the legal change in year t.

Exploitative innovations are less sensitive than exploratory innovations to the change in the enforceability of non-competes, probably because of the natural differences in the processes of exploitative and exploratory innovations.
Exploitative innovations hinge on the recombination of the firm's existing knowledge elements.
However, there is a natural upper limit of the maximal ways to recombine knowledge elements within the firm (Katila and Ahuja, 2002).
Therefore, even when the legal change creates a favorable environmental condition for the firm to further exploit its existing knowledge, the firm needs time to develop new ways to recombine existing knowledge elements because the easiest or most apparent methods of knowledge combinations may have been used in the past, and the rest of the possibilities are more difficult.
In contrast, exploratory innovations hinge on the knowledge new to the firm and require the firm to overcome the natural tendency to exploit in the first place.
However, because the increase in the enforceability of non-competes facilitates the firm to continuously exploit its existing knowledge, the firm's incentive to explore external new knowledge drops sharply, leading to an immediate decrease in exploratory innovations.

## discussion
<!-- para 2 -->
Non-competes have long been of interest to academics and practitioners because of their numerous implications for job mobility, competitive dynamics, and economic development at large (US Treasury, 2016).
Despite extensive research, to date, there is no consensus on the social desirability of non-competes with respect to innovation.
This study reconciles the theoretical ambiguity by introducing the notion of exploitation and exploration and shows that the impact of non-competes on innovation is contingent on the exploitative or exploratory nature of the innovation.
Using staggered legal changes regarding the enforceability of non-competes across U.S. states between 1995 and 2010 as a natural experiment and FDA approvals of new medical devices as a product-level measure of innovations, I find that an increase in the enforceability of non-competes increases local firms' exploitative innovations but reduces their exploratory innovations.
<!-- para 3 -->
This work adds to the existing literature on non-competes by showing their contingent effect on different types of innovations.
In contrast to prior studies that focus on indirect indicators of innovations such as new ventures (Samila and Sorenson, 2011) and patents (Conti, 2014), this study revisits the definition of innovation as “the introduction of a new product, process or service to the marketplace” (Edwards and Gordon, 1984:1), disentangles the intertwined mechanisms through which non-competes may promote or hinder innovation, and clarifies how legal institutions regarding non-compete enforcement shape geographical variation in the composition of innovations.
<!-- para 4 -->
The findings of this work also reveal a caveat in innovation research at large.
Specifically, “how innovative a firm is” is a longstanding question for researchers, managers, investors, government officials, etc., and this question is often reduced to compare the count of innovation outputs across firms.
However, few works have examined whether or not the assumption underlying such inference holds, namely, whether the count of a firm’s innovation outputs can reflect its genuine innovativeness.
In this study, I find that sample firms introduce more innovations following an increase in local non-compete enforceability and that such an increase in the number of innovations is driven by the increase in exploitative innovations that outweighs the decrease in exploratory innovations.
This result suggests that some firms seem more innovative than others in terms of the quantity of innovation outputs not because they are more active in pursuing novelty; in contrast, they are just more efficient in resource allocation by exploiting existing knowledge and experience without taking much risk to explore unknown knowledge terrains.
Consequently, this work highlights the caution of examining the nature of the innovation activity of the firm to better understand how innovative the firm actually is.
<!-- para 5 -->
It is interesting that the findings of this paper appear to run counter to some prior work, such as Conti (2014), who argues that stricter noncompete enforcement induces firms to invest more extensively in riskier R&D projects.
Given that exploratory innovation is normally more difficult and more uncertain than exploitative innovation, one would expect that an increase in non-compete enforceability may trigger more exploratory innovation.
That is not what I find in this work, and there are reasons for this discrepancy.
First, not all risky projects are exploratory; indeed, projects that heavily build on a firm's existing but obsolete competencies are also likely to fail because they are not valued on the market.
Second, Conti (2014) relies on patent data, while this work focuses on new products.
Given that innovation is a joint effort of multiple individuals through deliberate coordination and cooperation (Eisenhardt and Tabrizi, 1995; Faraj and Sproull, 2000; Tatikonda and Montoya-Weiss, 2001) but patents can be produced by an individual inventor alone (Singh and Fleming, 2010), the results of these two studies are not directly comparable.
For example, for medical device companies, successful application of a new patent requires the idea embodied in the patent to be novel and technically superior; however, successful development of a new medical device also requires the firm to be responsive to uncertainty in physicians' and patients' demands (Herzlinger, 2006), safety issues (Maisel, 2005), and regulatory requirements (Stern, 2017), in addition to technical novelty or superiority.
<!-- para 6 -->
This study also has some practical implications.
First, it shows that non-competes promote exploitation, stifle exploration, and result in an increase in productivity in the context of innovation.
However, in the long run, a firm's growth requires a balance between exploitation and exploration (He and Wong, 2004; Katila and Ahuja, 2002).
Therefore, the management of firms in an employer-friendly legal regime must recognize the risk of such an institution-induced "productivity dilemma" (Benner and Tushman, 2003) and take action to counteract the inertia toward over-exploitation.
Second, this work calls for more caution in policy making and evaluation.
Since exploitation is less difficult and more cost-efficient than exploration (March 1991), one possible outcome of legal constraints on labor mobility is that firms located in areas where non-competes are strictly enforced may appear more productive than firms located in areas where non-competes are not strictly enforced in terms of the number of innovation outputs, as shown in this paper.
However, in employer-friendly regimes, such improved innovation productivity is achieved at the cost of the inability, or reluctance, to search for new, alternative ideas or practices.
<!-- para 7 -->
This study has some limitations, which may inspire future research.
First, the hypotheses of this work are tested solely in the context of the medical devices industry.
While this empirical context allows me to obtain the direct measure of innovations—the FDA approvals of new medical devices—which contributes to the existing related literature that primarily relies on indirect indicators of innovations, this work does not take into account contextual differences across industries.
While the results in this work still apply to other contexts, the magnitude of the impact of non-compete enforcement on employee mobility may vary across industries (Starr et al., 2018).
For example, in an industry in which employees' knowledge or skills are highly industry-specific (e.g., the aerospace industry), non-competes are extremely effective because employees can find alternative employment opportunities in other industries that can leverage their existing knowledge or skills.
Thus, the impacts of non-competes on exploitative and exploratory innovations may be even stronger in such an industry environment.
Future research may explore how industry conditions matter in the relationship between non-compete enforcement and different types of innovations.
<!-- para 8 -->
Second, to exploit state-level variations in non-compete enforceability, this work only focuses on U.S.-listed firms.
This sampling scheme precludes any confounders related to cross-country institutional differences; however, a generalizability issue arises.
In other words, whether the findings of this study can apply to other countries with less-developed legal systems or labor markets is questionable.
While this issue is beyond the scope of this study, exploring cross-country differences can be a future direction to extend this work.
<!-- para 9 -->
Third, this work identifies treatment firms according to the locations of their headquarters.
Although I exert additional caution in using this identification strategy by collecting the location information of firms from their historical SEC filings, firms may distribute their innovation activities across different states and countries and are affected by the institutional environments of different places.
However, it is difficult to identify the actual geographical distribution of firms' innovation activities for several reasons.
First, information about the location of every member of the new product development team is not available.
Although information about the locations of patent inventors is available at the USPTO and can be used as a proxy for the geographical distribution of inventive activity, the degree to which a firm's inventive activities overlap with the new product development activities is unknown.
For instance, for a firm that produces both cardiovascular and neurological devices, the inventor of the cardiovascular technologies may not be involved in the product development team of new neurological devices.
Moreover, information about the location of patent inventors is only available for patents that have been filed.
For instance, a firm may introduce a new product without filing any new patent in that year.
Finally, identifying the location of each firm according to its headquarters is practically valid.
Research has shown that in established firms, such as publicly listed firms, their innovation activities are embedded in highly structured organizational processes, where the coordination pattern among organizational members and the resource allocation in research projects are subject to the decisions of top management (Khanna et al., 2016).
Therefore, although specific works in new product development can be distributed across different states, the firm's top managers are inevitably affected by the enforceability of the non-compete in the state in which the firm is headquartered.
Moreover, despite the complexity of the choice of law, it is a common practice for firms to resort to courts in states in which they are headquartered because they have been familiar with the court-specific judicial procedures (Sanga, 2014).
For example, before Kai-Fu Lee left Microsoft to Google, he was involved in several projects in different states.
However, when Microsoft applied for legal injunction to enforce Lee's non-compete in 2005, Microsoft filed the lawsuit in the Superior Court of the State of Washington, as Microsoft was headquartered in Washington.
Nonetheless, I believe that it would be better if future research could identify the actual geographic location of each firm's innovation activities because doing so would further advance our understanding of the institutional and geographical determinants of innovation.
