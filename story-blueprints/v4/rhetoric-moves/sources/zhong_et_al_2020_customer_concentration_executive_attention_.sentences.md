# zhong_et_al_2020_customer_concentration_executive_attention_ 句子库存

## introduction
<!-- para 2 -->
Innovation is key to the sustainable competitive advantage of firms (Ahuja, Lampert, & Tandon, 2008), and firms innovate through the recombination of knowledge components (Fleming, 2001; Katila & Ahuja, 2002).
Scholars maintain that deep, local searches, the recombination of existing components (i.e., search depth) (Helfat, 1994; Levinthal & March, 1993), and extensive exploration of new, distant knowledge (i.e., search breadth) (Ahuja & Lampert, 2001) are crucial for innovation.
However, according to General Electric Company's 2016 Global Innovation Barometer report, which surveyed 2,748 business executives across 23 countries, about 60% of senior executives favor "safe innovation," wherein they prefer their firms to search locally and cater to existing operations and customers.
Why do firms continue to practice such disproportionate allocation of resources between search depth and search breadth? [^1]
<!-- para 3 -->
The literature provides two important, but distinct views on why firms can be myopic in their search efforts: the capability view (Ahuja & Lampert, 2001; Gilbert, 2005) and the cognition view (Gavetti & Levinthal, 2000; Hodgkinson, 1997, 2015; Hodgkinson & Healey, 2011; Ocasio, 1997, 2011; Porac, Thomas, & Baden-Fuller, 1989).
According to the capability view, firms are backward-looking and fail to adapt to new technology because of the nature of their resources, competencies, and capabilities.
Perhaps the most notable conclusion of this stream of research is the notion of a “competence trap” (Levitt & March, 1988) wherein core capabilities may turn into core rigidities, thereby inhibiting innovation (Leonard-Barton, 1992).
In contrast, the cognition view suggests that firms are forward-looking and focus more on the roles of managerial attention and cognition.
The key notion is that of “attention traps” (Johnson & Hoopes, 2003; Kaplan, 2008; Ocasio, 1997), contending that myopic search is the result of cognitive inertia, or the imperfect, boundedly rational, and constrained cognitive representations of managers.
<!-- para 4 -->
Despite being highly insightful, the two research streams have developed separately with little interaction. “Capability” scholars often fail to consider the role of cognition, and “cognition” scholars treat capabilities as backgrounds (Eggers & Kaplan, 2013).
More importantly, both views concentrate on “supply-side” dynamics but neglect the effects of “demand-side” factors on the cognitive representations of top executives (Hodgkinson, 2015;
Ocasio, 1997; Rosa, Porac, Runser-Spanjol, & Saxon, 1999) and their allocation of resources (Adner & Levinthal, 2001; Priem, Li, & Carr, 2012).
As a result, we still know very little about whether and how the demand environment affects firm search behavior.
<!-- para 5 -->
In this study, we seek to address this gap by exploring how demand-side factors affect firm search behavior via executive attention.
Specifically, we posit that customers are key to an organization's demand environment (Adner & Zemsky, 2006; Priem et al., 2012).
When a firm's performance is attributed to only a few major customers (i.e., customer concentration) (Patatoukas, 2012), the benefits of ensuring continued profits lead top executives to develop an existing-oriented attention that confirms the correctness of the existing assessment of the environment, capabilities, and management practices (Danneels, 2003; Ocasio, 1997).
Such attention further drives top executives to match their firms' capabilities with the perceived opportunities in the demand environment (Hodgkinson & Healey, 2011).
In so doing, firms with a high customer concentration are more likely to engage in a myopic enactment process in which self-validating and compelling interpretations of the environment firm up the current foci of attention.
This further drives top executives to commit to exploiting familiar domains and constraining broad searches.
<!-- para 6 -->
Furthermore, firms do not search in isolation, and attention is embedded in certain industry and social structures (Gavetti, Levinthal, & Ocasio, 2007; Ocasio, 2011).
The attention-based view “differs from and extends Simon’s (1947) original formulation [of structure and cognition] by providing an explicit treatment of attentional processing as a multilevel process shaped by individuals, organizations, and the environment” (Ocasio, 1997, p. 188).
Therefore, we develop moderators at the organizational (performance feedback) and environmental (product market competition) levels that may either reinforce or restrain executive attention, thereby generating variations in the relationship between customer concentration and search behavior.
<!-- para 7 -->
We test our hypotheses using firms' patent activities from 1976 to 2005, and the results largely support our conjectures.
Our study contributes to extant literature in several ways.
First, we provide novel evidence on how customers affect firm search behavior and offer a demand-side explanation for why firms search locally.
Second, we demonstrate that executive attention channels the stimuli of the demand environment to search behavior, contributing to the cognition literature by foregrounding the demand-side factors and expanding the demand-based view (DBV) of how customers affect firm heterogeneity.
Third, our study brings the supply-side managerial aspirations on performance and beliefs regarding market competition into theorizing of nuanced effects of customer concentration, thereby providing a more comprehensive understanding of the interaction between supply- and demand-side factors in explaining organizational search, learning, and adaption.

## theory
<!-- para 2 -->
Organizations learn “by encoding inferences from history into routines that guide behavior” (Levitt & March, 1988, p. 320).
Central to this learning process is the organization’s search effort, which can vary with respect to two dimensions: search breadth, which is how widely a firm searches for new knowledge, and search depth, which is how deeply a firm exploits or reuses its previously accumulated knowledge (Katila & Ahuja, 2002).
Search breadth helps firms gain knowledge from multiple, diverse domains, thereby enhancing their ability to recognize more opportunities.
In contrast, search depth facilitates the acquisition of specialized expertise required to resolve complex or unusual problems in a specific field (Leiponen & Helfat, 2010).
Although both types of searches are essential for organizations, firms often devote disproportionate efforts to local and deep search.
This phenomenon has led to a stream of research (Ahuja & Lampert, 2001; Levinthal & March, 1993; Helfat, 1994) centering on an important question: why do firms commit to a status quo, search locally, and frequently fail to adapt?
To answer this question, studies have progressively focused on two research streams: the capability view and the cognition view.
<!-- para 4 -->
The “capabilities” stream of research focuses on resources and capabilities as a source of inertia or local search (Gavetti & Levinthal, 2000).
As Levinthal and March (1993) argued, “As they [firms] develop greater and greater competence at a particular activity, they engage in that activity more, thus further increasing competence and the opportunity cost of exploration” (p. 106).
Therefore, prior experience and competencies (Ahuja & Lampert, 2001; Helfat, 1994; de Figueiredo, Rawley, & Rider, 2015), complementary assets (Christensen, 1997), and patterns of resource investment (Ahuja & Katila, 2004; Gilbert, 2005) leading to current competitive advantages, may constrain firms’ future search behavior, and drive them to fall into a competence trap (Leonard-Barton, 1992).
However, this research fails to consider how managerial cognition affects organizational search (Eggers & Kaplan, 2013) and neglects the role of demand-side factors (Adner & Levinthal, 2001; Priem et al., 2012).
<!-- para 6 -->
The “cognition” research stream contends that the environment is socially constructed by managerial attention and cognition (Eggers & Kaplan, 2013; Ocasio, 1997; Porac et al., 1989), which serve as filters that direct attention toward certain features of the environment at the expense of others, until rendering managers incapable of revising their mental models to adapt to the environment (Hodgkinson, 1997; Peteraf & Shanley, 1997; Porac et al., 1995).
Eggers and Kaplan (2009) empirically demonstrated that firms enter new industries much slower if their CEOs focus on existing technologies.
Similarly, Gavetti and Levinthal (2000) showed that while cognitive representations facilitate experiential search in a particular direction, they also constrain explorative search in other directions.
In contrast, if the attention structure of the top management is broadened to include new and unfamiliar terrains (Li, Maggitti, Smith, Tesluk, & Katila, 2013), the firm is more likely to search broadly for fresh ideas.
A deficiency of this stream is that it also neglects “consumers and producers as networked actors who are bound together in equivocal transactions that are stabilized by their shared assumptions and frames of reference” (Rosa et al., 1999, p. 66).
<!-- para 8 -->
The DBV proposes that it is the demand heterogeneity that contributes to the heterogeneity of firm behaviors (Adner & Zemsky, 2006).
This view has greatly improved our understanding of incumbents' responses to new entrants.
For example, Christensen and Bower (1996) and Christensen (1997) argued that although incumbent firms are capable of responding to disruptive innovation, they often fail to do so, because managers focus on seeking profits from mainstream customers accustomed to sustaining technology, thereby making firms' resource allocation inefficient (Christensen, McDonald, Altman, & Palmer, 2018; Uzunca, 2018).
To cope with disruptive innovation, incumbents may leverage demand heterogeneity to reposition "old" technologies in the marketplace (Adner & Snow, 2010).
However, this literature implicitly assumes that new entrants are not myopic in their search efforts and thus limits our understanding of why some disruptors succeed while others fail (Christensen, Raynor, & McDonald, 2015; Gans, 2016).
<!-- para 9 -->
Scholars have begun to recognize the importance of integrating different views (Eggers & Kaplan, 2013).
For example, Kaplan (2008) examined the interactions between cognition and firm capability.
Both Gans (2016) and McElheran (2015) tried to integrate the supply- and demand-side perspectives to explain how market leaders embrace innovation.
Despite these developments, we still know little about the kinds of environmental stimuli that can be selected into the attention structure of top managers (Ocasio, 1997, 2011).
We argue that because customers are among the most important factors in an organization’s demand environment (Adner & Levinthal, 2001; Adner & Zemsky, 2006), they may affect the development of executive attention (Hodgkinson, 2015; Ocasio, 1997, 2011; Rosa et al., 1999).
Executive attention in turn may drive them to match their firms’ capabilities with perceived opportunities in the demand environment.
Moreover, as “both the supply and ‘demand’ sides of transactional networks” (Hodgkinson, 2015, p. 19) affect managerial attention and cognition of the environment (McElheran, 2015), introducing supply-side factors into the logic chain (demand-side factors, cognition, and organizational search) can significantly improve our understanding of the phenomenon of organizational search.
<!-- para 11 -->
Executive Attention as a Link between Customer Concentration and Firm Search
<!-- para 12 -->
Customer concentration captures a firm’s degree of dependence on its most powerful customers (e.g., Dhaliwal, Judd, Serfling, & Shaikh, 2016; Patatoukas, 2012).
Due to their resource limitations, firms cannot supply all of the possible combinations of products desired by every customer.
Instead, they may benefit from prioritizing those customers that are deemed most important (Levitas, 2013).
For example, researchers have found that firms with high customer concentrations can earn large and stable incomes; have enhanced relationships and coordination; and obtain valuable feedback (Patatoukas, 2012).
Thus, these important customers will gain more prominence in the resource allocation of firms.
<!-- para 13 -->
Firms need to make investment decisions not only to address their current customers, but also to maintain their future vitality by attracting new customers.
Nevertheless, it is often hard for firms to achieve such a balance, especially when the needs of their current customers may not match the needs of future customers.
In particular, firms with a high customer concentration are more likely to collect and transmit information, and allocate resources in ways that enhance the value, power, and prestige of existing major customers.
For example, Titman (1984) suggested that when firms depend on a few major customers for a large proportion of their sales, they make specific concessions to these major customers.
In this regard, concern for the realized or potential benefit of their major customers drives firms to prioritize the expectations, preferences, and needs of these customers.
<!-- para 14 -->
Meeting major customers’ needs typically requires searches that are deep and more focused.
Such search behavior may involve, for example, refining a particular competence, providing a more reliable output, or maintaining a consistent set of processes and procedures.
Such priorities tend to further reinforce existing routines and lead to the development of very specialized competence rather than experimentation and risk-taking (Ahuja & Lampert, 2001; Levinthal & March, 1993).
Moreover, the greater a firm’s customer concentration, the more critical a few customers are, leading to more of the firm’s organizational activities (e.g., R&D, marketing and sales, production, and logistics) being shaped by uncertainties involving major customers.
Search breadth by nature is uncertain and risky (March, 1991).
If it fails, it may cause a significant decline in profits from major customers.
Thus, disproportionate resource allocation to existing major customers limits firm search breadth and displaces the concern for future customers.
The marketing managers featured in Christensen and Bower’s (1996) study confirmed that they chose to prioritize technological projects based on what major customers wanted while neglecting innovative projects for peripheral customers.
<!-- para 15 -->
In their bestseller, The Customer Trap: How to Avoid the Biggest Mistake in Business, Thomas and Wilkinson (2015) suggested that the most innovative companies “create at the root level, [thereby] inventing core technologies with multiple applications," and that such companies are better at managing mega-customer demand, rather than just the demands of a few particular customers.
It may thus seem obvious that innovative products, which result from broad search efforts, are the best means for both retaining existing customers and developing new markets.
However, the largest existing customers often use their disproportionate size and buying power to disable such strategic imperatives (Thomas & Wilkinson, 2015).
The most powerful customers may constrain the search behavior of firms to the extent that the firms cannot escape from the customer trap, even though they acknowledge the need for change (Levitt & March, 1988).
Thus, we contend that firms with high levels of customer concentration are more likely to conduct a deep search into their existing knowledge base but are less likely to engage in a broad search for knowledge beyond the familiar.
Thus, we propose Hypothesis 1.
<!-- para 16 -->
Hypothesis 1: Firms with high customer concentration are (a) more likely to perform a deep search, and (b) less likely to perform a broad search.
<!-- para 17 -->
According to the managerial attention and cognition literature, it is not customer concentration (which is a structural attribute of firms' environment) per se but rather the focus of executive attention preceding the allocation of resources that directs the matching of resources and capabilities with perceived opportunities in the demand environment.
Accordingly, we follow prior research to define attention as “the noticing, encoding, interpreting, and focusing of time and effort by organizational decision-makers” (Ocasio, 1997, p. 189).
This definition is also adopted by cognition scholars (Hodgkinson & Healey, 2011; Ocasio, 2011).
Moreover, we consider executive attention as the socially structured pattern of attention by top executives within an organization (Hodgkinson, 1997; Porac et al., 1989).
In particular, we highlight two different types of executive attention: existing-oriented attention, which refers to the extent to which top executives pay attention to the existing assessment of the external environment, accumulated internal resources and capabilities, and management practices; and future-oriented attention, which captures the extent to which top executives pay attention to the future evaluation of the environment, resources and capabilities, and management practices.
In this sense, existing-oriented attention is closely related to concepts such as commitment to the status quo (Hambrick, Geletkanycz, & Fredrickson, 1993), cognitive inertia (Hodgkinson, 1997), and temporal focus on existing and known information (Nadkarni & Chen, 2014).
In contrast, future-oriented attention is consistent with a futuristic focus on unknown and new information (Nadkarni & Chen, 2014).
We believe that executive attention mediates the relationship between customer concentration and firm search behavior for several reasons.
<!-- para 18 -->
First, executives of firms with high levels of customer concentration tend to develop high levels of existing-oriented attention.
High levels of customer concentration help the largest customers gain a more prominent place in the attention structure of the firms' top executives (Ocasio, 1997).
Economic forces reinforce such "attention traps" (Johnson & Hoopes, 2003, p. 1058), causing top executives to believe that the existing assessment of the environment, ways of doing business, and resources and capabilities to meet environmental demands, are sound.
Customer concentration can further ensure the stable input of these economic forces.
For example, Patatoukas (2012) found that customer concentration is positively related to firms' return on assets (ROA) and return on equity (ROE).
Moreover, exchange embeddedness with major customers can lead to heuristic thinking or the avoidance of expensive cognitive processing to address new situations (Danneels, 2003), further resulting in weakened future-oriented attention.
<!-- para 19 -->
Second, the attention structure then significantly shapes firm search behavior.
Deep search within existing knowledge domains enables firms to develop and refine their core competences, and to provide reliable products that are consistent with top executives' existing-oriented attention.
Such investments can help to ensure the sales and profits required to maintain growth and facilitate long-term relationship building (Ahuja & Lampert, 2001).
In this process, firms enact their demand environment, and the tight coupling generated through that enactment nevertheless leads to a greater commitment to the existing assessment of the environment, resources and capabilities of firms, and management practices.
The result is a narrower focus of knowledge search (Danneels, 2003).
<!-- para 20 -->
Taken together, customer concentration drives top executives to be more existing-oriented rather than future-oriented, which further directs them to match their existing resources and capabilities with perceived opportunities enacted with their major customers (Eggers & Kaplan, 2013).
Prior research is consistent with our argument.
For instance, Tripsas and Gavetti (2000) reported that major customers drove the senior management team of Polaroid Corporation to prioritize existing customers that valued physical instant-print pictures; this action directed Polaroid's search activities toward improving their old technology, rather than exploring the emerging video camcorder technology.
In sum, "customer traps" may take effect via "attention traps." Hence, we propose Hypothesis 2.
<!-- para 21 -->
Hypothesis 2: Executives' existing-oriented attention mediates the relationship between customer concentration and firms' (a) search depth and (b) search breadth.
<!-- para 22 -->
Firms do not search for new ideas in isolation but are bounded by contextual factors (Gavetti et al., 2007).
Moreover, as Ocasio (1997) stated, one critical set of contextual factors shaping the allocation of attention is the “rules of the game,” defined as formal and informal principles such as incentive systems (e.g., Kaplan, 2008), routines, and environmental stimuli (Eggers & Kaplan, 2009) that structure the process by which managers interpret and form cognitive representations.
Therefore, following Ocasio (1997, 2011), we treat attentional processing as a multilevel process shaped by a set of moderators at the organizational (i.e., performance feedback, Chen & Miller, 2007; Greve, 2003) and environmental (i.e., product market competition, Hoberg & Phillips, 2010) levels that alter executive attention.
Performance feedback triggers managerial reflection on the existing assessment of the external environment, internal resources and capabilities, and management practices, whereas managerial beliefs of product market competition define the basis on which firms should compete collectively (cf.
Hodgkinson, 2015; Porac et al., 1989; Peteraf & Shanley, 1997).
Thus, we argue that they can moderate the relationship between customer concentration and search behavior.
<!-- para 24 -->
According to Cyert and March (1963), various forms of feedback, such as actual performance relative to aspirations, have major effects on firm search behavior.
When a firm’s actual performance is lower than its aspiration level, it triggers an attentional engagement process that drives the firm to reconsider its status quo (Greve, 2003).
When firms are dissatisfied with their actual performance, they search for alternatives that may deviate from their normal routines.
However, rather than focusing on the direct effect of performance feedback, we argue that performance relative to aspirations moderates the effect of customer concentration on firm search behavior.
<!-- para 25 -->
To begin with, when a firm’s actual performance improves relative to its aspirations, managers are more likely to attribute its success to the existing organizational structure and maintain the status quo (Chen & Miller, 2007; Greve, 2003).
For example, positive feedback, such as performance above aspirational levels, typically ensures that lessons from earlier experiences are reinforced and the extant way of doing business continues largely unchanged (Baum & Dahlin, 2007).
Thus, the relationship between customer concentration and search depth and breadth may be strengthened by reinforcing the link between customer concentration and existing-oriented executive attention.
As a prominent part of organizational structure, a high customer concentration is more likely to help executives form cognitive representations so that their firms have a better fit with the external environment.
The anecdotal evidence presented in Danneels's (2003) study also confirmed that a group of retailers "showed little incentive for scanning as long as transactions with current customers yielded satisfactory performance" (p. 571).
Therefore, positive feedback reinforces a biased assessment of firms' resources and capabilities and the belief that the existing ways of doing business is correct.
Firms must search deeply, rather than risk searching broadly, to safely meet the demands of major customers and ensure such a fit.
<!-- para 26 -->
In contrast, when a firm’s actual performance is low relative to its aspirations, it experiences a sense of failure, which presents a challenge to its existing ways of doing business.
In such cases, the existing ways of meeting the demands of its major customers in the managerial attention structure are significantly challenged.
A firm’s overreliance on its major customers is more likely to be viewed as the root cause of its poor performance, and its major customers pose a threat to the cognitive representations of managers on the external environment.
In an empirical study of accident costs from 1975 to 2001 on U.S. Class 1 freight railroads, Baum and Dahlin (2007) found that when a railroad’s accident rate deviates from aspiration levels (i.e., when there is a negative performance feedback), the decision-makers are forced to question the status quo and change their orientation to focus on what they will do to improve performance and avoid accidents in the future.
As such, negative performance feedback weakens executives’ existing-oriented attention.
Instead, managers can increase their future-oriented attention, which will in turn alter the relationship between customer concentration and firm search behavior.
<!-- para 27 -->
In summary, positive performance feedback reinforces attentional inertia and provides an impetus to further entrench the focus on major customers.
Thus, we propose Hypothesis 3.
<!-- para 28 -->
Hypothesis 3: Performance exceeding aspirations strengthens (a) the positive relationship between customer concentration and search depth, and (b) the negative relationship between customer concentration and search breadth.
<!-- para 30 -->
The organizational search literature indicates that how competitors influence a firm’s search behavior requires further research (Katila & Chen, 2008).
In this study, we focus on how product market competition interacts with customer concentration to affect firm search behavior.
We define product market competition as the degree of similarity (Chen, 1996) between a focal firm’s claimed or subjectively categorized products and services and those of other firms in the production space within and across industries. [^2] Firms that claim similar products often have similar factor inputs or suppliers, production technologies, and markets or customers (Hoberg & Phillips, 2010; Hsu, Li, Ma, & Phillips, 2017) and are therefore more likely to cognitively view each other as competitors (Hodgkinson, 2015; Porac et al., 1989).
Competition from this viewpoint is seen as different from that defined by traditional methods based on industry classifications, such as Standard Industrial Classification (SIC) codes.
Such an assessment allows us to obtain a more accurate measure of competition and to capture the categorization processes of firms across various industries (Cattani, Porac, & Thomas, 2017).
<!-- para 31 -->
Based on this view of market competition, we argue that competitive relationships are cognitively “embedded” in a sensemaking system that defines market categories and competition (Porac et al., 1989).
The concept behind the TNIC measure of competition is the categorization of firms in the same category as their competitors.
Thus, it captures the attentional process of individual firms and the attentional interlocks among firms (Cattani et al., 2017).
The more similar the products of two firms are, the greater is the competition that exists between them, and the more likely are these firms to confine their attention to each other (Peteraf & Shanley, 1997).
Such increased competition and constrained attention drive top managers to develop high existing-oriented attention because they may perceive the existing ways of practices as most effective in dealing with such competition (Ocasio, 1997).
<!-- para 32 -->
Furthermore, under the threat of fierce product market competition, top managers are more likely to narrow their field of attention as a means of simplifying operations (Younge & Tong, 2018), restricting information processing (Staw, Sandelands, & Dutton, 1981), and avoiding new activities, while seeking to protect existing activities (Chen & Miller, 2007; D'Aveni, 1989).
For example, Shi, Connelly, and Cirik (2018) found that when confronting external threats such as short sellers, managers adopt a defensive stance by halting new growth initiatives and limiting the range of growth modes in which they participate.
Moreover, Gilbert (2005) documented that as newspaper organizations faced threats from digital media, they centralized their decision-making powers, reduced experimentation, and focused on existing resources, resulting in their even greater reliance on existing routines.
Such firms may perceive that they are growing increasingly reliant on maintaining good relations with their major customers (Danneels, 2003).
In contrast, when product similarity is low, top managers are less likely to be pressured to narrow their attention because the existing environment, resources and capabilities, and management practices may be less challenged.
Thus, their firms cognitively perceive less competition.
The constraining effects of customer concentration are therefore attenuated.
Hence, we propose Hypothesis 4.
Hypothesis 4: Product market competition strengthens (a) the positive relationship between customer concentration and search depth, and (b) the negative relationship between customer concentration and search breadth.

## methods
<!-- para 3 -->
We tested our hypotheses with a longitudinal dataset of U.S. publicly listed firms from the 1976 to 2005 period.
To examine firm search behavior, we obtained patent data from the National Bureau of Economic Research's patent database (Hall et al., 2001), the Electronic Information Products Division of the United States Patent and Trademark Office (Sampat, 2012), and the Harvard Dataverse that provides information on patent subclasses.
We extracted the firms' customer-supplier data, industry segment data, and financial statement data from Compustat.
Our sample period began in 1976, the first year for which major customer data were made available.
Since 1976, the Statement of Financial Accounting Standards No. 14 (SFAS 14) and, later, the FAS 131 required suppliers to disclose any external customers that individually account for 10% or more of their revenues.
Following prior studies (Dhaliwal et al., 2016), we focused on corporate customers, rather than government customers whose economic incentives and activities can be quite different. [^3] In addition, to avoid potential truncation concerns about patent data (Graham & Hegde, 2015), our sample period ended in 2005.
We focused on manufacturing firms (SIC 2000-3999) as firm search behavior in this sector relates more naturally to supplier-customer relationships. [^4] The intersection of these databases created our main sample, consisting of 19,101 supplier firm-year observations.
## Measures
<!-- para 5 -->
Firm search behavior.
Using longitudinal and objective data to measure firm search behavior is a challenge for researchers.
Inspired by Katila and Ahuja (2002), we used the firms' patenting activities to proxy for the depth and breadth of firm search behavior.
Patent data provide a detailed and consistent chronology of how firms search, both within and across various fields of knowledge.
This measure has been widely accepted in the firm search literature (e.g., Katila & Chen, 2008; Yu, Minniti, & Nason, 2019).
According to Katila and Ahuja (2002), search depth concerns the “accumulation of search experience with the same knowledge elements” (p. 1187).
Thus, the more frequently a firm repeatedly uses prior knowledge, the deep is its search attempt.
Hence, in the patent context, the number of times, on average, a focal firm repeatedly cites previously referenced patents effectively proxies for the “accumulated search experience” and hence, search depth.
Following this logic, we measured Search depth by calculating the average frequency with which previously referenced patents cited by a focal firm in a given year were repeatedly cited during the previous five years (i.e., repeated citations).
This variable was computed as follows:
<!-- para 6 -->
$$ Search depth_{it}=\frac{\sum_{y=t-5}^{t-1}repeat citation counts_{iy}}{previously referenced citations_{it}}, $$
<!-- para 7 -->
where i refers to a focal firm and t refers to a given year. [^5]
<!-- para 8 -->
We also constructed the variable Search breadth by following Katila and Ahuja (2002).
As search breadth “corresponds to the theoretical notion of exploration of new knowledge” in the patent context, “the proportion of previously unused citations” (Katila & Ahuja, 2002, p. 1187) effectively proxies for this construct.
We calculated the share of the total citations of a focal firm’s patents in a given year that cannot be found in the firm’s lists of patent citations from the previous five years (i.e., new citations).
This variable was computed as follows:
<!-- para 9 -->
$$ Search breadth_{it}=\frac{new citation counts_{it}}{total citations_{it}}, $$
<!-- para 10 -->
where i refers to a focal firm and t refers to a given year. [^6]
<!-- para 12 -->
Customer Concentration.
Researchers have developed numerous measures of customer concentration, most of which are based on the percentage of a firm's sales to its major customers (customers that account for over 10% of the sales) (e.g., Patatoukas, 2012).
Following Patatoukas (2012) and Dhaliwal et al. (2016), we used a Herfindahl-Hirschman Index (HHI)-based measure to capture Customer concentration as follows:
<!-- para 13 -->
$$ Customer concentration=\sum_{j=1}^{n}\left(\frac{Sales_{ij}}{Sales_{i}}\right)^{2}, $$
<!-- para 14 -->
where  $Sales_{ij}$ represents supplier (focal firm) i's sales to major customer j and  $Sales_{i}$ represents supplier i's total sales.
This variable ranges between zero and one, with high values representing a more concentrated customer base.
In the extreme case, it equals zero if a firm does not disclose its sales to any major customer, and one if it relies on a single major customer for all of its annual revenues. [^7]
## Mediators
<!-- para 15 -->
Executive Attention.
Our theoretical argument proposed a process wherein top executives' attention is either existing-oriented or future-oriented [^8].
However, it is difficult and often impossible to directly measure managerial attention and cognition in large sample analysis.
Following emerging studies using archival approaches to capture executive attention (e.g., Cho & Hambrick, 2006; Kaplan, 2008; Nadkarni & Chen, 2014), we collected a set of archival data to examine the attention allocation process of top executives.
<!-- para 16 -->
We relied on two main archival sources to collect written and verbal statements of top managers: letters to shareholders (LTS) and earnings conference calls (ECC).
While LTS are of particular interest to management scholars (Nadkarni & Chen, 2014), ECC are frequently used in the accounting and finance literature (Loughran & McDonald, 2016).
Both sources involve public statements made by top managers to discuss the strategic themes that are important to their firms.
They thus provide first-order assertions without the interpretation of others and are therefore conducive to examining the beliefs and cognition of top managers.
<!-- para 17 -->
We obtained LTS data from Mergent Online and Lexis-Nexis, and ECC data from Factiva.
As not all firms have LTS and most ECC are only available from 1994, we obtained full coverage of LTS and ECC for about 14.6% of our full sample (2,783 observations).

First, we used a thesaurus to identify synonyms for the seed keywords, “existing” and “future.” Second, to validate the word list, we asked three content experts who were not aware of our hypotheses to rate the words in our list on a scale ranging from 1 (definitely existing-oriented attention) to 7 (definitely future-oriented attention) with a mid-point of 4 (unsure or neither).

For all cases, we evaluated the inter-rater reliabilities and used the criteria of higher than 0.80 to decide on keeping or dropping.
Examples of existing-oriented attention words were current, continuing, established, immediate, mature, present, prevalent, recent, and dominant.
Examples of future-oriented attention words were advancing, anticipated, approaching, coming, expected, new, next, potential, promising, proposed, and upcoming.
<!-- para 18 -->
Third, to verify the reliability of the computer-aided content analysis, such as the linguistic inquiry and word count dictionary, we recruited two research assistants to manually check the text materials for a random sample of 50 firm-year observations.
They helped to ensure the accuracy of the word count in each type of attention.
This evaluation led to an overall inter-rater agreement level of 86% for existing-oriented attention and 93% for future-oriented attention, respectively.
Any remaining discrepancies were resolved by discussion.
<!-- para 19 -->
Fourth, to ensure the validity of the dictionary of existing- and future-oriented attention, we conducted a separate study of 36 CEOs (55.56% male, mean age 39.01 years, mean work experience as executives 10.19 years) enrolled in a global EMBA program at a major university.
We asked the CEOs to submit a written report, in which they did a comprehensive analysis of the macro, industry, and internal environment of their firms and designed strategies to deal with the potential threats and seize the opportunities.
Then, we used the dictionary to construct the measures of existing-oriented and future-oriented attention for each CEO from their reports.
Next, following Loughran and McDonald (2016) and Nadkarni and Chen (2014), we used a scale also designed to survey existing-oriented and future-oriented attention to validate our dictionary-based measures.
The most relevant scale matching with our theoretical focus is the GLOBE survey questions for future orientation (House et al., 2004), which effectively captures individuals' choices between existing and future orientation.
The sample items include the following: "The way to be successful for my firm is to: Plan ahead—take life events as they occur," "I believe the accepted norm in my firm should be: Plan for the future—accept the status quo," and "I put more emphasis on: Planning for the future—Solving current problems." All items were measured using seven-point Likert-type scales.
The low (high) the score, the high the level of future orientation (existing orientation).
One month after the submission of the report, the same group of CEOs was asked to answer the scale of GLOBE survey.
Then, we obtained the survey-based future orientation measures for each CEO.
The correlation analyses showed that the survey-based measure of future orientation is significantly correlated with the dictionary-based measure of existing-oriented attention (r = 0.49, p < 0.05) and future-oriented attention (r = -0.56, p < 0.05).
According to Cohen, Cohen, West, and Aiken (2003), an absolute value larger than 0.30 indicates a moderate correlation, while 0.50 indicates a high correlation.
Thus, the correlations between the two methods of measuring the constructs with a similar theoretical focus provide strong support to the validity of the measures (Nadkarni & Chen, 2014).
In the final step, we measured top executives' existing- and future-oriented attention for each firm by the word count in a given LTS or ECC, according to the corresponding dictionary.
Following prior studies (e.g., Eggers & Kaplan, 2009; Kaplan, 2008; Nadkarni & Chen, 2014), the count was normalized by the total number of words in the document.
Next, we calculated the measure of existing-oriented attention as the ratio of the existing-oriented attention to the sum of existing- and future-oriented attention.
<!-- para 21 -->
Performance Feedback.
The calculation of performance feedback variables is affected by the performance metric on which aspirations are based (Posen, Keil, Kim, & Meissner, 2018).
As Gavetti, Greve, Levinthal, and Ocasio (2012) pointed out, “the usual profitability measures such as ROA are too general to test the proposition that search occurs in the vicinity of the problem symptom” (p. 12).
Thus, innovation performance metrics such as competitors’ patent citations are more relevant in our study of innovation search (e.g., Gaba & Bhattacharya, 2012).
Following Arrfelt, Wiseman, and Hult (2013), we computed a firm’s Social performance feedback and Historical performance feedback.
Specifically, Social performance feedback was measured as the patent citations received by a focal firm in year t minus the median number of patent citations of its peers (three-digit SIC industry) in the same year.
Historical performance feedback was measured as the firm’s patent citations in year t minus its own historical performance, an average of the previous two years’ patent citations.
<!-- para 22 -->
Product Market Competition.
The measure of Product market competition, based on the TNIC, was constructed as follows (for more details, refer to Hoberg & Phillips, 2010).
First, they combined web crawling and text parsing algorithms to analyze the text of product descriptions of the firms' 10-K filing statements.
Second, they calculated firm-by-firm pairwise similarity scores as a Hotelling-like product location space to directly quantify a firm-specific relationship between any two firms in terms of product similarity.
Third, as 10-Ks are refiled annually, they repeated the aforementioned two steps and updated the calculation of product similarity for each firm every year, thus obtaining a dynamic measure of product market competition for each firm.
<!-- para 23 -->
The variable construction process points to some of the advantages of TNIC over traditional SIC codes.
First, TNIC captures the categorization of firms while SIC is only a de facto system.
Second, TNIC is dynamic as it updates each focal firm's competitors every year.
Noticeably, based on the U.S. IT industry, Kim et al. (2016) found that roughly 35% of TNIC rivals in a given year were no longer rivals a year later.
Third, TNIC provides improved power to measure competition strictly relative to each given firm rather than among generalized SIC code groups.
Thus, it provides more accurate information on the identities of firms with which a focal firm has attentional interlocks.
In sum, we believe that TNIC is more consistent with our theoretical focus on the attention-based view, and thereby use it as our primary approach to measure competition and the traditional HHI index as robustness check.
<!-- para 24 -->
Specifically, Product market competition was calculated as minus one times TNIC HHI provided in Hoberg and Phillips’s (2010) database.
It is a continuous variable in which a larger number indicates a high degree of competition.
As Hoberg and Phillips’ (2010) data collection began in 1996, the time period for testing this moderating effect spanned 1996 to 2005. [^9] In our study, the correlation between the TNIC measure and the traditional sales-based HHI measure was 0.12 (p < 0.05), indicating a weak correlation between the two measures.
However, the estimation results held when we followed the convention to use the sales-based HHI approach to measure the degree of competition in an industry.
### Control Variables
<!-- para 25 -->
We included a number of control variables in the regression analyses.
We controlled for Firm size (the natural logarithm of total sales) because large firms have more resources available for their search activities.
Diversification, measured as the number of business segments of a firm, was included, because a larger number of business segments may lead the firm to search more broadly.
We controlled for Book to market, as the market valuation of a firm may also affect its search behavior.
Leverage, measured as the ratio of total liabilities to total assets, was included because highly leveraged firms may lack the financial resources to support their search activities.
We also controlled for Profitability, measured by ROA, because firms with high profits tend to reinforce their existing knowledge (Chen & Miller, 2007), and thus search more deeply.
Tangibility, measured as the ratio of net property, plant, and equipment to total assets, was included because firms with more tangible assets are often associated with more external funds to support their search activities.
We also included R&D, measured as the ratio of R&D expenditure to total assets, because R&D activities contribute to firms' capability to absorb extramural knowledge (Cohen & Levinthal, 1990).
We controlled for Firm age (natural log of the number of years since the firm's listing) because older firms tend to exploit their existing technological competencies rather than explore new and unfamiliar technologies (Sørensen & Stuart, 2000).
Different customers may have different tastes, preferences, and product requirements, which may produce different effects on firm search behavior (Adner & Levinthal, 2001); thus, we also included Customer heterogeneity, measured as the number of the types of customers, including business customers, end-users, and others.
Finally, search behaviors need not necessarily be restricted to patenting but can also include alliances and so on.
Hence, we included Alliance, a dummy variable equal to one if the firm formed an alliance in a given year, and zero otherwise; data were obtained from the Securities Data Company (SDC) database.
Following prior studies (Dhaliwal et al., 2016), we winsorized the continuous variables at the  $1^{st}$ and  $99^{th}$ percentiles to reduce the influence of outliers.
However, in separate analysis wherein we did not winsorize the continuous variables, the results continued to hold.
<!-- para 27 -->
As both dependent variables are continuous variables, we ran ordinary least squares regressions on the panel dataset to test our hypotheses.
Specifically, we used firm search behavior (Depth and Breadth) in year  $t+1$ as the dependent variables and Customer Concentration in year t as the independent variable.
We included year fixed effects and industry fixed effects (the results were consistent across two-, three-, and four-digit SIC industry levels), and reported heteroskedasticity-robust standard errors below.
<!-- para 28 -->
It is noteworthy that our theoretical and hypotheses development about across-firm comparisons suggests the use of an industry fixed-effects specification that focuses on testing differences across firms within the same industry, rather than a firm fixed-effects specification that instead focuses on testing differences within a firm across years.
The nature of the variable customer concentration also makes it difficult to use a firm fixed-effect specification.
In our study, the within-firm variance of customer concentration was rather low: the mean of within-firm variance across years was only 0.03, much smaller than the sample mean of variance 0.14.
The main reason for the small within-firm variation is related to our sample of manufacturing firms, in which the length of supplier-buyer relationships is typically very stable and enduring.
For example, the average length of supplier-buyer relationships in the U.S. was 32.56 years for automakers in 1992 (Dyer & Chu, 2000), 12.96 years for electric and electronic industries (Poppo, Zhou, & Ryu, 2008), and 12.06 years for computer manufacturers (Poppo & Zenger, 2002).
Thus, a firm's customer concentration is highly correlated with its individual effect; theoretically speaking, customer concentration is an important component of what creates differences across firms, and therefore, removing such effects would eliminate a critical source of heterogeneity. [^10] Consistent with our study, Campello and Gao (2017) also reported that customer concentration varied significantly across industries but much less so within an industry and a firm; thus, inclusion of industry fixed effects was deemed to fit their data better.
Hence, other prior studies of customer concentration have also chosen to not include firm fixed effects (see Dhaliwal et al., 2016).
We followed this approach in our main analysis.
In the Online Appendix, we also included a full set of industry-by-year fixed effects, and all the results still held.

Though we followed Katila and Ahuja (2002) and tested the effect of customer concentration on firm search behavior with a one-year lag, endogeneity might still be a potential concern.
For example, firms with high customer concentration may have other characteristics that are correlated with and affect innovation search.
To mitigate concerns of selection bias and omitted variable bias, we resorted to an instrumental variable (IV) approach.
<!-- para 31 -->
Specifically, following Campello and Gao (2017), we used the mergers and acquisitions (M&A) activity in the industries of customers (“downstream M&A activity”) as an instrument.
Prior research has shown that firms face a more concentrated customer base following M&A waves in their customers’ industries.
However, the downstream M&A activity in customers’ industries should not exert a direct influence on the search behavior of individual firms other than through the firms’ customer relationship, and therefore it can be considered as a valid instrumental variable.
As we required information on customer-supplier links to identify M&A events, we only focused on those firms whose customers can be identified in the database (i.e., major customers of public-listed firms).
Following Campello and Gao (2017), we collected information on M&A deals from the SDC database and calculated the instrumental variable in several steps.
First, we calculated a measure of acquisition activity, defined as the transaction values of M&As conducted by a firm scaled by the firm’s total sales.
Second, we calculated an industry-level acquisition activity measure, defined as the average of the acquisition activity of all firms in the industry over the past five years.
Finally, we took the weighted sum of the industry-level acquisition activities across all of the industries in which the focal firm’s customers operate, weighted by the focal firm’s percentage of sales to each customer, to obtain a measure of downstream M&A activity, known as Customer M&A.
<!-- para 32 -->
We then estimated the effect of customer concentration on firm search behavior.
In the first stage, we regressed the variable Customer concentration on Customer M&A, together with a full set of controls discussed earlier.
In the second stage, we regressed firm search behavior (search depth and search breadth) on the predicted customer concentration measure.
The results of the first-stage regression are presented in Model 1 of Table 4.
The coefficient estimate of customer M&A was positive and significant ( $\beta = 0.01, p < 0.001$).
The F-statistics from the first-stage regression passed the weak identification tests at the 0.001 level.
The Kleinberg-Paap statistics were all larger than 10, passing the associated under-identification tests.
These tests showed that the instrumental variable was valid.
Models 2A and 2B of Table 4 present the second-stage regression results.
As expected, in Model 2A with search depth as the dependent variable, the coefficient estimate of predicted customer concentration was positive and significant ( $\beta = 2.21, p < 0.05$); in Model 2B with search breadth as the dependent variable, the coefficient estimate of predicted customer concentration was negative and significant ( $\beta = -0.78, p < 0.01$).
These results further supported our prediction that customer concentration is positively related to deep search but negatively related to broad search.

Another potential concern is reverse causality: search depth (breadth) may reversely increase (decrease) customer concentration.
However, this relationship may lack theoretical rationale.
Customer concentration is a structural attribute that is often beyond the direct control of the firm.
As a simple example, in cases where the firm has only one customer, who is a monopolist in the downstream market, the firm's search behavior is unlikely to directly affect the customer's purchase decision.
Indeed, the DBV argues that while firms can create value propositions for the customers, only the customers can determine the value of such offerings.
Nonetheless, to empirically address the potential concern, we leveraged the deregulation of Acts and Orders by the Federal Government as a shock on downstream M&A activity (Campello & Gao, 2017).
This shock substantially changed the customer concentration of the firm and enables a difference-in- differences (DID) design to test for how firms affected by the shock changed their search behavior after the shock, compared with firms not affected by the shock.
We obtained consistent results from the DID analyses.
To save space, we report the details in the Online Appendix.

We conducted additional tests to check the robustness of our results.
First, we estimated search depth and search breadth equations separately in the main analysis.
However, these two variables were not independently determined.
When we ran a seemingly unrelated regressions model in which search depth and breadth were simultaneously estimated as dependent variables, the results continued to hold.
Second, our results were not affected by incorporating firms' past search behaviors.
Specifically, we added the lagged dependent variables to the equations and found that they had a significant and positive coefficient (i.e.,  $\beta = 0.59$,  $p < 0.001$;  $\beta = 0.42$,  $p < 0.001$; respectively), consistent with the notion that firm search behavior is path-dependent (March, 1991).
Moreover, our main results still hold.
Third, our results were robust to alternative measures of Customer concentration and other control variables, including the natural log of number of employees, which is a measure of firm size and the natural log of R&D expenditure, which is a measure of R&D activities.
Fourth, we used alternative search measures to examine the robustness of our results.
Specifically, we used conglomerate M&As as an alternative measure of search breadth.
Conglomerate acquisitions occur when firms acquire unrelated firms (Haunschild, 1993) and are considered a type of distant search.
The results showed that customer concentration had a significant and negative effect on conglomerate M&As ( $\beta = -0.03$,  $p < 0.001$).
We also used the traditional HHI index to measure product market competition for robustness checks.
The moderating effects continued to hold.
Fifth, we tested the moderation hypotheses in the IV regressions.
The main results continued to hold; however, the interaction effect of product market competition on the customer concentration-depth relationship became insignificant [^11].

## discussion
<!-- para 2 -->
In this study, we explore the relationship between customer concentration and firm search behavior.
Based on an empirical investigation of the patent search behavior of U.S. publicly listed firms from 1976 to 2005, we find that customer concentration increases search depth and decreases search breadth via executives' existing-oriented attention.
The finding helps us identify an additional source of organizational rigidity and inertia (Gilbert, 2005; Hodgkinson & Healey, 2011), and better understand why myopic searches and learning persist despite firms' acknowledgment of the value of exploration.
Furthermore, we find that executives' existing-oriented attention mediates the effect of customer concentration on firm search behavior.
Finally, a set of moderation tests reveal that when firms' performance exceeds their social aspirational levels or when product market competition is high, firms with high customer concentration tend to focus on deep and narrow search efforts.
<!-- para 3 -->
Thus, we contribute to the literature in several ways.
First, we contribute to the organizational search literature by attending to demand-side factors such as customer structure, and opening a new dialogue for examining the effects of more characteristics of the demand environment on firm search behavior (Sidhu, Commandeur, & Volberda, 2007).
Relatedly, we acknowledge that the effects of major customers in constraining organizational adaption have received much attention.
For example, regarding the confrontation of the competition from new entrants in an industry, Christensen and Bower (1996) and Christensen (1997) concluded that incumbents would often continue with sustaining innovation or the continuous “improvement in product performance” but do not respond vigorously to the disruptive innovation of new entrants because these innovations are characterized by “attributes valued only in emerging markets remote from, and unimportant to, the mainstream” (see Christensen et al., 2018 for a review).
As entrants move upmarket and mainstream customers start to adopt entrants’ offerings, incumbents fail.
<!-- para 4 -->
Our study extends the disruptive innovation research in two ways.
First, our theoretical focus on firm search behavior complements prior research by exploring the behavioral foundation of sustaining and disruptive innovation.
As both sustaining and disruptive innovations involve search depth and search breath, simply ascribing incumbents' failure to their deep and narrow search and taking disruptors' broad search for granted is theoretically incomplete (cf.
Gans, 2016).
Search depth and search breadth enhances our understanding of the knowledge search patterns of both incumbents and disruptors.
Second, disruptive innovation research focuses mainly on the dynamics of customers across different markets: high-end market for underserved customers and low-end market for overserved customers.
However, the theory speaks nothing of the differences in customer structure within each market (McElheran, 2015).
We argue that disruptor firms may also have major customers in low-end markets, just as incumbent firms do in high-end markets.
According to our findings, firms with high customer concentration, irrespective of being incumbents or disruptors, are more likely to search deeply and less likely to search broadly.
If search depth and breadth have normative implications, as argued by previous researchers (e.g., Levinthal & March, 1993), then both incumbent and disruptor firms could be myopic and face the risk of failure.
In so doing, we offer novel insights into why some disruptor firms succeed while others fail, a significant gap underexplored in the literature (Christensen et al., 2015; Gans, 2016).
<!-- para 5 -->
Our study also contributes to the cognition literature by foregrounding and incorporating the missing demand-side factors into the theory (Danneels, 2003).
Research on cognition mainly focuses on supply-side factors such as competitors which render firms impervious to innovation beyond the cognitive community (Peteraf & Shanley, 1997; Porac et al., 1989).
However, the cognitive community includes both customers and suppliers, and managers allocate their limited attention to both the supply-side and demand-side factors (Hodgkinson, 2015; Rosa et al., 1999).
Our study demonstrates that existing-oriented attention developed from continued exchanges with major customers leads top executives to perceive that their resources and capabilities match the demand environment while downplaying future needs, thereby resulting in deep and narrow searches within firms' existing domains of expertise.
We extend this stream of research "well beyond ... the supply side of transactional networks" (Hodgkinson, 2015, p. 19).
Future study can gain more insights in our direction.
<!-- para 6 -->
Moreover, we contribute to the growing literature on the DBV by uncovering how demand structure affects firm heterogeneity (Adner & Zemsky, 2006).
The DBV emphasizes how market demand and customer structure contribute to firm heterogeneity (Priem et al., 2012) and “has focused more extensively on the political dimension of the resource allocation process than it has on the cognitive/behavioral one” (Gavetti, 2012, p. 279) to explain such heterogeneity.
For example, Christensen et al. (2015) and Christensen et al. (2018) highlighted that the mechanisms through which major customers affect firm choices are external dependence on customers and internal resource allocation driven by power dynamics.
Thus, it fails to consider the role of behavioral mechanisms such as executive attention.
We demonstrate that demand-side factors affect supply-side search activities in a technological landscape (c.f., Sidhu et al., 2007) through the mediation of top executive attention that precedes and affects resource allocation (Eggers & Kaplan, 2009).
Hence, our study enriches demand-side research and paves the way for future studies in this growing area.
<!-- para 7 -->
We also contribute by integrating the supply- and demand-side factors to provide a more comprehensive understanding of firm search behavior (Hodgkinson, 1997; Levitas, 2013; Porac et al., 1989).
Specifically, the positive moderating effect of firms' superior performance feedback compared with industry peers lends strong empirical support to anecdotal cases, such as those provided by Christensen and Bower (1996), Danneels (2003), and Gilbert (2005).
In addition, prior studies conclude that competition increases the need for broad search (Levinthal & March, 1993).
However, our findings challenge such a statement because firms may be unable to respond to such needs due to the attentional capture of competition.
That is, firms may confine their attention to their perceived competitors (Peteraf & Shanley, 1997) and find it difficult to change their cognitive maps (Reger & Palmer, 1996) and mental models of competitive space (Hodgkinson, 1997).
Then, the supply-side managerial beliefs that define the basis on which firms should compete collectively enhance the attentional capture of the effects of demand-side factors such as customer concentration.
Competitive relationships are cognitively “embedded.” Hence, we contribute to a new perspective on a new measure of product market competition, which can capture the attentional processes of individual firms and attentional interlocks among firms.
<!-- para 8 -->
We also acknowledge that performance feedback of historical aspirations does not influence the main effects.
This result is somewhat surprising because the behavioral theory generally assumes that both kinds of aspirations trigger similar behavioral responses (Cyert & March,
1963).
We speculate that they may have different underlying cognitive processes (Washburn & Bromiley, 2012).
For example, social aspirations help managers to assess how well they should perform, whereas historical aspirations indicate how well they can perform (Kim, Finkelstein, & Haleblian, 2015).
As firms must first pay attention to the evaluation of stakeholders, managers prioritize social aspirations as the baseline performance level (Washburn & Bromiley, 2012).
Moreover, if historical performance feedback is high, managers may exhibit a stronger desire to sustain the performance momentum by focusing on existing organizational practices, leading to a positive moderating effect of historical performance feedback.
However, high historical performance feedback may also raise performance expectations, which increases the difficulty of meeting or exceeding such expectations; this underscores the need for caution as the risk of being unable to meet expectations increases (Kim et al., 2015).
This situation may suggest a negative moderating effect.
Both mechanisms may take effect simultaneously, resulting in an insignificant result we actually observed.
We see great potential in differentiating between the two types of aspirations and encourage future research to systematically tease out these mechanisms.
<!-- para 9 -->
Our study also has several limitations, which represent opportunities for future research.
First, although we relied on patent data to capture firm search behavior, firms' patenting propensity varies across industries, and some of them may not want to patent their critical knowledge.
Future research may gain more insights into search behavior by identifying other measures of search behavior.
Second, the operationalization of executive attention could be improved in future research.
Public statements made by top managers may not be neutral and are likely to have social desirability biases and emotional elements (Hodgkinson & Sparrow, 2002; Hodgkinson & Healey, 2011), which may make our measures unable to capture such nuanced human assessments.
Moreover, although denoting the mid-point as “unsure” does “not adversely affect reliability and validity” (p.251) of Likert-type scaling (Kulas, Stochowski, & Haynes, 2008), this approach for selecting words to measure executive attention may need further improvement.
Future researchers can empirically verify the mechanisms through improved measurement and designs such as field experiments and video coding designs.
Finally, this study is limited in offering insights into the determinants of customer concentration, which is poorly covered in the extant literature.
Our findings imply that customer concentration tends to be high in smaller, younger, and less profitable firms with greater R&D.
Accordingly, we call for future research to systematically investigate this promising topic.
Moreover, we are interested in the structural rather than the content characteristics of customers.
Thus, the heterogeneity of customer demands even within the same market segment may affect firm search behavior (Adner & Levinthal, 2001).
We call for more research to identify other customer characteristics and examine how changes in customer demands affect firm search behavior.
<!-- para 11 -->
In conclusion, our findings show that customer concentration is an important demand-side factor that shapes executive attention and affects firm search behavior.
The importance, relevance, and urgency of claims made by major customers tend to sideline competing claims from others and capture the attention of top executives to meet the expectations and needs of these customers.
Thus, customer concentration serves to increase a firm's search depth while decreasing its search breadth.
Moreover, both performance feedback exceeding social aspiration levels and product market competition tend to amplify the relationship between customer concentration and firm search behavior.
In sum, our study focuses on the demand side and the role of executive attention in channeling the effect of customer concentration, contributing a new perspective to explain the heterogeneity in firm search behavior.
